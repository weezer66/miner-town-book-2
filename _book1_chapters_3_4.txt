ch005.xhtml Gage’s right leg was a dead weight, a numb log that refused his brain’s commands. Some
primal part of him wanted to crawl into shadow the way wounded animals do, but sixty hours of forced
wakefulness had turned his muscles into shredded rope. Deep in his marrow, the piezoelectric hum
vibrated through the floor — a relentless interference trying to untune his soul. He looked at his
shaking hands and thought of Camilla. Back in the shack, she’d be hunched over some piece of scrap,
her quiet hands finding logic in a broken machine. He was the force; she was the insight. If he was
going to survive this Initiation, he couldn’t just be the Engine anymore. He had to find a way to
see the world through her eyes. Beside him, Ben and Otto were sprawled with their cheeks pressed to
the grit, breathing shallow and ragged. A figure jerked through the orange haze. Rosa. She moved
with a twitchy, insect-like rhythm, her skin pale as translucent parchment. “Rosa?” Gage croaked,
swallowing hard against a throat that felt lined with dry wool. “You still awake? God knows what
Icepick put in that food…” She stared at him with a fixed grin that didn’t reach her bloodshot eyes.
“Yeah. Emily’s not talking to me, though. She’s being quiet today.” As Rosa turned, Gage’s breath
hitched. Tied to her back with frayed mining rope was Emily’s body, slumped and heavy. As Rosa
swayed, Emily’s limbs flailed in a boneless arc — a broken puppet dancing to a madwoman’s tune. “She
kept stumbling into the walls until she just… drifted off,” Rosa whispered. “Look at the grey dust
in her hair. It almost looks like a crown of silver, doesn’t it?” She bent down and pulled a rusted
pickaxe from the corner of the chamber — left behind by some kid who hadn’t made it out. “Gage… you
look like you’re struggling. Do you want me to help you rest like Emily?” Rosa lunged. The combined
weight slammed into Gage’s chest. He clawed at the air, fingers like wet noodles, the rusted blade
inches from his throat. “Ben! Otto!” No answer. Just Rosa’s breath on his face, sour and metallic,
and the blade descending. He could see the rust-flakes on the edge. He could see Rosa’s pale eye,
calm in the way that nothing human is calm. The pickaxe touched his throat — a kiss of cold iron —
and broke the skin. Camilla, he thought. The first thing in his head, the only thing. He’s going to
leave her alone. Then, from the corner of his eye, a figure lunged. The pressure vanished. A single
sharp crack — bone striking bone, calculated. Rosa’s head snapped sideways as she was tossed off
him. Gage rolled over, gasping for air that tasted of copper. A line from Pappy’s stories pierced
the fog: You can’t pipe the deep carbon, boy. You have to break it. Looking at Rosa’s crumpled form,
Gage understood the Initiation. The Trinity didn’t want loyalty; they wanted to see if these kids
would snap or turn into diamonds under pressure. Otto lay trembling nearby, his hand still curled
into the mathematical strike that could only have come from Shu Chan’s training. The truth hit Gage:
the Initiation wasn’t a test. It was a culling. Icepick was weeding out the weak, turning survivors
into bots too broken to dream of mutiny. Gage was on his feet before he knew it, fists balled,
heading for the tunnel mouth. “I’m going to find Icepick. I’m going to—” “And do what?” Ben’s voice
was barely a rasp, but it stopped Gage mid-stride. Ben hadn’t moved from the ground. He was lying on
his side, arms wrapped around his ribs, too weak to stand. But his eyes were open and fixed on Gage.
“You can’t even walk straight. You go down that tunnel and they’ll break you like they broke Emily.”
Otto was sitting against the wall, staring at the hand that had struck Rosa. His fingers were
trembling. He hadn’t spoken since the blow landed — the precision that Master Chan had drilled into
him used not to protect, but to hurt a girl who was already broken. The silence coming off him was
worse than Ben’s shouts. “Otto.” Ben turned his head on the grit. “You saved him. That’s what that
was. Don’t make it anything else.” Otto didn’t answer. But his hand stopped trembling. Ben looked
between them — the Engine about to charge blindly into the dark, the Fighter about to collapse into
himself — and said the only thing that mattered: “We stay together. We think. Then we move.” Gage
stood in the tunnel mouth for three long breaths. Then he came back and sat down. Gage knew they
were running out of time. If they didn’t sleep, they would murder each other or die of exhaustion.
He forced himself to think like Camilla — to decode what was killing them before it finished the
job. He remembered the night before the gates opened. Camilla had been unusually silent, her quiet
hands working with frantic precision over a pile of scavenged medical scrap. Before he left, she’d
slipped a thin, cold object into the hidden seam of his sleeve. “For the fires you cannot see,
Gage,” she’d whispered. “Don’t pull it out unless you have to. If anything tastes wrong, or your
body starts to lie to you, test what they fed you. Test all of it. And let it breathe between
tests.” Now, with his mind fracturing under the sixty-hour strain — Rosa’s blade still warm on his
neck, Emily’s body tied to her back like a doll — Gage understood. The chamber wasn’t a test of
endurance. It was a slow execution. He had told himself the tang was rationing, the wakefulness
fear, the hum just nerves. Camilla had said don’t pull it out unless you have to. He had to. He
ripped the seam open. He pulled out a thin sliver of something — colder than skin, translucent, the
color of nothing in particular. He didn’t know what to call it yet; he only knew it was hers, and
that meant it had cost her something to make. He pressed the strip into the mashed potatoes. It
stayed clear — no reaction, nothing to reset. He touched it to the dried chicken. Nothing. Finally,
he dipped it into the water. The reaction was instantaneous. The polymer turned a violent, bruised
purple that seemed to throb with its own malice. Camilla had taught him the basics before the gates
opened. Three colors, three kinds of bad. Green for rot. Yellow for poison. Purple for the kind that
went for your head — the worst kind, she’d said, because it made you stop being you. The water
wasn’t just dirty. It was drugged. As he pulled it out, the color began to ripple — like an oil
slick shifting on a hot sidewalk, the purple retreated from the edges but stalled, settling into a
dull, bruised grey that wouldn’t fade further. Camilla had warned him: the strip needed to breathe
between tests, needed time to bleed the last reaction out before it could read clean. He’d rushed
through the first two without waiting because neither had triggered a response. Now the sensor was
stuck — saturated with the water’s signature, stubbornly holding its color. It would clear
eventually. But not yet. “The water,” Gage rasped. It wasn’t the food priming them; it was the very
thing they used to wash the bitterness away. Camilla hadn’t just given him a tool; she had given him
her Eyes. He looked at Ben, already drifting into ecstatic madness, shouting about a chicken
sandwich. Otto had to knock Ben out to stop the shrieks. As Ben’s body slumped into the grit, his
consciousness slid away from the mine and into the sanctuary his parents had built — an invisible
fortress against the culling of the soul. In his dream, Ben saw his father’s face. Oscar had always
told him that the Trinity didn’t just harvest coal; they harvested the capacity to care. He knew the
system wanted human engines — tools driven by grinding fear. To survive without becoming a bot, you
had to maintain the one thing the ledger couldn’t calculate: Empathy. The memory shifted to the day
Leroy arrived. For years, Leroy had been the family’s enemy — the one who stole their bread when the
rations failed, the one who left bruises on Oscar’s face whenever Oscar wouldn’t surrender what
little he’d put aside. Leroy had crossed into something worse than a man, a creature that ran on the
calculus of scrap or be scrapped. Ben had spent his childhood watching his father come home with a
split lip and saying nothing about it. And then, one morning, there Leroy was — bleeding out on
their doorstep, a jagged knife wound leaking his life into the dirt. Ben remembered the cold logic
that had briefly flared in his chest: Let him die. One less mouth. One less threat. The culling
instinct, the protocol that stripped humanity to ensure survival. But his sister Gretchen’s voice
cut through: The system made him this way, Ben. If we let him die for what it turned him into, we’re
doing Trinity’s work for it. That was the moment empathy became a weapon of rebellion. By choosing
to save Leroy, they refused to be culled. They fed him their own rations — starving their bellies to
prove their spirits couldn’t be bought. Leroy eventually changed. Over the months that followed, he
taught Ben how to read a mine wall for stress fractures, how to ration water so it lasted three
shifts instead of two, how to move through the shaft rows without drawing a guard’s eye. He became
the older brother Ben never had — a man redeemed by the very people he’d tormented. It lasted eleven
months. A guard cornered Gretchen near the water pump one evening — not for any infraction, just
because he could. Leroy saw it happen and did the only thing his old instincts knew how to do. He
put the guard on the ground with a single blow. The guard survived. Leroy didn’t. Andre’s men came
for him before dawn. Ben heard the scuffle through the wall — the thud of boots, a single shout cut
short, and then the sound of something heavy being dragged across the grit. By the time he reached
the door, the path outside was empty except for a dark smear that led toward the disposal tunnels
and stopped at the dark. Ben never saw Leroy again. The system took him quietly, the way it took
everything that drew its attention. Just an absence where a man had been, and a lesson that branded
itself into his chest: Leroy had used violence to protect someone he loved, and the machine had
swallowed him whole. Not because the violence was wrong — it wasn’t. But because in this system, a
fist was just an excuse for the machine to do what it had always wanted to do. The system didn’t
punish cruelty. It punished disruption. And violence, no matter how righteous, was the loudest
disruption there was. That was the night Ben decided. Not a vow spoken aloud — just a quiet closing
of a door inside himself. He would find a way to fight that didn’t give the machine an excuse to
take another Leroy. Gage watched Ben’s twitching face. Ben’s mind was somewhere else — back on a
doorstep with Leroy bleeding into the dirt, back in the moment Oscar had taught him what to do when
the man who had hurt you arrived dying at your door. Ben was holding onto that. Ben was holding onto
it because he could feel the chamber trying to take it away. And in that moment Gage understood. The
chamber didn’t just want their muscles. It wanted to erase the human moments from their history. It
wanted to delete the empathy that allowed them to act as a group. Oscar had been right — the Trinity
harvested the capacity to care, and the Initiation was the threshing floor. If Otto was the
alignment and Gage was the force, Ben was the Binding Agent — the one whose resolve to abstain from
violence, whose stubborn insistence on seeing a neighbor instead of a competitor, was the only thing
keeping them from turning on each other in the dark. Without that glue, Gage’s strength was just a
weapon looking for a target, and Otto’s precision was just a machine with no purpose. “He’s not just
dreaming of a sandwich,” Gage whispered to Otto, his own mind clearing. “He’s holding the line. If
he breaks, we all become bots.” Gage’s fingers traced the Logic Strip. He remembered Camilla’s
return from the Heights — two full days gone, quiet hands stained with industrial grease, breath a
panicked rattle. She hadn’t just found the strip; she’d hunted it in the treacherous waste-chutes of
the Mid-Spires. She’d read about the strips in one of Pappy’s hidden Scripts — a faded medical
pamphlet from the buried world. The Scripts had taught her the legend; the salvage taught her the
rest. She hadn’t told him about the automated culling-sweeps that turned scavengers into grit, or
how she’d spent hours buried under pressurized medical scrap to evade the Technocrats’ thermal
sensors. She had risked her own deletion by the system to pull this single sliver of clarity from
the elites’ trash. She had been preparing for his Initiation almost since the day she could read.
Pappy had warned her, in fragments, about what they did to the children inside: that the food was
the trick, that the chamber didn’t kill the body but emptied the head, that anyone who went in came
out less. Years of watching Hollows on the surface had confirmed his fears. By the time Gage’s
seventeenth birthday approached, she had been collecting tools and warnings for him like armor,
scrap by scrap, against the day the gates would open and he would walk in unprepared. Holding it now
in the dark, Gage didn’t see a diagnostic tool. He felt the weight of her sacrifice — a piece of her
insight bought with her own safety. She had faced the system’s most lethal protocols so he wouldn’t
face them blind. Gage stood, legs shaking but mind finally seeing clearly. They knew the weapon now
— the water and the food, dosed by a lottery that turned every meal into a coin flip. They could
stop drinking. They could ration what was clean. And they were still standing — not because they
were the strongest kids in the tunnel, but because Ben had stopped him from charging Icepick, and
steadied Otto when his hand still shook, and held them to the one sentence that mattered: We stay
together. We think. Then we move. Gage didn’t have a name for what that was worth. He just knew it
was the only thing they had that the system hadn’t managed to take.

ch004.xhtml Three days. In the brutal calculus of Miner Town, three days was the precise duration
required to wither the human spirit and begin the biological harvest. Every child feared it from the
moment they understood the weight of the mountain — a dark transition called the Initiation. “The
beginning of a glorious future,” the sector warden they called Icepick would drone at every
ceremony, his voice as cold as the deep-vein coal he coveted. Standing on his platform, he looked
down at the intake not as children, but as raw materials. “This is the day you earn your keep. The
day you stop being a mouth to feed and start being a hand that gives.” Gage stood in the dust-choked
queue with Otto and Ben, feeling the iron weight of the moment pressing into his shoulders. He
turned his head one last time and caught his family in the dim light. He didn’t just look at them —
he paused at the threshold, his gaze lingering on Camilla as if memorizing the lines of her face for
a much longer journey. The scar on his brow seemed to pulse. Behind him, beyond the queue, Camilla
tugged at Rita’s sleeve. “Why did Gage look at us like he was saying goodbye, Ma? Isn’t he coming
back after three days?” “Of course he’ll be back,” Rita replied, though her voice lacked truth.
She’d seen too many kids march into that tunnel only to return as Hollows. “He just won’t be the
same.” Legend had it that Icepick himself designed the ceremony to ensure every recruit was broken
before they ever set foot in the active shafts. As the massive iron gates slid open with a soul-
jarring screech, a heavy air enveloped the kids. Yet once swallowed by darkness, the initial fear
briefly evaporated, replaced by a calculated deception. For a few hours, the youngsters wandered
freely. To them, there was no surveillance, no guards, and most importantly, no hunger. Each recruit
carried a vacuum-sealed pouch of food — Spire-made tech that felt like a king’s fortune in a land of
scarcity. Gage tore into the heavy plastic, and a cloud of thick white steam billowed out, smelling
so rich it made his head spin. Warm, buttery potatoes and the savory salt of poultry. To a boy who’d
spent his life eating grey starch mush, it felt like holding an edible cloud in his blackened
fingers. They ate like starving animals, washing it down with cool, clear water that felt like a
lost memory of rain — though something at the back of the throat warned them, a thin metallic tang
like a copper coin held under the tongue. Gage lowered the cup. The light caught a rainbow shimmer
on the surface — a slick that hadn’t been there before. He could feel it now, a hum behind his eyes,
his pulse climbing for no reason. A chemical warning he’d swallowed too late. The food hadn’t been a
gift. It had been a delivery — though it would be hours before he’d know what it had delivered: a
rainbow fuel designed to lock the nervous system into permanent wakefulness until the mind
fractured. Icepick wasn’t feeding children. He was priming them. As Ben laughed, face smeared with
grease, Gage couldn’t shake Pappy’s voice. This was exactly how the Great Blind Eye had begun — not
with a bang, but with a bribe. Pappy had told him stories of the old world trading the sky itself
for a few more hours of fake winter, and now, looking at the sterile plastic in his hands, Gage saw
the pattern repeating. The Trinity wasn’t feeding them out of mercy; they were fattening the Engines
before the harvest. He looked at the silver needles on the horizon and realized that for the elite
in their isolated ecospheres, this Initiation was just a high-stakes strategy game, and he, Ben, and
Otto were finally on the board. High above, Elara adjusted her interface. She watched three heat-
signatures huddled against a salt-stained wall. To her, Gage, Ben, and Otto weren’t children
experiencing their first full stomach; they were newly activated units on her ledger. She noted the
Engine sharing his polymer pouch — a deviation in resource management — with a flick of her finger.
After the meal, the youngsters gathered to compare pouches and trade rumors — what each had heard
about the Initiation, which older sibling had survived, which had come back as a Hollow. But as
hours bled together, horror settled in. Gage realized they’d been awake for nearly eighteen hours
without rest. By the second evening, panic took root. Their hands ached, their backs felt like
stiffened hide, but their minds remained wide, terrifyingly awake. They tried racing through the
tunnels to exhaust their bodies — only blistered feet and more pain. Still no sleep. By the twenty-
fourth hour, the gathering broke apart. Conversation came in fragments now — sentences that started
in one mouth and ended in another that had forgotten what was being said. The kids who had been
trading rumors a few hours earlier could not remember what each other had told them. Some sat
against the walls staring; others walked the tunnel running their fingers over the stone like they
were searching for something. The voices echoed differently now. Words ran into themselves. By
thirty hours, the hallucinations began. A girl Gage didn’t know stood in the middle of the chamber
and shouted at someone who wasn’t there, raising her arms to defend herself from a blow that never
came. Another boy laughed at the ceiling for nearly an hour without stopping. Gage saw the salt
patterns on the walls move — slow at first, then quickening into something almost alive. He looked
away. When he looked back, the patterns were still moving. By thirty-six, the bodies started to fail
in different directions. Some kids collapsed where they stood and lay motionless, eyes open,
breathing shallow, unresponsive when shaken. Others struck themselves — pulling at their own hair,
biting their hands, running into the walls just to feel something other than the wakefulness. Otto
held Ben back from one of these. Gage tried to call out and found his voice gone, swallowed by the
same sandstone that was already collecting at the back of his throat. By the forty-eight-hour mark,
time didn’t flow; it fractured into jagged shards. Gage tried to lift his arm, but it felt like a
stone pillar fused to his shoulder. Every time he blinked, it was as if sandpaper scraped his
eyeballs — the Trinity had removed the off-switch from his brain. Then came the hum. Deep in the
walls, the Stubborn Carbon began its predatory song — a piezoelectric pulse that vibrated in
perfect, cruel synchronization with the rainbow fuel in his veins. The world began to melt. Walls
seemed to breathe, expanding and contracting like the ribcage of a giant beast. His vision trailed
in sickening lag, leaving ghostly shadows that took a split second to snap into place. Gage turned
his sandpaper eyes toward the shadows where the Urban Servants stood like statues. He couldn’t form
the words anymore, but he remembered something he’d thought about them at the gate — that they had
been here before, that they had been him, that this room was where they had been made. In this
shimmering void, the rainbow fuel stripped away the barriers between Gage’s memory and the present.
In the corner of his vision, he saw Pappy — not the vibrant storyteller, but the hollowed man from
the sun-scorched run. Every calorie he’d consumed felt like lead in his gut. It wasn’t just food; it
was a stolen life. “I am the Engine,” Gage whispered, his voice a jagged rasp. “But the fuel… the
fuel is their blood.” The hallucination tried to pull him under, but the Map on his brow flared with
phantom heat. The scar was a compass. He pictured Camilla in the shack, her quiet hands scraping rot
off a copper lamp. If he let his mind melt here, she would be left alone. The Pappy Debt couldn’t be
repaid by dying in a hole; it could only be settled by becoming the fire that burned the Trinity’s
ledger to ash. “Not yet,” he growled. “Not before I burn the gates.” Beside him, Otto sat perfectly
still. The same drug was in his blood, the same hum in the walls — but where Gage was fighting to
stay above the surface, Otto had gone inward. He remembered the red Porsche replica he’d salvaged as
a boy — the toy he’d called his Otto-mobile before he was old enough to know that wasn’t its name.
The name had stuck to the boy long after the toy was lost. He had been six the first time he
understood what brave-and-stupid meant. He had seen four older boys cornering an old man in the
alley behind the ration cart. The old man was small, frail, easy to break. Otto, who was small and
frail and easy to break himself, had charged at the four of them with a piece of pipe. He woke up in
his own shack three days later, with five stitches in his scalp and his right hand wrapped in rags.
He couldn’t remember being carried home. He couldn’t remember anything between the alley and the
rags. On the third night of his recovery, half-asleep, he saw a blue light moving past his shack —
faint, the kind of glow you might miss if you blinked. He followed it. It led him to an empty back-
row shed where the old man was sitting cross-legged on the floor, looking nothing like the figure
who had been beaten in the alley. Stronger. More upright. A pale blue light at the edge of his skin
like the last shimmer of something the world had forgotten how to make. A butterfly rested on his
shoulder, wings opening and closing slowly. “My name is Shu Chan,” the old man said. “Where I come
from is dust now. I was the last guard of a place that no longer exists. I have been waiting for
someone to find me.” He touched Otto’s forehead with one finger, and for a heartbeat Otto stood
inside a stillness so deep that the world made sense. Otto spent the next two years in that shed,
learning what Shu Chan called the long-lost art — a way of holding the body that turned breath and
skeleton into a fortress no chemical and no blade could enter. Shu Chan called the discipline
karate, a word from the buried world. He called the light at his skin the breath; he called the
butterfly the proof. “I did not pick you, Otto,” Shu Chan said the night before he died. “You picked
me, that day in the alley. I had been waiting a long time for a successor. I had begun to think no
one would come.” Otto had gone home that night and slept with a smile, knowing he finally meant
something to someone whose meaning could not be taken from him. Now, in the black eternity of the
Initiation chamber, the smile was a ghost — but the breath Shu Chan had taught him was not. He held
it. He held it. He held it. Gage watched him, drawing strength from that silence. They were no
longer children being unmade; they were the friction that would eventually seize the machine.
Something had hardened in Gage that the drug couldn’t dissolve. The Engine was finally idling in
sync with the earth, waiting for the moment to strike.
