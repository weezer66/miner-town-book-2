# Chapter 3: Found One

*He was a man carrying a piece of corporate property welded to his skeleton that he couldn’t use and they couldn’t be bothered to collect.*

— Book 1, Chapter 14: Phantom Leg

## Now: The treatment room, beneath the city

The tape was the problem.

It sat across the back of her right hand in a clean white cross, holding the line flat, and when they laid it down it had caught a single hair. Every time she bent the knuckle, the hair pulled. She bent the knuckle. It pulled. She bent it again to see. The bag above her hung from a small brass clip shaped like a beetle, the only thing in the room anyone had bothered to make pretty.

She hadn't slept. She'd gone under a few times and come up in the same place: grey ceiling, a hum in the wall too even to be a pump, and the left hand that wasn't there curled into a fist so tight she could feel the nails. She let it stay closed. Opening it would take attention she was saving.

Each time she surfaced, the oldest medic was in the chair by the wall, hands folded, scrubbed pink to the wrist, and the clear bag had gone down by exactly as much as Camilla would have guessed.

"You're counting the drip," the medic said.

"Somebody should."

"Somebody is." She got up and checked the shoulder without being asked, two fingers pressed lightly at the edge of the seal. The sealant had set hard and glassy overnight, the blue darker where it was thickest. "It holds. You lost about a third of your blood. It's coming back. And you're angry about the tape."

"You said I would be."

"Good. Angry's better than quiet."

---

The medic went out and came back with a man and a case.

He was thin, with a pencil stub behind each ear, both sharpened down to nothing, and ink on his thumbs. He set the case on the end of the bed, opened it toward her, and stood back. He didn't take the thing out. He let her look.

"He builds them," the medic said. "You can touch anything. You can say no to all of it, now or later."

It lay in a cut-out bed of grey foam: an arm, shoulder to fingertips. Dull grey metal, unpolished, with a grain to it like a well-used file. Jointed at the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist, and the joints didn't pretend to be anything else. The fingers were long, longer than hers had been, lying a little curled. The thumb was set lower than a thumb should be. It gave the whole hand the look of something asking.

Camilla read it front to back. Seams on the fingers finer than a hair. A darker ring at the top, where it would meet her. No cables, no battery door, no screws, and that bothered her most. A thing with no screws was a thing you weren't meant to open.

"It fits to the shoulder," the engineer said. His voice was dry, used to rooms where nobody asked him anything. "A cup sits over the seal. It doesn't cut. Where the arm stopped, the nerves that ran it didn't. They're still sending. It listens to them."

"The arm I had keeps making a fist."

He took that as data. "Then it will make a fist. For a few days it'll be slow. Half a breath behind you. Every body sends a little differently. It'll learn your timing."

"How long have you had this?" she said. The engineer didn't answer that one. She filed it next to the screws.

She was already somewhere else.

Gerald, back home: the man from Trinity-2 who'd lost a leg on the highway. He'd told her about the replacement they'd sold him: a sleek leg that answered to its maker, until one morning it didn't. A license lapsed overnight. No alert. The city had stopped recognizing him, and the leg belonged to the city, so the leg stopped recognizing him too. He'd dragged himself to a chair on his elbows while a barista watched.

"Who owns it?" she said.

"It was made here, from what this place has," the engineer said. "It's registered nowhere above. No account attached. No license, because there's no one to sell one. Nobody will come asking for it back."

"That's not what I mean." Her right hand had found the edge of the blanket and was folding it into a hard crease. "Can it be switched off?"

The pencils went still.

It was the medic who answered. She came and stood where Camilla could see her face. Camilla noticed that, and filed it.

"Yes." No pause before the word. "From this room. By me, or by him, if your shoulder rejects it, or if it starts hurting you in a way we can't fix. You'll be told before it happens, and you'll be told why."

"That's you two. Who else?"

The medic looked at the engineer, and the engineer looked at the arm.

"I won't tell you no one," the medic said. "I haven't opened every door in this place, and I won't swear to what's behind the ones I haven't. Here's what I can tell you. Nothing has ever been taken off a person in this room against their will, in my time here, and my time is long. I'd know. I'd have been the one holding the instrument." She paused. "The rest isn't mine to give. There's someone you can ask. You'll see him again."

Flitzberg, then. The man with the dolls. Camilla put the gap where she could find it again.

She folded the crease once more and let it go.

Ben's hands, shaking on a spray can. Gage carrying Otto out with nobody to take the other side. Every panel she'd opened in the last week, holding the housing shut with her knee because there wasn't a second hand to do it.

And the last machine anyone had put on her had been the metal ring the first trial room clamped to her skull, and nobody had asked her then. This one was asking.

"Yes," she said. "Do it."

---

They did it with the lights up and her watching, because she said she'd watch or they'd have to do it without her, and the medic took her at her word. The scissors went through her sleeve without a sound.

The cup was cold. It settled over the seal, and for three breaths it was only weight, heavier than she'd guessed, dragging her down on that side. Then it warmed, and the warmth went in deeper than the sealant had, all the way to the place in her shoulder that had been clenched since the drone found her at the railing. A sting ran in under the seal and spread along her collarbone and up her neck in branches. She knew what that was. It was finding the wires.

She set her teeth. She'd tied a tourniquet with them. She could hold still for this.

"Breathe," the medic said.

"I am."

"Breathe more."

The sting went and left something behind. She'd been braced for pain. What came instead felt like being heard: a line of her that had been talking into nothing all night, and something on the other end finally picking up.

The engineer set the arm into the cup and turned a collar she couldn't see. Something clicked home. Then weight, real weight, pulling on the shoulder in the old direction, and her body, which had been leaning right all night without telling her, straightened up on its own.

"Don't try to move it," the medic said. "Think about moving it. To the arm, thinking is moving."

The hand that wasn't there had been closed all night. She did something she had no name for, at the place in her head where she had always kept her left hand, and told it to open.

Nothing.

Then the grey fingers on the blanket, at the end of the grey arm that was suddenly the end of *her*, opened. All together. Late.

Her chest locked. She could see the fingers, and under the hum she could almost feel them, and the almost was worse than nothing: a hand at the far end of a long pipe, answering a question she'd already stopped asking. Her skin went cold from scalp to heel. There was a thing lying against her that was stronger than she was, and her right hand went to the metal wrist to hold it down.

The metal wrist let her.

She looked at it. Then she closed the fingers again, slowly, and watched them follow: index, middle, ring, little, each a fraction behind the order she sent. A machine with a delay in it was a machine with a timing, and a timing could be learned. She opened and closed the hand five times, counting the gap, and on the fifth it was shorter.

"It's listening," she said. "With what? There's no wire in this. Where does it *decide*?"

The engineer looked up from his slate, and something finally moved in his face. "There's a part of it that listens. It isn't a wire. I can show you where it is. I can't tell you how."

"Later," the medic said. "For now you lie still, think about the question, and give me less trouble." She lowered the bed a notch. "I have patients who do as they're told. I've never once enjoyed them."

Camilla lay back. When she thought about her left hand, it moved. She thought about it a great many times before the lights came up.

---

When the lights came up behind the dark window, before anyone else was awake, she asked to walk.

The medic let her, an arm under her good elbow, the bag rolling beside them on a wheel that ticked. Nine steps from the bed to the connecting door. She counted them. The ninth nearly put her on the floor.

The tank stood taller than Gage. A clear cylinder on a dark base, full of something thicker than water, faintly blue, the blue of the sealant can. Otto hung inside it with his eyes closed, a clear mask over his nose and mouth and a line running up from it into the lid. His arms floated a little away from his sides. His hair lifted and settled. He wasn't standing and he wasn't lying down. He'd stopped swimming and the gel hadn't let him sink.

A bubble had come loose from the mask seal and was climbing beside his cheek. She watched it. It took as long as it would take to walk across a room.

Then she looked at everything that wasn't him, because that she could do something with. Heat off the base. Two pipes, one wrapped in white, one bare. A panel with four colored lines, three moving and one flat.

"Tell me how it works. All of it."

The medic had expected that. "The gel's cycled. Warm, then cool, then warm again; the wrapped pipe carries the heat in. While it's warm we tell his muscles to work. Small signals, through the gel. It carries current the way salt water does, only better. Nothing he'd feel as pain. His body thinks it's hauling. Then we cool it, and his body thinks it's resting."

"How long's a cycle?"

"An hour, a bit more. Lots of short days instead of one long one. A body gets used to a real day. We want his to keep being asked." She watched the blue go a shade paler near the base. "His heart's strong. His breath is steady. The instruments can't find anything wrong with him. Whatever's missing isn't in the body. So we keep the body doing what a body does, and we keep it loud. If any part of him is near enough to hear, we want it to hear something worth coming back to."

The paler blue reached his hands. Otto's fingers tightened by a hair, all ten at once, told to haul by a machine.

Camilla's metal fingers, at her side, tightened too.

She hadn't sent them anywhere.

They opened again, slowly. She put her good hand over the wrist and held it there, and didn't say anything to the medic. She didn't know why she didn't. She only knew she wanted to understand it before anyone else decided what it meant.

"Has it done anything?" she said. "On the panel."

"His lines have been flat since the trial room." The medic touched the panel's edge. "Except once. Last night, in the hour your brother and Ben sat with him, one line moved. I don't know what it means. I'd be lying if I said I did."

"Which line?"

The medic pointed. The flat one.

Camilla looked at it until her eyes watered. It stayed flat. She didn't need it to move. She needed to know which one to watch.

"When they wake up," she said, "don't bring them to me. Take them to Otto first."

"They'll want to see you first."

"A line moved when they sat with him. So they sit with him first." She leaned on the medic's arm to turn, and the nine steps back looked like more. "That's not a favor to me. That's a reading."

"I'll tell them it was your instruction."

"Tell them it was my words."

---

She was in the chair by the window when they came.

The dark glass worked one way; she'd checked. Through it she watched the tank room's outer door open, the medic step aside, Ben come in first, and stop.

He went straight to the tank and put both hands flat on the glass, fingers spread. His mouth was moving. Gage came no further than two steps past the door. He stood with his feet planted and asked the medic something, and she answered at length, and Camilla watched her brother listen: the muscle in his jaw working, and the old knife scar across his brow, the one they called his map, pulling tight and loose and tight.

Ben put his forehead to the glass beside Otto's hanging hand. Gage let him.

She got up.

It took two tries. The arm pulled her left and the room swung, and she held the chair back until the floor agreed to stay put. Sitting would have been easier. She could be in the bed when they came through, blanket to her chin, and they'd talk low, as everyone had around Pappy, their grandfather, at the end. But Gage had already seen her on the floor twice, and she'd heard what *half-dead* sounded like in his mouth. She was going to be standing.

She let go of the chair. She put her weight even on both feet and the arm where it would be seen. When she heard the connecting door, she was ready.

Gage saw her first. She watched it hit him: she was standing, and that reached his face before anything else could. Then his eyes went to her left side and stopped, and she let him look, because there was no point making him do it later.

He crossed the room before anyone spoke and put his arms around her on the side that was still hers, so carefully she nearly laughed. Gage had never been careful with anything in his life except a pencil. Her good hand came up at once and grabbed a fistful of the back of his shirt.

The metal hand came up too.

She felt it go, half a breath behind the rest of her. It rose off her side and paused at his back, high, between the shoulder blades, as if checking its own permission. She was too busy holding on with the other hand to tell it anything. And the metal hand settled flat on her brother's back with no more weight than a palm laid on a sleeping dog.

She could feel how much it could have done. All that strength resting against his spine.

She hadn't told it to be gentle.

"Lost an arm," she said into his shoulder. "Found one."

He didn't let go. Over his shoulder she could see Ben in the doorway, grinning and wiping his face with his sleeve, not coming any closer.

"You too," she said. "Come here."

He came. His chin knocked her temple, and he said, "Sorry, sorry," and didn't move, and she had them both, one hand of each kind.

Gage stepped back first. His eyes went to the arm and stayed there.

"Does it hurt?"

"No." She let Ben go. She lifted the arm and turned it, watching the elbow follow a fraction after her eyes. "It's late. A little behind me, every time. They say it listens to what's left of the nerves in my shoulder, and it'll learn my timing in a few days. Right now it's guessing." She flexed the fingers one after another, and they followed in order, each a little behind. "It guesses well."

"They put that on you overnight. While I was down the hall." His hands had closed. "Who decided?"

"They offered. I said yes." She gave him the look from across the dinner table, the one for when he was about to be slow about something. "What would you have done?"

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. Then he opened his hands.

She held the metal hand out to him, palm up. He'd done this once, back home, holding out a copper tag with their dead father's work number on it: *You read the system better than I do.* Her turn.

He took the hand and pressed the palm with his thumb, and she watched him feel it give, a little. Not like flesh, and not like iron either.

"It's stronger than me," he said.

"Much."

She closed the fingers. Late, and then all at once. His hand went in and didn't come out. His whole arm went hard, his shoulder jerked up, and he made a short, high, undignified noise she would keep for the rest of her life.

Ben laughed out loud. It cracked out of him, a sound she hadn't heard since the mine, and he clapped a hand over his own mouth.

"Cami."

"Late," she said, not letting go, "and still faster than you." She opened the fingers and winked at him. "It's learning what I want. Give it a week and it'll learn what you need telling, too. Which is usually the same thing twice."

He shook his hand out and turned it over, back and palm and fingers. No mark anywhere. That bothered him more than a mark would have.

"And it's learning you," he said.

"Yes."

She laid her good hand over the metal wrist and held it there, the way she'd held every machine she ever opened: Pappy's old radio, the water meter, the coil heater Ben had sworn was dead. A hand on the casing, still, feeling for what it did when it thought nobody was touching it. The arm hummed under her palm. Faint. Even.

"I'll learn it first," she said. "I'm faster."

Gage believed her. She watched it reach him: the scar on his brow went smooth, and his eyes came off the arm and onto her and stayed there, steady, from a long way back. She'd seen that face at Pappy's bed. The face of someone at the top of a shaft who's just been told how deep it goes, and is working out whether the rope is long enough.

He put his own hand over hers, over the metal, and left it there. Ben put his on top, because Ben would.

---

The connecting door opened behind them.

Flitzberg didn't come in. He stood in the doorway with his collar open over the marks Gage's fingers had left. He'd been there a while, she could tell from where he stood, and he wasn't pretending otherwise.

Gage moved half a step, between him and her. His hands stayed open. She saw him make them.

Flitzberg's eyes went to the metal hand before they came to her. He knew. She hadn't told anyone, and he knew.

"It moved before you decided to," Flitzberg said. "That is not the same as without you. It is still learning where you end."

Gage's head turned to her, Flitzberg still at the edge of his eye. "Moved."

"Twice," she said. "In the tank room, when Otto's hands did. And on your back, when I had hold of you."

Ben looked at the hand that had been under his a minute ago. He didn't step away from it. He put his own hand in his pocket, and she watched him notice that he had.

"That's not an answer," she said to Flitzberg.

"It is the true one. I do not have a better one yet."

She held up the metal hand. "Who else can switch it off?"

He didn't look at the medic. He didn't look away.

"I can."

Ben stopped breathing. She heard him stop.

"From where?"

"From anywhere in this place."

Gerald, on his elbows, and a barista watching.

Gage's open hands didn't move. A vein stood up along the back of the left one.

"Who else?" she said.

"Today. All of it, as I promised your brother." Flitzberg's voice was still scraped raw from last night. "You will have terms. Bring them."

"I've got them now."

"Then you will have better ones by the time I send for you."

He nodded once to the medic, and the door closed behind him without a sound.

The medic left breakfast on a cart in the doorway and didn't come in.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Ben said, "Cami—"

"Eat," she said.

---

They ate on her bed, Ben at the foot with his knees up, Gage on the edge so the mattress tipped, and she let the tilt pull her into his side. Ben kept tearing his bread into small pieces and not eating them. Nobody said Otto's name. Nobody said Flitzberg's.

Through the dark glass the tank stood lit in its own room, the gel going pale, then deeper, then pale. On the glass at the height of Otto's hands, Ben's two prints were still there, fingers spread, fogged at the palms, and in the gap between them, where a third hand would have gone, there was only blue.

She watched the prints shrink. She watched the flat line. She ate with the hand that was hers, and the other one lay on the blanket and closed, half a breath behind, every time she thought of Otto.
