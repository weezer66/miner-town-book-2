# Chapter 2: The Journey Ahead

*"The question is not what I did to you. The question is: what will you do to regain the freedom that was once your birthright?"*

— Book 1, Chapter 25: Don't Do It

## Now: The SACRIFICE Chamber

The count came back before anything else did.

*Five. Four.* She had been counting when she went under — counting for the boys at the railing, counting charges, counting seconds on a volcano that had never been there — and some stubborn part of her had kept the numbers warm in the dark, waiting for the rest of her to return and pick them up.

Then the cold came back. A metal floor under her cheek. The hiss of a spray can, and Ben's hand on her shoulder, shaking, holding her still while the sealant hardened over the place where her arm used to be.

Then the arm itself came back, which was worse, because it wasn't there.

Her left hand was clenched. She could feel it: the fingers curled into a fist, nails biting her palm, the wrist cocked at an angle that ached. She told the hand to open. Nothing happened, because there was no hand to tell. Gerald had described this to her across a bowl of Refined Mash, a lifetime ago. A leg that wasn't there, and hurt anyway. She had asked him three questions and known the answer before he finished the third. *Your brain still has the map. It just lost the territory.*

She had thought she understood him. She hadn't.

Then the sound.

It took her a moment to place it, because it wasn't a sound she'd ever heard her brother make. A wet, straining noise, half breath and half word, and under it the creak of fabric pulled tight. Camilla turned her head an inch, which was all her head would turn.

White light. The room had flooded with it while she was gone. And in the middle of the light, Gage — the whole six-foot-five, two-hundred-and-twenty-pound fact of him — had a man by the throat.

Mr. Flitzberg.

She checked his jaw first. She couldn't help it. She had watched Gerald's face slide off this man like smoke, and some part of her would be checking his jaw for the rest of her life. No seam. No flicker. Smooth pale skin, full cheeks, eyes that had never squinted against a violet sun. Tonight he was wearing his own face. She didn't know yet whether that was better.

"Why?" Gage's voice cracked down the middle. "Otto is here and still gone. Camilla is half-dead. What did we ever do to you?"

*Half-dead.* So that was what she looked like from up there.

Flitzberg didn't reach for Gage's hands. He didn't flinch. "The question is not what I did to you," he said, in the same smooth, made voice he'd used since the dolls and the cookies. "The question is: what will you do to regain the freedom that was once your birthright?"

"What does that mean?" Ben shouted, from right above her.

Flitzberg's eyes moved across them. Gage, Camilla, Ben, Otto.

Otto.

He was standing exactly where he'd stood when the simulation began, between Gage and the place Ben had been. Shoulders square. Feet planted. Chest rising, falling. Eyes open. Nothing behind them. The blue butterfly, the ring of stones, the smile, the wave — all of it had happened somewhere else, and Otto had stayed there, and left his body standing here to keep his place in line.

Four faces in the room that should have looked back at Flitzberg. Three did.

"You are ready now," Flitzberg said. "Ready for the journey ahead."

Gage's arm tightened.

Camilla knew that arm. She had watched it lift coal blocks heavier than she was, and she had watched it shake at the dinner table when he was trying not to say a thing. It was shaking now, and the shake wasn't weakness. It came from two parts of her brother pulling in opposite directions: everything in his body that wanted this man gone, and everything else in him that knew the three people behind him needed answers more than they needed a corpse.

"You are angry at the right person," Flitzberg said, with what throat he had left. "Do not let that comfort you. The right person is also the only one who will answer your questions, and a dead man answers nothing."

Gage didn't let go.

And that was when Camilla understood something about the room. There were no guards in it. No one behind the glass was moving. The man who had sealed corridors with electricity and dropped her into a tank of eels had walked into this room alone, on purpose, and let the strongest boy in Sector One put a hand on his windpipe. A man who did that had either lost his mind or wanted to be within reach.

She didn't think he had lost his mind.

"Gage," she said. It came out as a scrape.

His head turned a fraction.

"Stop."

He had promised her. In a corridor outside four doors, a day or a lifetime ago, she had said *if I tell you to stop, you stop, even if every part of you is screaming to charge*, and he had nodded, and it had been the first real promise he'd ever made her.

His fingers opened, one at a time, as if each one had to be persuaded separately. He stepped back. Not far.

"Then answer," he said. "Why? What did we ever do to you?"

"Nothing." Flitzberg rubbed his throat once and let his hand fall. "You were not punished. You were chosen. I understand that from where you stand the two are difficult to tell apart. That is the fault of the city that raised you, and it is a fair complaint, and I will hear all of it. But first your sister needs more than a spray can."

He raised one hand, slowly, where they could all see it, and beckoned toward the glass.

They came in quietly: two medics with a wheeled stretcher, a third carrying cases. Ben was between them and Camilla before the door finished opening, his whole small body planted like a wall. The oldest of the medics — a woman with grey at her temples and steady hands — stopped, set her case on the floor, opened it, and turned it around so Ben could see everything inside.

Camilla looked too. She couldn't help that either. Clean instruments, laid out in fitted foam. A sealant canister twice the size of the one in the wall compartment. Coiled tubing. A row of vials with handwritten labels, which meant someone here still wrote things by hand.

"Clean sealant," the medic said. "Blood volume. Pain control that won't fog her. You can walk beside the stretcher with your hand on her shoulder the whole way. We've done this before, and we've never once needed family to let go."

Ben looked back at Gage. Gage looked at the case — at instruments that gleamed, in a world where everything they'd ever owned was scratched — and something in his exhaustion made the decision his pride couldn't. He nodded.

"Not yet," Camilla said.

The medic looked down at her.

"I'm not leaving till he answers about Otto." She swallowed, because her mouth tasted of copper. "You can do the blood thing here."

The medic glanced at Flitzberg. Flitzberg, after a moment, inclined his head. So they lifted her onto the stretcher right there on the chamber floor, with a care she had never seen anyone spend on a miner's body, and hung a bag of something red from a hook, and the pain pulled back an inch without taking her mind along with it. That, more than the light or the calm man rubbing his throat, made the room real. Somewhere under the city they'd been told was the whole world, there were people whose hands were gentle by training.

A medic had gone to Otto. She shone a light in his eyes, read an instrument, and looked at Flitzberg with a face that was its own report.

"What's wrong with him?" Ben's voice cracked on the last word. "He's right there. He's breathing. Why won't he come back?"

Flitzberg looked at Otto — the boy who had held ten stones over a lake of fire until his nose bled, and then let them close over him — and gave the answer.

"I do not know."

"You did this —"

"Yes. And I do not know." He let it stand. "Our instruments say nothing is wrong with him. Heart, breath, every working of his body, steady and well. By every measure we have, he is unharmed. The instruments are wrong. Some part of your friend spent itself in that room, and it has not come back. We will keep his body strong and his door open. Past that, I will not pretend to a knowledge I do not have. You have been lied to with confidence your whole lives. I will not add to it."

"That's not good enough," Gage said.

"No," Flitzberg agreed. "It is not. It is only true."

Camilla watched him the way she watched a machine she didn't trust yet — the hands, the shoulders, the small places where a fault shows before it fails. She couldn't find the lie. That frightened her more than finding one would have.

"The guard at the four doors," Gage said, flat. "He said you pass all four, you live. You fail — and he didn't finish it. Finish it."

"Some who fail die," Flitzberg said. "Not by our hand — understand that exactly. No one is executed in this ring. But the rooms you walked are not theater. The drone that took your sister's arm was not pretending, and the heat that softened her sealant was real heat. A test that cannot hurt you cannot measure you. When a team breaks in those rooms, the rooms do not become gentle because the people in them have stopped being able. Others fail more quietly, and serve here in smaller ways. A few go home with a story that explains their absence, and they are watched, kindly, for the rest of their lives. That is the finished sentence."

"And Camilla's arm?" Ben was crying and not hiding it. "And Otto? Was that the price of your test?"

"Yes."

The word landed alone.

"Your sister's arm was such a price," Flitzberg said. "It fell on her. It could have fallen on any of you. And she paid it the way I have seen few grown soldiers pay anything — on her feet, telling the three of you to get back to the panel while she bled. Do not remember her as someone this place happened to. Remember her as someone who met it standing."

"Don't," Camilla said.

He looked at her.

"Don't make it a story." Her voice shook and she let it. "I didn't meet anything. I counted. Somebody had to count."

For a moment Flitzberg said nothing at all. Then he nodded once, the way a man nods when he's been corrected and knows it, and turned back to the boys.

"You were not being tortured," he said. "You were being read. And what we read in the four of you, this ring has been looking for, for a very long time."

"Read for what?" Gage said. "You keep talking around it. You said freedom. Birthright. What does that *mean*?"

Flitzberg was quiet. Camilla had the strange impression that he wasn't reaching for words so much as measuring an amount, the way Rita measured water.

"There was a time," he said, "when the people of this region moved where they chose, sold what they dug, and had a say in who set their quotas. I am not telling you a legend. The records still exist — deeds, charters, pay-books, rolls of names — old enough that the city above has stopped guarding them, because it assumes no one who cares is left who can read them. Nobody stole your freedom in the night. It was transferred: lawfully, gradually, in documents. An emergency measure here. A safety regulation there. A lottery introduced as a mercy. And a transfer always has paperwork."

*If you can read the manual,* Pappy used to say, tapping the Master Scripts he kept under the floorboards, *you own the machine.* Camilla had believed him her whole life. Lying on the stretcher with a bag of someone else's blood running into her, she found she still did.

Ben had gone very still — the stillness that meant his mind was working through a pattern and didn't like where it ended.

"Paper," he said, and there was no hope in it. "You can show a man the deed to his own house while he's standing in the rain outside it. The people who signed those papers own the offices where papers matter. They own the clerks and the guards at the doors. There's no counter in Miner Town where you can stand in line and ask for your freedom back."

"Correct," Flitzberg said. "You cannot appeal to them — they own the places where appeals are heard. And you cannot rise against them, for a harder reason. Think what happens in Miner Town after any trouble. Rations shorten. Quotas climb. Guards double at the gates, and examples are made in the shack-rows, of people who had nothing to do with the trouble at all. The city above does not fight its challengers. It bills their neighbors."

"Then what's left?" Gage said. "If we can't ask and we can't fight — what is this place? What are *you*?"

"That answer is long," Flitzberg said, "and it's owed to you whole: what this place is, how it came to be, and what it wants with the four of you. You will have all of it. Not tonight. I am not hiding it from you. I am saving it for when you can weigh it."

Gage stared at him, and some of the stare was refusal — and then Ben swayed where he stood, and the refusal went out of it. They had nothing left, and all three of them knew it.

Flitzberg turned toward the inner doors and stopped, and said the rest without turning back.

"You asked what you ever did to us. Nothing. Hold on to that. The rest can wait." The doors opened onto a corridor whose lights woke one after another, reaching ahead into the dark like stepping stones. "Tonight, eat. Sleep. Sit with your friend, and speak to him. For all our instruments can tell, he hears every word. Your sister will be stronger by morning. And the answers will still be here when you wake. They have waited almost a century. They can wait one more night."

The medics lifted the stretcher. Ben walked beside it with his hand on her shoulder, exactly as promised.

Behind them, Gage went to Otto. He said Otto's name once, quietly, and got nothing back. Then he bent, took Otto's arm across his shoulders, and lifted him — and Camilla, twisting on the stretcher to see, had the strangest feeling of the world folding over on itself. She had seen this before. Outside the mine gates, on the fourth morning, Gage walking out of the dark with Otto across his shoulders and Ben by the collar, while the guards waited for a broken boy who never came.

He was carrying Otto out again. This time Otto didn't know it.

As they passed the crystal at the center of the room, she looked up. Three quarters of it glowed a steady green. The last quarter still pulsed a cold, slow blue, like something that hadn't been decided yet.

Camilla counted them out of the room, because counting was how you held a thing still long enough to fix it. Four of them through the door. Three of them awake.

It was a count she didn't know how to finish.
