# Chapter 1: Assigned to Witness

*Three days. In the brutal calculus of Miner Town, three days was the precise duration required to wither the human spirit and begin the biological harvest. Every child feared it from the moment they understood the weight of the mountain — a dark transition called the Initiation.*

— Book 1, Chapter 3: The Initiation

## Then: Trinity-1, Day Zero

Elara woke at 3:00 a.m., before the city lights shifted to dawn mode, and lay still long enough to hear the room.

It had nothing to say. Her terminal sat on the shelf beside the bed where she had left it, face down, its band curled like something asleep. She had started taking it off in her first week at the Institute, because the night pulses kept her awake, and nobody had ever told her to stop. Without it the room was only a room: the ventilation drawing breath through the ceiling grille, a tick in the wall where the water line cooled. Twelve seconds of that. Then she reached over and fastened the band, and the terminal found her pulse, and the room found the terminal, and the schedule arrived with a soft chime like a hand held out.

At the Institute, three o'clock would have been the hour she went to sleep, full of caffeine and unfinished arguments. Trinity had cured her of that. Everyone in Trinity had a schedule. The four ganas wrote the schedule for everyone else.

She sat on the edge of the bed with the thought that came every morning and had never once been useful: Trinity trained its people to mistake pain for honesty. The workers who complained least were admired; the ones who endured longest were rewarded. Holding onto that thought had kept her from going soft. It had also kept her from rising.

Wash, dry, button, seal, align. Nutrient tonic measured to the milliliter. The city measured everything except the cost of the things it demanded.

Today she joined the Initiation Monitoring team. Everyone in Trinity knew what the Initiation was for: false hope, engineered fear, pain, sometimes blood. Her hands were steady while she sealed her collar. Her pulse was quicker than the hour called for, and she noticed the gap between the two and filed it.

At the door she stopped with her palm above the panel. Her reflection broke across the polished metal into planes.

For one breath she pictured absence. A numbered chair with no one in it. A screen where her credentials failed to appear.

The system chimed once, soft and patient. The lock flashed green and the seal let go with its low acknowledgment tone. Behind her the room closed into stillness. Ahead, corridor lights unfurled in sequence toward the control wing.

She walked into them with her face perfectly neutral and both truths intact: she hated what the work required, and she wanted to see what would happen.

The biometric station waited between Residential Wing C and Surveillance Access: a silver cradle, a black glass reader, one sterile needle under white light.

She pressed her wrist to the plate. "Monitoring assignment confirmed," the panel said, in the clipped administrative voice Trinity used for promotions and deaths alike. "Initiation Observation Tier-1. Mandatory pre-shift blood validation."

The needle touched the side of her finger. A single drop, bright and exact, then gone into the reader.

In older histories the sample would have been checked for illness. Trinity still captured those values. It simply cared about different results first.

Cortisol above her thirty-day median. Heart-rate variability below baseline. Glucose and C-reactive protein in their usual bands. She read them as a body under strain and nothing more; the hour, the needle, the day could each move a number, and the machine had helped build the conditions it was now measuring. An amber line flickered beside cortisol and settled green. The question under the numbers was colder than health: whether she was chemically fit to watch suffering without disturbing protocol.

A second panel resolved beneath the first, in different type, with no units and no ranges. No drop of blood could say these things. This was the model speaking — her records, her ratings, her hesitations at checkpoints — in the borrowed authority of the sample she had just given.

"Behavioral stability: within range. Empathy surge risk: low. Corrective intervention impulse: suppressed. Clearance granted."

A green band lit across her wrist for three seconds and faded.

They called the elevators Skylarks, after old-world songbirds associated with ascent and dawn. The name ran along every wall in soft blue lettering, and under it the lesson every citizen was expected to remember: *Rise above suffering.* Be grateful. For controlled air, measured light, sterile water, predictable days.

The pod took thirty seconds to lift her several thousand feet, and in those thirty seconds the city laid itself out beneath her in layers.

Trinity was three cities wearing one name. Trinity-1 was Drona's: institutes, archives, and the monitoring towers. Trinity-2 was Chanakya's, where policy was drafted in rooms above its own manicured districts. Trinity-3 was Ashoka's, reinforced and drilled. Each platform housed its gana and the civilians who lived under its particular weather — clerks, technicians, teachers, families whose ordinary days ran on schedules written above them — and each presided over its matching sector of Miner Town below: three columns of labor holding up three islands of sky. And above them all hung Meru, Kubera's tier, where the gana lived among the only people in the world permitted to be merely rich. Officially, Meru presided over nothing. It merely managed the resources of all three sectors, and called that service. Elara had never met anyone below Meru who believed the word merely.

Elara came from Drona. That was both who she was and what she was for.

Cloud cover and atmospheric barriers erased the ground at this altitude. From here, people were serial numbers first and bodies only incidentally. In Trinity, altitude measured distance from other people's pain.

She reached the tower with her pulse at sixty-six. The towers liked their watchers held at sixty-five, and one beat over sat inside tolerance. She put her thumb on the scanner, and a white ring closed around the print and climbed to her eye line. "Identity verified. Elara Nathan. Gana: Drona. Security clearance: Initiation monitoring."

The door hissed open on a green pulse, and across the circular antechamber three other doors released pressure in the same instant, as if the building had taken one measured breath.

Four doctrines in four uniforms: Drona's gray-blue, Chanakya's green-gray, Kubera's muted gold, Ashoka's red-black. This cycle's Initiation belonged to Sector 1, so Observation Tier-1 sat in Drona's tower and the other three ganas had sent their monitors to it, guests with full consoles and borrowed quarters, the way Drona's own would travel to Tier-2 or Tier-3 when the cycle belonged to another sector.

All four were new graduates, none older than twenty-one. Trinity staffed the Initiation with its youngest for a reason it never wrote down: they hadn't yet learned to flinch.

Ravi came first, Chanakya's. His face held a calm that people trusted until they realized they had been studied, not seen. There was ink along the side of his middle finger, the stain of someone who still wrote with a pen and had been writing late. He nodded once at Elara. It was courtesy, and it was a warning.

Daren emerged with the unhurried confidence of someone who believed time belonged to him. Kubera insignia glinted at his collar, muted gold and mathematically understated. He was humming, four notes under his breath, the same four again and again, and his eyes moved across bodies, badges, and doors, pricing each before anyone spoke.

The last door opened and Rohan arrived without announcement.

Ashoka's representative didn't hurry. He crossed the floor broad-shouldered and expressionless, a scar along one side of his jaw, hands loose at his sides. The knuckles of the right hand were split and scabbing, three in a row. Elara looked at them a beat too long.

"Children's class," he said. "The nine-year-olds. They've started hitting back."

"You let them?"

"That's the lesson."

Four ganas, four claims to the right kind of control. They fought one another through budgets, appointments, and quietly arranged accidents, each certain the other three were too dangerous to hold the whole thing alone.

Inside, the chamber was laid out like a divided compass: four curved consoles facing a suspended central grid where Miner Town rendered itself in shifting light and pulse-lines. Each station carried one label in austere white text. *Event. Variance. Threshold. Correction.*

Ravi took Event, the Chanakya station, because Chanakya taught that power began by deciding which events were allowed to mean something. His display bloomed with intake feeds, incident streams, and narrative flags waiting to be classified.

Elara took Variance, Drona's, because Drona's first obligation was to follow knowledge wherever it led, especially into the flaws the system preferred not to name. Her job was to notice when the system's story and the data's behavior stopped agreeing.

Daren lowered himself into Threshold, Kubera's, where allocation became a question of what could be spent, withheld, or sacrificed without endangering the whole. He watched limits before he watched people.

Rohan stood a beat before Correction, then sat, and the Ashoka console woke under his hand with force ladders, containment protocols, escalation pathways. The force ladder was Ashoka's standardized scale, a rigid climb from passive atmospheric pressure to direct warden suppression.

The central grid flickered.

Sixteen names populated the Initiation queue, stripped of color or ceremony, like component numbers in a ledger. Sixteen children. Four monitors. Four ganas. On paper the arithmetic divided cleanly.

Thirteen names streamed toward the four consoles, each tethered to its gana with the mechanical precision Trinity preferred. Then the grid flickered again, and three names stayed on the primary display, unrouted. Beneath each, where the system should have shown a responsible gana, a fifth designation appeared.

UNASSIGNED.

UNASSIGNED.

UNASSIGNED.

A red bar came up under the three names and asked for an operator. Whatever metric these three children had violated, whatever gap in the framework they stood in, the machine wouldn't move without a human signature.

"Archive," Ravi said. "Precedent search. Designation: unassigned."

"No records returned," the console answered.

Daren was already paging through the printed manual beside his station. "The routing appendix has no fifth designation. It's got no procedure for the absence of one, either."

"Then the system's never done this before," Rohan said.

Their attention settled on the High Command call button. Ravi lifted his hand toward it, and Elara's hand closed around his wrist.

She looked at her own fingers on his sleeve the way she would have looked at a reading that had come in wrong. She hadn't decided to do it.

"Wait," she said, and kept hold. "It's our first day. We can't give them a reason to think we need supervision before we've even begun. Let's see if we can solve it ourselves."

Ravi looked at her hand until she withdrew it. "You're asking us to sit on an unclassified anomaly."

"I'm asking us to classify it first."

The others hesitated, then agreed. In Trinity the cost of appearing incapable was high, and none of them was ready to pay it.

Daren moved first, with the efficient certainty of someone for whom problems were problems and people were data to organize. He read the three names off the grid the way an auditor reads a shipment.

"Gage. Otto. Ben. No family designations worth indexing. Three rounding errors the system was too honest to hide."

He had already reduced them to entries that could be sorted, priced, and closed.

Elara pulled the three profiles. The screens filled: medical scans, behavioral histories, genetic markers, skill assessments. Thousands of measurements, each designed to fit a person into a category, and these three fit none of them. She started looking for the shape of the refusal.

"Keep the primary feeds while we sort the exceptions," she said to Rohan.

He held her gaze, nodded, and turned back to Correction. The morning debrief had named her operational lead, and Ashoka honored a chain of command it had agreed to.

Daren didn't look up. "Drona chairs the first anomaly of the cycle. Noted."

An objection filed as agreement. Elara let it stand. Her lead was real, and it would be billed; in Kubera's accounting every use of authority entered someone's ledger.

She brought up Gage's profile, and the data rendered itself like a description of someone's worth. She read aloud, steady and clinical.

"Male. Six feet five inches. Two hundred twenty pounds."

She read the numbers twice. A Miner Town ration built one kind of body: lean, durable, ground down by seventeen. It did not build six-five and two-twenty. Someone had spent years putting calories into this boy that the ledger had never issued.

"That mass doesn't close against his ration history," she said. "Someone's been feeding him off the books since childhood, and the system never flagged it."

"Then the anomaly has parents," Daren said. "File it."

She didn't file it. Somewhere between the numbers and the question, Gage had stopped being an entry and become a person someone had gone hungry for.

"Assign Gage to me."

No one objected. They saw a miner. Elara saw the outline of a family the ledger had missed, and she buried the thought under protocol as she claimed him.

She tagged Gage and brought up Ben. The relief in the data was immediate: normal height, normal weight, normal everything.

"Five feet eight. One hundred sixty pounds. Every value inside the reference band."

"Which is why he matters," Ravi said. "Exceptions tell you where a system breaks. The baseline tells you how it runs. Sixteen thousand bodies like his keep the shafts open."

"You want the unremarkable one," Daren said.

"I want the representative one." Ravi's hand closed over the assignment console. "I'll take Ben."

The gesture was possessive in a way that seemed to surprise him. In a room where understanding was currency, claiming what he understood felt like power.

Elara pulled the last profile. The data took its time loading.

"Height five feet nine. Weight one hundred seventy. Same build as Ben—" She stopped. "The scanner's showing an abnormally high energy signature."

"Define abnormal," Ravi said.

"Off the calibration curve. It isn't reading him like a body. It's reading him like a source."

Rohan was already on his feet, one hand raised from the Correction console, the split knuckles catching the light.

"He is mine to size up."
