# Chapter 9: Two Levels Down

*“Don’t draw me a forest, Gage.” Her voice hardened, and for a moment she sounded like Pappy. “Draw me a way out.”*

— Book 1, Chapter 17: Mirror Mirror in the Box

## Now: Two levels down, third door

The third door had a handle on the inside.

Camilla checked before she checked anything else. She put her good hand on it, pushed down, and the latch gave with a dry little cough of old grease. Somebody had oiled it, once, a long time ago, and then nobody had needed to.

Then she turned round and looked.

Benches, six of them, under grey dust sheets that had been tucked at the corners like beds. A sink with a brass tap gone green at the neck. A drill press in the corner under its own sheet, the shape of it showing through, a heron asleep on one leg. And the long wall facing the door was tools, hundreds, each hung on a hook inside its own outline, painted on the plaster in white so a hand reaching back in the dark would know where to put a thing down. Files in order of coarseness. Pliers in order of nose. Eleven screwdrivers, smallest on the left. A horseshoe magnet, red paint flaking off the bend.

Ben came in behind her and stopped. Gage had to duck the lintel.

"Somebody worked here," Ben said.

"Somebody worked here a lot." Camilla pulled the sheet off the nearest bench. The top was scarred maple, burned in one place in a perfect ring where a hot pot had sat, and worn hollow at the front edge where forearms had leaned for years. The vice at the end still had a scrap of brass in its jaws. Whoever it was had left in the middle of a job.

Gage was already at the far bench with the flat wooden box under his arm. He didn't say anything about it. Nobody asked.

She went along the wall. Every outline full, every tool home, until the fourth row from the top, where one outline held nothing. It was the length of her forearm, a long shaft with a hooked end and a ring at the other, like a key for a lock the size of a door. The white paint inside it was cleaner than the paint round it. Whatever had hung there had hung there a long time, and then gone, and the hook was still out, waiting.

She didn't know the tool. She knew most tools. She put the shape away in the place where she kept things she'd come back for.

Ben's hand went to his right trouser pocket, flattened there, and came out empty. He looked at the drill press.

---

The shelf was over the sink, high, where steam wouldn't reach.

She saw the spines from across the room and was standing under them before she'd decided to walk. Grey cloth boards, gone shiny at the corners. No titles, only a number stamped at the foot of each spine in small square figures. She reached up with the metal hand without thinking and it went, half a breath behind, and drew one out by the top of the spine the way you're told not to.

She knew the weight before she opened it.

The pages were stitched in bundles with green thread. The diagrams were the fine kind, every line the same width, a cutaway of a pump with the water drawn as arrows. In the outside corner of every page the number sat inside a little printed square. She'd learned to read off pages like this one. She'd learned the word *impeller* off one, on her stomach on the floor of the shack with a candle stub, while Pappy pretended to sleep. Pappy's Master Scripts had a number at the foot of every spine, in small square figures, and she'd always thought he'd inked them himself.

She smelled it. Dust and glue and, faint under that, something like the inside of a tin.

"Cami?" Ben said.

"It's a manual." She put it back in its gap, exactly where it had been, spine flush with the others. Her good hand stayed on it a while. "It's just a manual."

---

The engineer came after the lunch bell, if there was a lunch bell. She'd stopped being sure the bells meant anything.

He stood just inside the door with both pencil stubs behind his ears and his hands behind his back, and he kept them there. She'd asked for that. He seemed to have expected it.

"You may begin," he said.

She sat at the scarred bench with the arm laid on the maple in front of her, palm up. Last night she'd gone over every finger of it in the dark with her right thumb and found the two places on the inside of the forearm where the grain of the metal changed direction, one below the wrist and one below the elbow. Two dimples, shallow as fingerprints. She pressed both at once with the thumb and the heel of her good hand and turned, the way you open a stuck jar.

Nothing. She pressed harder and turned the other way.

The cover lifted. It came off in one long half-shell, light as a peapod, and she set it on the bench with its hollow side up.

She'd been ready for wires, the way you're ready for blood when you open a bandage, and the arm hadn't bothered with any. Down the middle ran a core, dull grey, the bone of the thing, and small dense pistons where the tendons ought to be, and lying over all of it, along the inside where the cover had been and spread across the bone like frost on a pane, a lattice finer than hair. Finer than the hair on her arm. She had to tilt her head to see it at all. It caught the bench lamp in one direction and vanished in the other.

It lay where her nerves would have run. It lay the way her nerves would have run, branching where they'd branch.

She thought about closing her fist.

Something went through the lattice, a shift from grey to a fraction less grey, wrist to elbow, too fast to be a ripple and too slow to miss. Then the fingers closed. Index, middle, ring, little. Less than half a breath now. She counted it. Less.

"It's listening up," she said. "All the way."

"Yes."

"Where's it go?"

"To the shoulder. You will need a mirror."

There was a mirror on the wall, a dentist's mirror on a long handle, hanging in its outline between two awls. Ben took it down and gave it to her and went back to the doorway, out of the engineer's way, and stood with his hand in his pocket.

She followed the lattice with the little mirror up past the elbow joint, where it ran in under the upper casing, to the darker ring where the arm met the cup. There it thickened. It gathered into a band the width of her smallest finger and went in, toward her, under the seal.

On the inner lip of the cup, upside down in the mirror, was a stamp.

It was a circle with marks inside it. It had been worn almost smooth, the way a coin goes smooth in a pocket, the way the step into the shack had a dip in it from forty years of feet. Some of the marks might have been letters. Some might have been a picture. She turned the mirror and turned her head and turned the mirror again and couldn't make any of them be anything.

"What's it say?"

"I cannot read it." The engineer hadn't moved. "No one here can. I have asked."

"Who made it?"

He took one pencil stub from behind his ear and turned it in his fingers and put it back.

"The listening core is older than this ring's charter," he said. "We copied it. We did not invent it."

"Copied from what?"

"I have only ever seen the copies."

She looked at the lattice a while longer. She wanted to touch it so badly her good hand had gone up off the bench on its own, and she put it flat again. You don't touch a thing you can't see the whole of. Pappy had said that about the old pump, and then touched it, and lost the end of a fingernail.

She lifted the cover. She lined the half-shell up by the seam, wrist end first, and pressed both dimples, and turned. It went home with a sound like a drawer shutting in another room.

She closed the hand five times. Five times it followed, in order.

The engineer looked at the arm, and then at her, and took his hands out from behind his back. He didn't come closer. He only let them hang.

"Mine take longer," he said.

---

Gage had built it again.

He'd built it on the far bench, with his back to the room, and when she came and stood beside him he didn't cover it. Clay basin. Splinter wall. The one splinter split a hair, with his thumbnail. He'd built it from memory and it was the same to the millimetre, and she knew because she'd moved two of the pieces this morning and he'd put them where she'd moved them.

"He said take it apart," she said.

"I do." He pressed a matchstick into the clay. "Every time."

"They've got the trigger. You don't have to keep building it."

"Trigger's theirs." His finger came down on the split splinter and rested there. "Wall's not."

Nineteen nights. She'd been counting since the briefing room; she couldn't stop. Nineteen nights, and somebody at the junction to say when, and somebody in the tunnel the water wanted first. His finger stayed on the wall. She looked at the finger, and he felt her look, and he lifted it off and put the hand flat on the bench beside the model, open, the way he'd been keeping it all day.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Nineteen," he said.

"I can count."

He swept the splinters into the box with the side of his hand and pressed the clay flat. Then he took out a matchstick, and laid it down, and started again.

---

She found Flitzberg's rooms by the music.

The door was open. The dolls began just inside it and went up to the ceiling: painted wooden figures in graduated sizes, shelf on shelf, hundreds. She'd seen them once before, half-dead, and taken them for a museum. Rested and fed and with two arms, she saw they'd been dusted. All of them. Somebody did that.

He was at his table with his back to her, drawing on clean white paper. A pencil, and a rubber worn to a nub, and the music coming from somewhere she couldn't find, strings going round and round one tune.

"Come in, Camilla."

He didn't turn round. She came in and stood where she could see his jaw in the dark of the window glass. His own face, the smooth one with the full cheeks. She'd check again before she left.

"Who else can switch my arm off?"

He set the pencil down and turned in his chair.

"Three people," he said. "The medic who fitted it. The man who built it. And I." He let that stand. "No one above. No house. No Grandmaster has been told that it exists, and none will be."

"The medic said she hadn't opened every door here."

"She has not. I have." He folded his hands on his knee. "I cannot give you proof you would accept, and I would think less of you if you accepted any I could give. So you will go on doing what you did this afternoon. And when you have taken it apart to the last wire, you will know whether I lied."

She thought about the lattice, finer than hair, going in under the seal.

"There aren't any wires."

"No," he said. "Then you will have to work harder than I would have liked."

She looked past him at the shelves. On the one nearest his elbow, at the height of her eyes, stood a doll in a miner's jacket with a lamp painted on its forehead, grey moustache, the paint on the cheeks rubbed pale. He saw her looking. He reached over and took it down, and twisted it at the waist, and lifted off the top half.

Inside, a smaller one. The same jacket. The moustache gone, the face younger. He opened that one too. Smaller still: a boy, no lamp. And inside the boy, one no bigger than her thumbnail, painted plain, no face on it at all.

He set them out in a row on the drawing.

"You wore Pappy's face," she said. "And Gerald's."

"Yes."

"Why them?"

He looked at the four small figures, and his manufactured calm did not change at all, and that was the worst of it.

"I keep the faces of people this office owes," he said. "It is a short collection, and it grows. I put one on when I need to be reminded what the debt looks like from the front." He touched the smallest doll with the end of his finger and didn't move it. "Your grandfather was owed a great deal. I wore him to meet you because I thought you should be met by someone we had failed. I see now that was a cruelty. I did not see it then."

The music went round its tune again.

She found the metal hand had closed at her side. She hadn't sent it. She let it stay closed.

"Who's the little one?"

"No one, yet." He began putting them back, smallest first, each half twisting home onto its other half. "That is why it has no face."

She waited until the miner was whole again and back on the shelf, lamp facing out. Then she looked at his jaw, close, the way she'd look at a weld.

"Keep wearing that one," she said. "It's the only one I've checked."

---

Ben was in a room she hadn't been in, on the level between.

The medic had brought him; she was standing at the back of it with her hands folded and her pink-scrubbed wrists, and she inclined her head to Camilla and didn't speak. One wall was screens, small, a grid of them, grey pictures with no sound. A man at a desk underneath them wore a headset and didn't look round. The pictures moved. The pump. A shaft head. The ration queue, from above. Miner Town, all of it, without its noise.

Ben was standing in front of one screen in the bottom row. The gates.

There was a girl at the gates. Thin, in a brown coat with the sleeves turned back, a scarf over her hair against the grit. She stood a little to the left of the tunnel mouth, where the wall gave some shade, and she wasn't doing anything. She was standing. The guards had their backs to her. Her feet were set on a pale patch in the dirt, a patch the size of two feet, a little worn.

Gretchen.

The man at the desk said, to no one, "She comes at the first bell. She goes at the last."

Ben didn't move. His right hand went to his pocket and pressed flat over nothing, and stayed. His other hand came up off his side toward the screen and stopped halfway and hung there in the air between him and her, open, and then it went down again slowly, and he put it in his other pocket too.

He couldn't go up to her. He couldn't send so much as a word; nobody who came down here went back. Ben had known that since the briefing room, and nodded once, and asked nothing else.

Camilla looked at the screen next to it, and the next. The basins. The queue. The washing lines between the shacks. She looked at every grey woman with her arms in water and every grey woman in the line, and the shape of the shoulders, and the way one of them held her head, and it wasn't Rita, and the next one wasn't Rita either. She went along the whole bottom row and up the next.

Rita wasn't at the gates. Rita wasn't at the basins.

She didn't know if that was better. She went back to the start of the row and looked again.

On the screen, Gretchen shifted her weight from one foot to the other, and set it back on the worn patch.

---

The workshop was empty when they got back. Gage's bench was swept, the box shut, and the clay pressed flat. He'd gone up to sit with Otto; it was his hour. Somewhere above them the tank was going pale and then deeper and then pale, and the flat line was being flat.

Ben sat on a stool by the door. His hand was in his pocket.

Camilla took the horseshoe magnet off its outline. From a tin on the second bench she took a sewing needle, long, with a burr at the eye. A cork from a bottle stopper in a drawer full of them. A shallow tin dish off the drying rack, which she filled at the green tap.

She held the needle in her good fingers and stroked it down one leg of the magnet, eye to point, and lifted it clear at the end, and brought it back through the air and stroked again. Same leg. Same direction. Always lift it away; never drag it back, or you undo it. Pappy's voice, or a page with a number in a little square. She couldn't have said which. Thirty. Forty. Fifty.

She cut a thin slice off the cork with a knife from the wall, laid the needle across it, and set the cork on the water.

It turned. It went round almost a full circle, slow, and swung back past where it wanted to be, and back again, the cork nudging the edge of the dish.

She pushed the dish along the bench until it was in front of Ben.

He took his hand out of his pocket. He didn't touch the dish. He leaned over it, close, and the needle trembled when he breathed.

"His did that," Ben said.

The needle swung, and swung less, and came to rest pointing at the wall, at nothing, at the one direction it knew.
