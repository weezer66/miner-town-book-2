# Chapter 24: First Contact

*Ben pulled out a small brass compass. His father had given it to him years ago — the only thing he'd carried from home.*

— Book 1, Chapter 25: Don't Do It

## Now and then: First contact

The note came under the workshop door, folded once, in Flitzberg's backward-leaning hand. *All three of you, please. My rooms. When Otto can spare you.*

Otto could spare them. He said so himself.

He was propped on the bed beside the empty tank with three pillows behind him and a cup of something thin and yellow held in both hands, and the cup was the whole of his morning's work. He had got it as far as his chest. The grey-haired medic stood at his elbow with her hands folded, not helping, which Camilla had learned was the hardest help she gave.

"Go," Otto said. The scrape was still in his voice. He looked at Gage, who hadn't moved off the doorframe. "I'm not going anywhere. I checked."

Gage went. At the first landing he stopped and looked back up the stairs, and then he went on.

---

The music was playing when they reached the door, the same strings going round the same tune, and under them the slow thing like a heart. The dolls stood on their shelves to the ceiling, every face turned a little to the left. Someone had moved the miner with the painted lamp down a shelf, to the height of a sitting man's eyes.

Flitzberg had set out three chairs. He didn't sit.

"I made you a promise," he said, "so I will tell you what has happened before I tell you what I want. Two of the four Grandmasters have asked the table to sit out of season."

"The once-a-year," Gage said. "The room nobody's told."

"Yes. It has not been asked in living memory. This week it has been asked twice." He turned his pencil a quarter turn on the paper so it lay square to the edge. "One of them has brought a matter to it. Someone in Ashoka has laid the four houses' books side by side, and found the same small leak in all four."

Camilla heard it in his voice from the first morning, in the room with no handle on the inside of the door. *If one did, it would find the same small leak in all four, and no one to blame for it.*

The metal hand closed on the arm of her chair a beat after the rest of her did.

"It's you," she said. "The leak's this office."

"Yes."

Ben was looking at the floor between his boots. "Somebody saw the bend."

"Somebody saw the light bend." Flitzberg gave him the look he kept for the dolls. "With a pencil. On paper. Six years of copying, and no one ever asked her to lay it side by side, and then someone did."

"What happens when they ask you?" Gage said.

"I answer them. That was always the arrangement. No one who made it expected the question to come up from a woman on her knees."

He went to the shelf behind his table, below the dolls, and took down a cardboard folder tied with string. The string had been knotted and unknotted so many times it had gone to fur at the bow. He sat, at last, and untied it.

"These are the examiners' notes from the night you came through the four doors," he said. "In the hand of an examiner called Sef. She has annotated candidates for thirty years. In thirty years I have kept perhaps a dozen of her lines." He turned a page. The paper was grey, the ink grey on it, the letters small and upright and leaning on nothing. "For CONTROL, she wrote: *The strong one spent his strength making the others stronger.*"

Gage's hands were open on his knees. He looked at them.

"For PLAN." Flitzberg didn't look up from the page. "*Fear present in all four. Reasoning continuous in all four. The girl thinks in exits, the gentle one thinks in patterns, and they listen to each other without ranking.*"

The tips of Ben's ears had gone red. Camilla watched them do it. He didn't look at her, so she didn't look at him either, and between them the chair arms creaked.

"And what did you write?" she said.

"I wrote nothing. I said it aloud, to Sef, at the second console." He closed the folder. "*Unlikely heroes. Possible.*"

"That's not much."

"From me it is a great deal." He laid his hand flat on the cover. "I will answer the copyist before the table sits. I will not send her a letter. I will send her someone who reads patterns, to sit on her floor and work beside her, and he will not tell her what he is."

He looked at Ben.

The room went on playing its tune.

"You want him to steer her off us," Camilla said.

"No. She isn't hunting this office. She is hunting a hand that moves through all four houses, the same hand that put a number on your brother. She stopped at us because we were lying across her road." His fingers stayed flat on the folder. "I would like her to look past us to what she came for. I would like someone to help her do it. And I would like to know what this office looks like from her floor. I have only ever seen it from mine."

"No," Gage said.

It was the only word for a while. His hands stayed open on his knees, palms up, and he didn't close them, and Camilla saw what that cost him in the cords of his wrists.

"He's the count," Gage said. "Five nights."

"I know how many nights." Ben's right hand had gone to his hip and pressed flat over the pocket that wasn't there. It stayed. "I've never been up. None of us has, past the gates. They used to say the air up there's so clean it hurts." He swallowed. "I want to go. I want to see what she's got on her floor. If somebody up there's reading the same thing I read off his wall, I want to read it next to her." The hand pressed harder. "And I'm scared I'll open my mouth and the wrong thing'll come out. Both. I want both."

Camilla thought of the bench two levels down. Last night the listener had picked up Gage's knuckle on a slab of granite through two hands' depth of stone, clean, and an hour later it had heard the lathe two rooms away and called that a knock as well. Eyes for the wall that couldn't yet tell a blow from a neighbor. She'd meant to give it three of the five nights.

"If the count's wrong," Gage said, "somebody's standing on the turbine floor."

"Then I'll come back and get it right."

They both looked at her. They always did. She had told herself once that she'd get used to it.

"He goes," Camilla said. "Under the ring's cover, and back by the third night for the last run. If you're not on that stool when the glass turns over, Ben, I call it without you." Her voice didn't move. "I'll hate it. I'll still call it."

Gage looked at her for a long time. Then he reached across with one open hand and put it on Ben's shoulder and left it there, and Ben let it sit.

"Three things," Flitzberg said. "You will not name this ring, to anyone, for any reason. You will not lie to them, unless a lie is the only thing standing between you and harm. Then you will lie, and you will come home and tell me you did." He set the folder back on its shelf, under the miner with the painted lamp. "And you will come home."

"That's three," Ben said.

"The third is the one I care about."

---

The ring let him out at the change of first bell, through the back of a lift in Drona's lower stacks, among forty runners in gray-blue who all seemed to know where they were going.

The light hit him first. It came from everywhere, off white stone and glass and the polished brass of rails, and his eyes ran with it, and he walked with his head down like a man in a dust storm. Then the height. The stacks went up past where he could tip his head, and between them, through gaps, there was sky with nothing in it, no smoke, no grit, a blue so plain it looked painted. He breathed in and coughed. His lungs had been waiting for the dust and didn't know what to do without it.

On his left wrist the terminal the ring had given him sat warm against the bone. When a gate tasted it, a line came up and was underlined, and the gate let him by. JUNIOR RUNNER, LOWER STACKS. He was no one in particular. Everyone he passed wore one, and everyone glanced at their own wrist the way Miner Town glanced at the sky before a shift.

Gretchen would be at the gates by now. First bell. She'd be standing on her worn patch in the shade to the right of the tunnel mouth, where she'd moved her feet, looking at a door he'd gone in through two months ago. She was somewhere under all this light. He didn't know how far down. He kept walking, and the wonder came up in him anyway, warm and shameful, and he let it, because she'd have wanted one of them to see it.

His hand went to his hip. Cloth. He took it away.

The runners' car took him across to Ashoka on a cable over nothing. He didn't look down. He counted the pylons instead, nine, and the men in red-black at the far platform, two, and the seconds the second one took to look at his wrist, four.

Three flights down into old stone, a door had DEAD ROOM painted on it in plain white. A man was standing outside it.

He was older than Gage and not as tall, with a scar along his jaw and knuckles on his right hand that had been split and glued so often the glue had become part of the hand. He looked at Ben's face, then at his wrist, then at his boots, which were the ring's boots and good ones, and back at his face.

Ben looked back. He knew what a commander was. He'd watched Camilla become one in a room that was all four rooms. He saw the man take his measure and not finish it, and he saw him decide he wasn't going to be allowed to finish it, and he saw him accept that.

The man saw him seeing it. One corner of his mouth moved.

"Rohan," the man said. "Terminal in the locker. Shoes off. Walk on the gaps."

---

She was on the floor, among four long columns of paper that ran from the wall nearly to Ben's socks, on her knees with a pencil behind each ear. One heel had been darned with a lot of green thread. She didn't stand.

"Nayan," Rohan said. "The runner."

"Runner." She didn't look up. "Read me Chanakya, third year, autumn."

He found the column, the green-gray house, second from the shaft. He found the third year by counting the sheets back from the door, and autumn by the dates, and knelt on the left of it because the numbers were on the right, and read. Filter media. Survey fees. Ink. A shipment of lamp glass that had come in short and been written off by a clerk with a number. He read it the way he'd read a coil, down the wire, one turn at a time, and Nayan's pencil moved on her pad while he did, keeping up.

Then his eyes went sideways.

In the left margin of every column, at long intervals, there were small squares drawn in pencil. Unnumbered. He read across from one square to the next one over, and the next, Kubera to Chanakya to Ashoka to Drona, all in the same week. Bearings, filters, dressings, paper. He went down forty days and read across again. Then again.

The four lines weren't on the same day. They were in the same week, a day apart, one house after the other, and the house that went first was a different house every time. Kubera first, then forty days on, Chanakya first, then Ashoka, then Drona, then Kubera again. Round and round. Six years of it, and no house ever first twice running.

A circle with no head and no foot. A voice had said it on a wall in a room with no handle on the inside of the door. *It would belong to no house, so that no house could rot it.*

Ben's mouth closed.

He knelt there with his finger on a square in Ashoka's column, and the stone was cold through his trousers, and he could feel his own pulse in the finger.

Nayan's pencil had stopped.

"You stopped," she said.

He could feel the whole room on him, though the commander hadn't moved and the corner by the locker was only a corner. He thought of Flitzberg's three things, and of what a lie would cost and what it would buy, and found it bought nothing here. Nobody in this room was going to hurt him.

"I saw something," he said. "I don't know how it works."

That was true. He didn't know how a line written into four foundations a century ago could still be buying lamp oil. He didn't know who drew the order, or what wire it ran down.

Nayan lifted her head. Her eyes were very dark and not unkind, and they went over his face line by line, a column she had not copied yet.

"That isn't the same as not knowing what it is," she said.

"No."

She held his eyes a while longer. Then she tore a strip off the bottom of her pad, wrote the date and the hour on it, and under that, in her small level hand, *runner stopped, Ashoka, yr 6*, and laid it on the floor beside his finger, square to the column.

"I copy what I read," she said, and went back to her pad.

---

There was a man in the corner by the locker.

Ben had come in and counted the room, which was what he did with rooms, and he'd got to two and then gone back and made it three. Medium height, medium build, sitting on the floor with his back to the stone and his hands on his knees. A face like a door with nothing painted on it.

Ben looked at it properly. A small white nick through the left eyebrow, old. A habit of breathing through the nose in fours, in and in and out and out, like a man who'd once been taught to and then forgotten he'd been taught. A collar that had been turned so the frayed side was in.

"Hello," Ben said to him.

The man's hands stopped on his knees.

His eyes came up and found Ben's and stayed there. Something went across his face and was gone, a man walking into a room he'd lived in for years and finding the lamp already lit. Across the columns, Rohan had gone still.

"Kaan," Rohan said, after a while. "The ear."

Kaan took a pencil stub from his pocket and a slip of paper from the other one. He wrote on it in block letters, pressing hard, and leaned forward and laid it on the stone beside Nayan's knee.

*3/1186-F.*

"He read it off a retrieval slip in the analyst's coat," Rohan said, "at the appointment. The analyst holds an old file. That's its number."

Nayan looked at the number. Then she got up, stepped over two columns on the gaps, and took a thin sheaf from under a whetstone near the wall.

"Custody ledgers," she said. "The records archive keeps them public. I copy them on the third day of the week, because I copy everything." She knelt and laid one sheet beside Kaan's slip. "Yesterday's."

Her copy of the ledger page had a pen drawn at its edge on a short string, because she copied what was there. Most of the lines were dull: a deed lent to a surveyor, a map to a court. One near the bottom read, in her hand copying someone else's: *One folio, source copy. Released for one night. Author's custody.* A signature beneath it, not neat. *Ravi.*

Above it, the number. *3/1186-F.*

The return column beside it was blank.

Two papers, side by side on the stone, one in block capitals pressed hard enough to dent, one in a copyist's level hand. One number. Both of them things anyone could have picked up.

Ben was looking at the dates.

The custody line had been signed at first bell. He looked at the strip of paper Kaan had laid down. Kaan had written a date under the number, small, and the date was the same.

"When did you set the appointment?" Ben asked him.

Kaan held up one finger, and then pointed at the date he'd written.

"Same morning," Ben said. "He went and got it the morning you asked him to bring it."

Nobody said anything. The lantern ticked in its glass. On the wall, a brass pin held the corner of the fourth panel, a little crooked, and the sheets either side of it stood a finger's width clear.

Nayan drew a box round the number on her copy. She didn't write anything inside it. Nobody in the room knew what the folio said, or what the number was for, only that a man had signed for it in ink in a building no instrument could see into, and kept it, and taken it to a meeting.

Rohan was looking at the two papers. His thumb moved once along the split knuckle.

"That's the other road," he said.

Ben waited. Rohan didn't say the other road from what, and from the set of his mouth he wasn't going to, to anyone, and Ben found he could live with it.

---

It was dark when Rohan took him up. At a stair head on one of Ashoka's high floors there was a window, tall and narrow and very clean, and Rohan said "Wait," and went to find the runners' warden, and left him alone with it.

Ben looked down.

The cloud was under him. He hadn't known it would be under him. It lay over everything in a long grey floor, and the moon was on it, and while he watched a gap opened in it, slow, a long way down, and he saw the ground.

A lake, a coin of it, black and bright. The mountain's dark side. Along its shoulder a ledge of rock lifted pale out of the black, the North-East Shelf, with a smudge on top of it that was the Junkyard, all its rust gone to nothing at this height. He'd climbed through that. He'd slept in it. Somewhere to the left of the lake, too small to see, was a tunnel mouth with a worn patch of dirt in front of it the size of two feet.

And under all of it, under the shelf and the lake and the gates, in the rock where no lamp from up here would ever reach, there was a ring with no head and no foot, and Otto in a bed with a cup on his chest, and Gage awake on a stool, and Camilla counting nights on her fingers.

His hand went to his hip and pressed flat. Cloth.

He kept it there. The gap in the cloud closed again, slower than it had opened, and he went on looking at the place where it had been.
