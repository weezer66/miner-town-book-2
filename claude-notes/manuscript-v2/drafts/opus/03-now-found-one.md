# Chapter 3: Found One

*He was a man carrying a piece of corporate property welded to his skeleton that he couldn’t use and they couldn’t be bothered to collect.*

— Book 1, Chapter 14: Phantom Leg

## Now: The treatment room, beneath the city

The tape was the first thing she hated.

It sat across the back of her right hand in a clean white cross, holding the line flat, put on by someone who meant it to stay. Camilla had worked the near corner up with her thumbnail in the dark and pressed it back down, because the line went somewhere under it and she didn't know yet where the somewhere ended. The bag above her had a small brass clip at the bottom shaped like a beetle, the only thing in the room anyone had bothered to make pretty.

She hadn't slept. She'd gone under a few times, the way a float goes under, and come up at the same place: grey ceiling, a hum in the wall too even to be a pump, and the left hand that wasn't there curled into a fist so tight she could feel the nails. She let it stay closed. Opening it took a kind of attention she was saving.

Five things in the room: bed, stand, chair, a cabinet with no handles, a long low window of dark glass. They kept coming out five, the most reliable thing that had happened to her in three days.

The chair had the oldest medic in it, hands folded, sleeves pushed up, watching the panel by the bed the way Pappy used to watch a pressure gauge.

"You're still here," Camilla said.

"I said you would be angry about the tape. I wished to see whether I was right."

"You were."

"Good. Anger is a working sign." She stood and checked the shoulder without being asked: two fingers at the edge of the seal, a light press, like testing bread. The seal had set hard and glassy in the night, the blue gone darker where it was thickest. "It holds. You lost about a third of your blood. It is coming back."

"I was standing," she said.

"Yes. I am told."

---

The medic went out and came back with a man and a case.

He was thin, with a pencil behind each ear, both sharpened down to stubs. He set the case on the end of the bed and stood back from it.

"He builds them," the medic said. "He will show you. You may touch anything. You may say no to all of it."

He opened the case.

It lay in a cut-out bed of grey foam, an arm, shoulder to fingertips. Dull grey metal, unpolished, with a grain to it like a file. Jointed at the shoulder, the elbow, the wrist, and the joints didn't pretend; they were honest about where they bent, the way a crane is. The fingers were long, longer than hers, lying a little curled with nobody telling them anything.

Camilla read it front to back. Fine seams on the fingers. A darker ring at the top, where it would meet her. No cables, no battery door, no screws, which bothered her most. A thing with no screws was a thing you weren't meant to open.

"It fits to the shoulder," the engineer said. "A cup sits against the seal. It does not cut. It listens to the shoulder, to what is left of the nerves there, the signals your body still sends to the arm you had."

"The arm I had keeps making a fist."

He took that as data. "Then it will make a fist. For a few days it will be slow, half a breath behind you. Every body sends a little differently. It will learn your timing."

She was already somewhere else.

Gerald at his broken table, the leg off and leaning against the wall. Titanium, threaded into the bone. A Thursday morning, a coffee shop, a leg that stopped halfway through a step because a license had lapsed overnight and nobody had told the man wearing it. No alert. The system had stopped recognizing him, and the leg belonged to the system, so the leg stopped recognizing him too. He'd dragged it through the city on his elbows.

"Who owns it?" she said.

The engineer looked at the medic.

"I'm asking him. He made it."

"It is made here, from what this ring has. It is not leased. Nothing pays for it to keep working. No one will come asking for it back."

"That's not what I mean." Her right hand had found the blanket edge and was folding it into a hard crease. "Can you switch it off?"

The pencils went still.

It was the medic who answered. She came and stood where Camilla could see her face, which Camilla noticed, and filed.

"Every machine in this ring can be stopped. This one too. This office told the four of you it would not lie to you, and that holds from the top of it down to me."

"Stopped from where?"

"That I cannot give you."

"Can't, or won't?"

The medic gave the question the weight of a true answer, more than most people gave Camilla's questions. "Is not mine to give. There is someone you may ask. You will see him again."

The man with the dolls, then. Camilla folded the crease once more and let it go.

A machine that would read her nerves, made by people who'd fed her to eels, with a switch somewhere she couldn't see. Gerald's leg had a switch too, and nobody had told him until the morning they used it. Somebody had just told her.

She thought about Ben's hands shaking on a spray can. Gage carrying Otto out with nobody to take the other side. Every panel she'd opened this week holding the housing with her knee.

"Yes," she said. "Do it."

---

They raised the bed. The medic cut the sleeve away with scissors that went through the cloth without a sound.

The cup was cold. It settled over the seal, and for three breaths it was only weight, heavier than she'd guessed, dragging her down on that side. Then it warmed. Then it pressed, all over at once, a fine even pressure like a hand made of sand, and under it came a sting that ran in beneath the seal and spread along her collarbone and up her neck in branches. She knew what that was. It was finding the wires.

She set her teeth. She'd tied a tourniquet with them. She could hold still for this.

"Breathe," the medic said.

"I am."

"Breathe more."

The sting went and left a buzz behind, a hum at the shoulder with no tune, the sound a transformer makes when it's working well. The engineer was watching a flat slate, and the pencil stubs had come out from behind his ears into his other hand without his noticing.

"Open the hand," he said.

The hand that wasn't there had been closed all night. She did something she had no name for, at the place in her head where she'd always kept her left hand, and told it to open.

Nothing.

Then the grey fingers on the blanket, at the end of the grey arm that was suddenly the end of *her*, opened. All together. Half a breath late, the way a voice comes back off the far wall of a shaft.

Her chest locked. She could see the fingers, and under the buzz she could almost feel them, and the almost was worse than nothing: a hand at the far end of a long pipe, answering a question she'd already stopped asking. Her skin went cold from scalp to heel, a thing too strong lying against her with its own ideas, and her right hand went to the metal wrist to hold it down.

The metal wrist let her.

She looked at it. Then she closed the fingers again, slowly, and watched them follow, index, middle, ring, little, each a fraction behind the order she sent. Every finger guessed where she was going and went there, and every guess was a little better than the one before.

"It's listening," she said. "With what? There's no wire in this. Where does it *decide*?"

The engineer looked up from the slate, and his face did something at last. He looked at the medic.

"Tomorrow," the medic said. "Today you will lie still and think about the question, and be less trouble." She lowered the bed a notch. "I have patients who do as they are told. I have never once enjoyed them."

Camilla lay back. The arm lay beside her on the blanket, too long, the color of a pipe. When she thought about her left hand, it moved. She thought about it a great many times before morning.

---

When the lights came up behind the dark window, before anyone else was awake, she asked to walk.

The medic let her, an arm under her good elbow, the bag rolling beside them on a wheel that ticked. Eleven steps from the bed to the observation room door. She counted them. The eleventh nearly put her on the floor.

The tank stood taller than Gage. A clear cylinder on a dark base, full of something thicker than water, faintly blue, the blue of the sealant can. Otto hung inside it with his eyes closed, a clear mask over his nose and mouth and a line running up from it into the lid. His arms floated a little away from his sides. His hair lifted and settled. He wasn't standing and wasn't lying down. The gel held him the way water holds a swimmer who's stopped swimming.

She looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at everything that wasn't him, because that she could do something with. Heat off the base. Two pipes, one wrapped in white, one bare. A panel with four colored lines, three moving and one flat.

"Tell me how it works."

The medic had expected that. The answer came fast.

"The gel is cycled. Warm, then cool, then warm again; the wrapped pipe carries heat in. While it is warm we tell his muscles to work. Small signals, through the gel. Nothing he would feel as pain. His body believes it is hauling. Then we cool it, and his body believes it is resting."

"How long's a cycle?"

"An hour, a little more. Many short days in place of one." She watched the blue go a shade paler near the base. "His heart is strong. His breath is steady. The instruments find nothing wrong with him anywhere. Whatever is missing is not in the body. So we keep the body doing what a body does, and we make it loud. A working body is a loud thing. If any part of him is near enough to hear it, we want it to hear something worth coming home to."

The paler blue reached his hands. Otto's fingers tightened by a hair, all ten at once, told to haul by a machine.

Camilla's metal fingers, at her side, closed half a breath behind. She hadn't sent them anywhere. They opened again, slowly, and she put her good hand over the wrist and kept it there.

"Has it done anything? On the panel."

"His lines have been flat since the trial room." The medic touched the panel's edge. "Except once. Last night, in the hour your brother and Ben sat with him. One line moved. I do not know what it means. I would be lying if I said I did."

"Which line?"

The medic pointed. The flat one.

Camilla looked at it until her eyes watered. It stayed flat. She didn't need it to move. She needed to know which one to watch.

"When they wake up," she said, "bring them here. Before me."

"They will want to see you first."

"I know what they want." She leaned on the medic's arm to turn, and the eleven steps back looked like more. "If they see me first they'll stand round my bed all morning counting what I haven't got. Let him go first. He's been last all week."

"Your words, or mine?"

"Mine. Tell them they're mine."

---

She was in the chair by the window when they came.

The dark glass worked one way; she'd checked. Through it she watched the observation room door open, the medic step aside, Ben come in first, and stop.

He went straight to the tank and put both hands flat on the glass, fingers spread, a man holding a door shut. His mouth was moving. Gage came no further than two steps past the door. He stood with his feet planted and asked the medic something, and she answered at length, and Camilla watched her brother listen: the muscle in his jaw, the scar-lines on his brow, the old map, pulling tight and loose and tight.

Ben put his forehead to the glass beside Otto's hanging hand. Gage let him.

She got up.

It took two tries. The arm pulled her left and the room swung, and she caught the chair back until the floor agreed to stay put. She could be in the bed when they came in, blanket to her chin, and they'd talk low, the way people had talked around Pappy at the end.

She let go of the chair. She put her feet where the floor was firmest. When she heard the observation room door, she was ready.

Gage saw her first. She watched it hit him: she was standing, and that got to his face before anything else had time to. Then his eyes went to her left side and stopped, and she let him look, because there was no point making him do it later.

He crossed the room before anyone spoke and put his arms around her on the side that was still hers, so gently she nearly laughed; Gage had never been gentle with anything in his life except a pencil. Her good hand came up at once and took a fistful of the back of his shirt.

The metal hand came up too.

She felt it go, half a breath behind the rest of her. It rose off her side and paused at his back, high, where the shoulder blades were. She didn't tell it what to do next; she was too busy holding on with the other one. And the metal hand, with nobody telling it anything, settled flat between his shoulder blades with no more weight than a palm laid on a sleeping dog.

She could feel how much it could have done. All that strength resting against her brother's spine, and it had decided, a half-second after she had, to be gentle.

"Lost an arm," she said into his shoulder. "Found one."

He didn't let go. Over his shoulder she could see Ben in the doorway, grinning and wiping his face with his sleeve, not coming closer.

"You too," she said. "Come here."

He came. His chin knocked her temple, and he said, "Sorry, sorry," and didn't move.

Gage stepped back first. His eyes went to the arm and stayed there.

"Does it hurt?"

"No." She let Ben go. She lifted the arm and turned it, watching the elbow turn a fraction after her eyes did. "It's late. Half a breath behind me, every time. They say it listens to the shoulder, what's left of the nerves there, and it'll learn my timing in a few days. Right now it's guessing." She flexed the fingers one after another, and they followed in order, each a little behind. "It guesses well."

"They put that on you overnight."

"They offered. I said yes." She gave him the look from across the dinner table, the one for when he was about to be slow about something. "What would you have done?"

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out of it.

She held the metal hand out to him, palm up. He'd done this once, back home, a copper tag on his shaking palm: *You read the system better than I do.* She'd turned it over and read him the whole labor force off the back of it. Her turn.

He took the hand and pressed the palm with his thumb, and she watched him feel it give, a little, not like flesh and not like iron. Gage, testing a beam.

"It's stronger than me," he said.

"Much."

She closed the fingers. Half a breath late, and then all at once. His hand went in and didn't come out. His whole arm went hard, his shoulder jerked up, and he made a short, high, undignified noise she would keep for the rest of her life.

Ben laughed out loud. It cracked out of him, a sound she hadn't heard from him since the mine, and he clapped a hand over his own mouth, a boy caught stealing.

"Cami."

"Half a breath late," she said, not letting go, "and still faster than you." She opened the fingers and winked at him. "It's learning what I want to do. Give it a week and it'll learn what you need to be told, too. Which is usually the same thing twice."

He shook his hand out and turned it over, back and palm and fingers. No mark anywhere. He kept looking anyway.

"And it's learning you," he said.

"Yes."

She laid her good hand over the metal wrist and held it there, the way she'd held every machine she ever opened, Pappy's old radio, the water meter, the coil heater Ben swore was dead: a hand on the casing, still, feeling for what it did when it thought nobody was touching it. The arm hummed under her palm. Faint. Even. Waiting.

"I'll learn it first," she said. "I'm faster."

She watched him believe her.

It went into him slowly, the way water goes into dry ground. The map on his brow went smooth, all the old lines lying flat, and his eyes came off the arm and onto her and stayed, very steady, from a long way back. She'd seen that face at Pappy's bed. The face of someone at the top of a shaft who has just been told how deep it goes, and is working out whether the rope is long enough.

He didn't say anything. She'd been reading that face since before she could read.

---

The medic left breakfast on a cart in the doorway and didn't come in.

They ate on her bed, Ben at the foot with his knees up, Gage on the edge so it tipped, and she let the tilt pull her into his side. Ben kept tearing his bread into small pieces and not eating them. Nobody said Otto's name.

Through the dark glass the tank stood lit in its own room, the gel going pale, then deeper, then pale. On the glass at the height of Otto's hands, Ben's two prints were still there, fingers spread, fogged at the palms, and in the gap between them, where a third hand would have gone, there was only blue.

She watched the prints shrink. She watched the flat line. She ate with the hand that was hers, and the other one lay on the blanket and closed, half a breath behind, every time she thought of him.
