# Chapter 12: In the Blue (I)

*“My name is Shu Chan,” the old man said. “Where I come from is dust now. I was the last guard of a place that no longer exists. I have been waiting for someone to find me.” He touched Otto’s forehead with one finger, and for a heartbeat Otto stood inside a stillness so deep that the world made sense.*

— Book 1, Chapter 3: The Initiation

## Now: The tank

In the blue, there was no time.

Otto knew that the way one knows things in dreams: completely, and without being able to say how. There was warm and there was cool. The warm came and sat on him and went, and after it the cool, and he could no more count them than count his own breaths, which he was fairly sure were still happening somewhere.

There was a pull in his arms. It came and went, as if someone far away kept asking his muscles to lift something and then letting them rest. He did not know who was asking, or what the weight was. His arms answered anyway, the way a dog answers a whistle from a man it has never met.

There was a sound, very faint and very steady, which might have been his own heart or might have been something else keeping time for it.

And there was the blue. He could not have said whether he saw it or only knew it. Light through closed lids, perhaps. Or the colour his mind gave to the deep place Master Chan had taught him to go, the colour of the breath at an old man's skin. It was everywhere, and it held him the way water holds a man who has stopped swimming.

His tongue lay against the roof of his mouth and had been lying there a long while. That was the one part of himself he could find. He kept going back to it, the way you keep touching a sore tooth.

---

Sometimes there were voices.

They came from a long way up, and they came without words. The words were taken off them somewhere between there and here, the way the skin comes off a thing when it is boiled, and what reached him was the rhythm underneath. A run of low sounds. A stop. A run again, shorter, with a lift at the end.

He did not know whose they were. He knew they were his. The way you know your own front step in the dark.

One of them came more than the others. It had a pattern: a sentence, then a pause the exact length of a sentence, room left for a reply, then on without one. Patient. Even. A voice that had sat for hours over a coil with a bent pin, and talked to the coil.

Otto listened to it the way he had listened to rain on the shed roof. He could not have said what it said. He could have said it was raining.

---

He had stopped swimming a long time ago. He remembered that much.

There had been a ring of stones. Ten. A floor of fire under them, and three people who needed the stones to stay up, and he had gone down into the stillness Master Chan had taught him, deeper than he had ever gone, to find what it took to hold them. He had found it. The cost had come out of his nose and run warm into his mouth, and he had swallowed it rather than let go to wipe it. He remembered the taste more clearly than the fire.

Gage's face, grey, tipped up at him. *Promise me.*

*I promise, brother.*

He had said it so Gage would move. They had both known. The circle had been shoulder width and closing, and Otto had looked at the hole and the three of them below, and smiled, because the smile was the last thing of his that still worked, and waved. One hand, open. The stones had drifted in.

So that was his. He had broken a promise to the one person who had never broken one to him, on purpose, and done it well.

He thought he would mind more. In the blue, minding was another thing that came and went, like the warm.

---

The shed was still there when he looked for it. The shed was always there.

Back row of Miner Town, a slat missing at the bottom where the dust came in on the wind and lay in a line across the floor like something drawn. He was six, then seven. The old man sat cross-legged in the middle of it with the blue light at the edge of his skin, faint, the kind you would miss if you blinked, and the butterfly on his shoulder opening and closing its wings in no hurry at all. Shu Chan called the light the breath. He called the butterfly the proof. Proof of what, he never said, and Otto had learned young that asking twice was a good way to spend an evening holding a stance.

"The breath first," Shu Chan said. "Then the bones. Then the standing. The fist is the last thing, Otto, and the least."

"I'm good at the fist."

"Yes. That is the problem."

So he stood. Horse stance until his thighs shook and then past it. Shu Chan stood with him, and never shook, and when the old man held a stance a long time the big toe of his left foot lifted off the boards a hair and stayed lifted, like something under the floor was pulling at it and he was declining.

One night, late, with the lamp down to a bead, Shu Chan said a word under his breath.

"Kalinga."

He said it the way other men said a name they were afraid to forget. Then nothing. The toe stayed lifted.

"What's that?" Otto said. His legs were gone to water. "Kalinga."

For a long while the old man did not answer. The butterfly closed its wings.

"The last place I stood," he said. And then, correcting himself, or adding to it, or taking it back, "A door."

"Where?"

"Breathe, Otto."

That was all he ever got. He had tried it again a year later, and once more the week before the old man died, and both times Shu Chan had looked at him the way a man looks at a stranger who has used his name. Otto had decided it was a prayer. Later, a place, one of the buried-world places gone to dust, where the old man had stood and watched for something that never came. He had never settled which.

He still hadn't.

---

He tried to come up.

He did it the way he had done it a thousand mornings on the shed floor: find the edge of the stillness, put a hand on it, push. He found warm. He found cool. He found the pull in his arms, asking. He pushed anyway, with whatever he still had, and the effort went out of him into the blue and came back as more stillness, the way a stone thrown down a well brings back, instead of a splash, a deeper quiet.

He looked for a wall. A hand. A weight. Something to push against, or hit. He had been a boy who hit things; he had a scalp full of stitches to prove it, and under the blue he could feel the exact place they had been, a small itch the shape of a comma. There was nothing to hit. The stillness was his own. He had made it, breath by breath, until it was deep enough to carry three lives, and he had made it so well that it had closed over his head.

He was not trapped. He was adapted. He had matched the room so perfectly that there was no longer any difference between him and the room, and a thing that is no different from the room cannot leave it.

The old man would have called it an achievement. All that training to arrive at the one stance you couldn't stand up out of. Otto would have laughed, if laughing still happened to him.

---

Above him, the patient voice ran its sentence, and left its gap, and did not go on. A chair scraped, or a door did. Then the voices were a long way off, and then they were not anywhere.

The pull came back into his arms. Lift, it asked him. Rest. Lift.

He lay in the blue with his tongue against the roof of his mouth and a word in him, down at the bottom, where the light did not reach. He went down to it the way he had gone down to the stones. It sat there, round, cold, heavier than anything that size had a right to be.

*Kalinga.*

He could not lift it. He had not stopped trying.
