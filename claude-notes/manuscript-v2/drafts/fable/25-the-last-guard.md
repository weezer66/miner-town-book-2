# Chapter 25: The Last Guard

*"I did not pick you, Otto," Shu Chan said the night before he died. "You picked me, that day in the alley. I had been waiting a long time for a successor. I had begun to think no one would come."*

— Book 1, Chapter 3: The Initiation

## Now: The last guard

Ben came back down with the city still on him, a smell like cold metal and clean laundry, and sat on the end of Otto's bed and told him about a man called Rohan.

Otto had been awake three days. He could hold a cup now. He'd walked to the tank-room door and back twice with the grey-haired medic one step behind him, saying nothing and ready to say it. His legs remembered the floor late, like a name that arrives a second after the man has gone past.

Ben gave it in order. Up there, a commander. Behind the commander, an old master, the last of his kind, who'd once had a student who went below and never came back. Ben had said *Chan* to the commander without meaning to, and the commander had gone very quiet.

"He wants to put you in front of the old man. Once. Tonight." Ben picked at the blanket. "He said to tell you the master's about a hundred."

"Then I'd better not keep him waiting."

Flitzberg came himself. He stood in the doorway with his hands folded and his cheeks smooth, and looked at Otto like a man deciding whether a wall would take his weight.

"Once," he said. "Under the cover your friend wore. You will be back before the lamps go down. You will not say where you sleep."

"I've had a month of lying down," Otto said. "Standing up's the part I'm short on."

Gage sat in the corner with his hands open on his knees and said nothing so loudly that the medic turned to look at him. Otto didn't look. If he looked, he'd stay.

---

The stairs were the worst of it.

He counted them, because Ben would ask. Forty-one to the first landing, his thighs gone to water by thirty, the rail cold and then warm under his hand. The young medic had wrapped his knees under his trousers without being asked, and the bandage squeaked against itself on every step like a boot that hadn't been worn in. At the top he stood and breathed, in through the nose, down past the belly, down into a floor that wasn't boards. Out slow.

*You breathe like a boy about to hit someone.*

"Nobody," he said to the stair, and went on.

The lift was a box that moved without being told where to go. It had a worn patch on the floor the shape of two feet, a little apart, where someone had stood every day for years and never once shifted their weight. He put his own feet in it. They fit. The box went up until his ears closed, and opened onto air.

Nothing was dripping.

That was the whole of what he saw at first, and it took him a long time to see past it. He was seventeen years old and had never stood anywhere that wasn't dripping. The dark here was dry. The lights went up instead of down, stacked in the towers in rows and then in rows above those, until his neck wouldn't tilt any farther and they were still going. The air had no taste at all. It went into him like water from a cup that had never had anything else in it.

A man was waiting under the first light.

Otto sized him up before he could stop himself, the way you check a floor before you trust it. Big, broad through the chest, in a red-black coat with the collar up. Twenty or so. A scar along the jaw, old. He stood with his weight off his left knee and didn't know he was doing it, and the knuckle of his right hand was dark and crusted under a glaze of glue. A fighter who'd been hit lately, and hadn't hit back, and hadn't slept.

The man looked at Otto for as long as Otto had looked at him, and Otto watched him look for something to measure, and find nothing to put a hand on. A thin boy in borrowed grey, bandaged knees, a wrist band that said he was no one in particular.

"You're very still," Rohan said.

"I've been practicing."

Something moved in the scarred jaw that might have been the start of a smile and decided against it. Rohan turned, and walked, and set his pace to the knees without being asked.

---

The hall was dark except for one lamp at the far end, and it smelled of feet and rubber and old sweat, the honest smell of every place in the world where people had been thrown down and got up. Otto stopped in the doorway and breathed it in.

A mat the colour of sand. A red line painted round it for a boundary. Along the near wall a shoe rack, three shelves, every slot empty but one, and in that one a single small sock, inside out, grey at the heel, that had sat there long enough to go stiff.

"Whose class?" Otto said.

"Mine." Rohan was looking at the sock. "Suspended."

He said the word as the medic said *stages*, with everything else packed down under it.

Otto smelled the tea before he saw the man.

Something that wasn't tea. A smell like wet iron and a green thing dried too long, and under it a sweetness that stayed on the back of the tongue. He knew it as his hand knew the shape of the cup it came in, with a skin across the top that you had to break with your lip. It came from the far end of the hall, where the one lamp was, and he walked toward it without deciding to.

The old man sat on a cushion below the lamp with a cup in both hands.

He didn't stand. He was ancient past the point where standing proved anything, worn down to bone and skin and the long white hair over his shoulders. Ivory cloth, no crest. At his waist a belt that had once been black and had been rubbed, thread by thread, nearly back to white. His eyes came up off the cup and rested on Otto, and Otto's chest went tight, and he didn't want a word for it.

The old man in the shed had looked at him like that, over the rim of a cup, on the third night, when Otto had followed a blue light he could have missed by blinking.

"You are Otto," the master said.

"Yeah." His voice came out rough from the stair. "Yes. Master."

"I am Vayun. The commander tells me you were taught."

"I was."

"Then do not tell me about it." Vayun drank. "Stand in horse stance. There. Where the floor is pale."

Otto looked down. The mat was worn paler in a patch a stride wide, where a great many people had been made to stand and stay standing. He stepped into it. Rohan stayed at the red line and didn't cross it.

Feet wide. Knees out. Weight down into the floor, down through the bandages, down into the dark under the hall that wasn't the dark under the shed and was. His thighs took it and began, at once, to burn. Three days of walking to a door and back. He let them burn. The burning was his. It was something to stand on.

Vayun watched him and said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far off a door closed and the sound came down the hall and lay on the mat.

A minute. Two. His legs shook and he let them shake and didn't come up. Sweat ran into the corner of his mouth and tasted of the gel he'd slept in, faintly, like warm pennies. He breathed in and down and out slow, and the shaking went into the breath and came out smaller.

And his left big toe lifted off the mat.

He didn't do it. He felt it go, a hair's width, and stay, as if something under the floor had taken hold of it and he was declining, and his eyes went to it before he could stop them, and then to the old man's.

Vayun set his cup down on the mat.

He did it slowly, and he missed. The cup's edge caught the mat and tipped, and a little tea went over the rim, and his hand, which had been steady all this while, stayed on the cup afterward and wasn't.

Rohan, at the line, took a breath and let it out without a sound.

Otto held the stance. The toe stayed up. The word came out of him on the next breath, so low it was nearly the breath itself, and he hadn't meant to say it, and hadn't known it was there until it was in the air between them.

"Kalinga."

---

The eleventh stone. He'd had his fingers under it for weeks in the blue without knowing, and now it was out, and lying on the mat in front of a man who knew what it weighed.

Vayun's hand came off the cup.

"Up," he said.

Otto came up out of the stance. His legs told him about it all the way, and he let them, and stood.

"Who taught you that word?"

"He did. He said it in a stance. Like that one." Otto wiped his palms down his trousers and heard himself do it, and stopped. "I asked him what it was. He said it was the last place he stood watch. Then he said it was a door. Then he sent me home."

"He would." The old man's mouth moved by less than a smile. "What did he call himself, below?"

"Shu Chan."

"Chan." Vayun said it to the cup. "Yes."

Otto waited. Rohan waited at the line. The lamp hissed.

"There was a war," Vayun said, "a long time ago, in a country that is dust. A king won it and walked the field afterward and saw what winning was, and laid his sword flat across his hands and never lifted it again. This house is named for him. He stands in stone below this hall, with the sword across his palms." He drew a breath that went a long way down for a man his age. "After the six days, when the city was rebuilt, this house was given a boundary to keep. A small post, at the edge of a place that had been sealed. To watch it. They named the post for the war. For the laying down, not the fighting."

"Kalinga."

"The post. Not the war." Vayun turned the cup a quarter turn on the mat, where it had spilled. "It stood for a long time. Then the city decided it no longer wanted to remember that there was anything to watch. The post was dissolved. It was taken off the maps. Its guards were given other duties and told nothing. All but one."

Otto's legs wanted the floor. He stayed on them.

"He was my student. He fell better than anyone I have taught before or since, and I have taught for eighty years. When the post was struck he came to me and said that a thing sealed is a thing someone must stand outside of, or the seal means nothing. Then he went below." The old man looked at the lamp. "I did not see him again. For thirty years I thought him dead. For the last ten, I knew it."

*Where I come from is dust now. I was the last guard of a place that no longer exists.*

Otto had heard that at six, in a shed, and taken it for the kind of thing old men said. He stood on a pale patch of mat with four nights left of a count he hadn't been told the whole of, and under this hall, under this city, under the lift and the forty-one stairs, was the sealed place, and in it a bed with his blanket folded down once. He didn't say so. He swallowed, and the swallow was loud.

"He lived in a shed," he said instead. "Back row. Behind the Junkyard. I saw him get beaten in an alley by three men and he got up afterward like he'd lain down on purpose. I followed him home."

"He fell well."

"He said he'd been waiting for someone to find him."

Vayun closed his eyes. When he opened them they were on Otto's left hand, which hung at his side, and Otto looked down and saw what the old man saw: at the edge of it, where the skin met the dark of the hall, a faint shimmer, the kind that comes off a slag heap at noon.

He hadn't called it. It was there.

"He gave it to you," Vayun said.

"What is it?"

"His."

Otto waited for more. There wasn't more. He saw the old man's face settle on that answer like a door coming to rest in its frame, and understood he could stand here till the lamp burned out and get nothing past it.

At the red line, Rohan spoke. He hadn't since the sock.

"We teach his forms in every hall in this house, every morning." His voice was low and had something caught in it. "We thought the rest of it went into the ground with the post."

Vayun didn't look round. "So did I."

---

"He came to me in the blue," Otto said.

He hadn't planned to say that either. The hall was taking things out of him tonight, like the gel taking the crust off his skin.

"When I was under. He sat in the yard behind the shed with his back straight and told me I'd adapted so hard I'd disappeared. He said adapting was finished. He said it was time to fight." Otto looked at his right hand, the first finger. "So I fought. One finger. It took me a long time. I came back."

Vayun regarded him across the spilled tea.

"And now you are standing in Ashoka's hall with your legs shaking, asking what comes after."

"I didn't ask."

"You asked when you came up the stair." The old man's eyes narrowed with something that was nearly amusement and had a great deal of weight under it. "Very well. Your teacher gave you the fist last, as he was taught. I will give you what he would have given you next, if he had lived to see you this age. Two things. The second is the harder."

He held up one finger. Chan had done that, in the yard.

"Force that arrives without anger. You have that. I watched your legs burn and your face do nothing. Good."

A second finger.

"And the stop after the correct action."

Otto waited.

"A fight ends. The threat is finished, the man is down, the door is shut. And the body does not know. It wants one more. It wants to be sure. Every student I have ever lost, I lost there, in the breath after the right thing was done, when they went on and did one wrong thing more." He lowered the hand. "When the threat has ended, you stop. The stop is the whole of the art. The rest is how you arrive at it."

"How do you know it's ended?"

"You see it. You cannot see it if you are afraid. Fear is loud. It fills the eyes." Vayun picked up his cup, found it nearly empty, and drank what was in it anyway. "A man who is not afraid gives the other man nothing to read."

Otto turned that over. He thought of a hole in a floor of fire, and ten stones, and how still he'd had to be to hold them, and how the stillness had been the only thing in that room the fire couldn't read.

"Nothing to read," he said.

"Nothing." The old man set the cup down. This time it stood. "Now. Stand where you were."

Otto stood on the pale patch. Vayun rose.

He did it in one movement, from cushion to feet, without a hand on the floor or a sound, a man of ninety-eight years standing up like a flame finding air. The belt that had been black swung once at his waist and settled. He crossed the mat without seeming to hurry and stopped in front of Otto, close enough that Otto could see the tea on his lip.

"Do not move," Vayun said, "unless you must."

Then his hand came.

It came from nowhere, from the shoulder, no wind-up and no warning, open-palmed and fast as a dropped stone, straight at Otto's face, and Otto saw it.

He saw where it was going to be. Chan had said that, in the yard, with the butterfly on his shoulder: most fights are won by the man who can be still long enough to see where the other man is going to be. Otto was still. He saw the hand's whole road from the shoulder to the air in front of his nose, and he saw the end of it, which was a breath short of his skin, and so he didn't move. His eyes stayed open. His weight stayed down in the floor. His toe, he found afterward, had lifted again.

The palm stopped where he'd seen it stop. The air it had pushed arrived a beat later and moved his hair.

Vayun looked at his own hand, and then at Otto's eyes, and nodded once.

He went back to his cushion and lowered himself onto it, slower than he'd risen, and picked up the empty cup and looked into it anyway.

"Your teacher would have liked to see that," he said, to the cup.

Otto stood on the pale mat and breathed. In through the nose. Down past the belly. Down into a floor under which, a long way down, an old man had once stood watch, and been forgotten, and gone on standing.

Rohan was looking at the sock.

---

The lift went down until his ears closed. Forty-one stairs, with the rail cold and then warm.

He didn't count them this time. He counted nights instead, four, and found he couldn't make them weigh what they should, and let it go. His knees had stopped telling him anything. The bandage squeaked.

Gage was at the bottom.

He stood on the last landing with his back to the wall and his hands open at his sides, palms out, the way he'd kept them for weeks so that nobody down here would mistake them for anything else. He'd been there a long time. Otto could tell by the floor.

He came down the last six steps on his own, and stopped on the one above Gage so their eyes were level.

"Well?" Gage said.

Otto thought about it.

"He had the same tea," he said, and put his hand in one of the open ones, and Gage closed it.
