# Chapter 3: Found One

*He was a man carrying a piece of corporate property welded to his skeleton that he couldn’t use and they couldn’t be bothered to collect.*

— Book 1, Chapter 14: Phantom Leg

## Now: The treatment room, beneath the city

The tape was the problem.

Everything else in the room had been arranged to be ignored. The light was low and came from nowhere in particular. The bed held her the way the stretcher had, without finding any one place to press. The seal on her shoulder had gone from cold to nothing, which was what a good seal did. But the tape across the back of her right hand had caught a single hair when they laid it down, and every time she bent the knuckle the hair pulled, and she bent the knuckle, and it pulled, and she bent it again to see.

She had been told she'd be angry about the tape. She was keeping that for later, like the hand that wasn't there. She'd put the phantom away at the back of the shelf when they lifted her off the floor, closed, and it was still closed now. She could feel the nails of it in a palm that had gone into a drone's light two days ago.

Sleep came in pieces and left the same way. Each time she surfaced the oldest medic was in the chair by the wall, hands folded in her lap, scrubbed pink to the wrist, and the clear bag on its pole had gone down by exactly the amount Camilla would have guessed. The fourth time, the bag was empty and the medic was looking at her.

"You are counting the drip," the medic said.

"Somebody should."

"Somebody is. I would not have you lie awake to do my job." She stood, and a second person was standing behind her who had not been there before, a man with a flat grey case under one arm and ink on his thumbs. "You are stronger than you were. Strong enough, I think, to be asked a question you will want answered properly. Do you want it tonight, or do you want it with your brother in the room?"

Camilla looked at the case. It was the length of an arm.

"Tonight."

---

The man set the case on the rolling table and opened it toward her, the way the medic had opened hers on the floor. He didn't take the thing out. He let her look.

Dull grey, unpolished, matte as a well-used file. Jointed at the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist, and the joints made no attempt to be anything other than joints. The forearm had seams in it finer than a hair. The fingers were long, longer than hers had been, and lay a little curled, the way a hand lies when nobody's telling it anything. The thumb was set lower than a thumb should be. It gave the whole hand the look of something asking.

"It is for you," the medic said. "If you want it. The decision is yours, and it is not a decision you must make now, or ever."

"How does it know what to do?"

The engineer answered that. His voice was dry and used to rooms where nobody asked. "It listens to the shoulder. Where the arm stopped, the nerves that ran it did not. They are still sending. The cup sits over the seal and hears them. At first it will be guessing what you mean. It learns your timing in a few days. After that, it is yours."

*The map stays drawn after the country is gone.* She had told Gerald that over a broken table with mash between them, like a manual. She hadn't known the map could be read.

She lay looking at the arm, and what she saw was a man on a broken chair with sweat running into his eyes, working a leather casing off his thigh. A sleek leg that had answered to its maker and then one morning hadn't. A license lapsed sometime overnight. No alert. The system had stopped recognizing him, and he'd carried the dead weight of it welded to his bone until he ground the smart parts off with a borrowed grinder and hung drone struts in their place, because struts didn't ask anyone's permission to swing.

"Who owns it," she said.

The medic didn't pretend to misunderstand. "It was made here. It is registered nowhere above. No account is attached to it, and there is no license, because there is no one to sell one."

"Can it be switched off?"

"Yes." No pause before the word. "From this room. By me, or by him, if the shoulder refuses it, or if it begins to hurt you in a way we cannot correct. You will be told before it happens, and you will be told why."

"That's you two. Who else?"

The medic looked at the engineer, and the engineer looked at the arm.

"I will not tell you no one," the medic said. "I have not opened every door in this place, and I will not swear to what is behind the ones I have not opened. What I can tell you is this: nothing has ever been taken off a person in this room against their will, in my time here, and my time here is long. I would know. I would have been the one holding the instrument."

Honest, and short of the whole. Camilla filed the gap where it would stay found. She'd want a door for that, later. She'd want every door.

The arm lay in its case, fingers a little curled.

The last machine anyone had put on her had been the ring in the first room, around her skull, and she hadn't been asked. This one was asking. That was the only new thing about it, and it turned out to be enough.

"Yes," she said.

---

They did it with the lights up and her watching, because she said she'd watch or they'd do it without her, and the medic took her at her word.

The cup went over the seal first, cool and then warm, and the warmth went in deeper than the sealant had, all the way to the place where the shoulder had been holding a door shut for two days. Something in there opened. She'd been braced for pain. What came was more like being heard, a line of her that had been talking into nothing since the railing, and something on the other end at last picking up.

The engineer set the arm into the cup and turned a collar she couldn't see. There was a click like a wrench finding its socket. Then weight. The arm was heavier than her own had been, and the weight pulled on the shoulder in the old direction, and her body, which had been leaning to the right for two days without telling her, straightened up on its own.

"Do not try to move it," the medic said. "Think of moving it. The thinking is the movement, to it."

She thought of closing her hand.

Nothing. Then the fingers folded, one after another, into a fist, half a breath after she'd finished thinking it, exactly the way the phantom had folded on the floor of the fourth room, only this time there was metal where the nothing had been. The fist sat there at the end of her, closed. She hadn't felt it close. She'd felt the shoulder say *close* and then she'd watched it happen somewhere else, late, like a shout coming back off a far wall.

Her mouth had gone dry. That was fear, and she let it stand where it was, and looked at it, and then looked past it.

*Open.*

The fingers opened. Late again. The same lateness, to the hair. A machine with a delay in it was a machine with a timing, and a timing could be learned. She opened and closed the hand five times, counting the gap, and on the fifth it was shorter.

The medic was watching her face and not the hand. "It is guessing," she said.

"It guesses well."

"It will guess better. You will teach it without meaning to."

Camilla turned the wrist. The wrist turned, a fraction behind her eyes. She'd spent her life with her ear against machines, listening for what they wanted before she touched anything. This was the first one that had ever put its ear against her. She lay there in the low light with her new hand opening and closing at the end of the bed, late, and later, and less late, and didn't notice when she slept.

---

Morning in that place was a decision someone else made. The light came up by degrees and the room agreed to be grey. She was on her feet before it finished, and the floor was cold through her socks, and the arm hung at her side and did not swing when she walked, because she hadn't thought to swing it.

"The boys," she said. "Are they up?"

"Not yet. I will wake them when you ask it."

"Don't bring them here first. Take them to Otto."

The medic's face did nothing, which in that face was a question.

"You said a line moved." Camilla had got that out of her at the tank, at some hour that wasn't night and wasn't morning, with the metal hand flat on the glass. "When they sat with him. Then they sit with him first. That's not a favor to me. That's a reading."

"I will tell them it was your instruction."

"Tell them it was my words."

---

She had gone to him before dawn, with the medic a step behind her and the corridor lights waking ahead, one and then the next. She'd stopped once, to see. They'd stopped with her.

The observation room was small and bright, and most of it was taken up by the tank.

It stood taller than Gage, a clear cylinder on a dark base, and it was full of something that wasn't water. Thicker. Faintly blue, the blue of the sealant from the can. Otto hung inside it with his eyes closed, arms a little away from his sides, a clear mask over his nose and mouth and a line running up from it into the lid. His hair moved in the gel, so slowly she had to look away and back to be sure. He was neither standing nor lying down. The gel held him the way water holds a swimmer who has stopped swimming.

A bubble had come loose from the mask seal and was climbing beside his cheek. She watched it. It took the time it would take to walk across a room.

The panel beside the tank had seven lines on it. Six were straight. The seventh rose and fell in a slow even wave that she decided was breath.

"Tell me how it works," she said. "All of it."

The medic came and stood beside her. "He is not frozen. He is warm. The gel is cycled: warm for a time, then cool, then warm again. While it is warm, we tell his muscles to work. Small signals, through the gel. Nothing he would feel as pain. His body thinks it is hauling. Then we cool it, and his body thinks it is resting. Then again."

"How long is a cycle?"

"An hour, a little more. A short day. Then another."

"Why not a real one?"

"Because a body in a real day grows used to it. We want his to keep being asked."

"And the signals. Where do they go in?"

"Through the gel itself. It carries current the way salt water does, and better. We shape where it goes."

Camilla put her right hand on the glass. Then, after a half-breath's argument with herself, the left. The metal fingers arrived and lay flat, and through them she felt nothing, and through the glass under her right palm she felt the gel start to warm.

The blue went paler. Inside it Otto's fingers tightened by a hair, told to haul by a machine, and the cords of his forearms stood up under the skin.

"A working body is a loud thing. If any part of him is near enough to hear it, we want it to hear something worth coming home to."

"Has it done anything?"

"His readings are flat. They have been flat since the trial room." The medic looked at the panel, then at Camilla. "Except once. Last night, in the hour your brother and the other boy sat with him. One line moved. I do not know what it means. I would be lying if I said I did."

"Which line?"

The medic touched the panel. The fourth. It lay straight as a shelf now.

"But it moved."

"It moved."

Camilla looked at the fourth line for a while. Then she looked at Otto's hanging hand, the one nearest the glass, and counted the fingers on it, which was stupid, and did it anyway. Five. Five on hers. Ten in the room, and only five of them anyone's.

---

She was standing when they came in.

She had thought about it. Sitting would have been easier; the arm pulled, and the floor was cold, and her legs had the bad-week feeling of a shift that had gone on too long. But Gage had seen her on the floor in the fourth room and on the floor of the trial room before that, and she'd heard what *half-dead* sounded like in his mouth. He wasn't going to get her lying down twice. So she stood in the middle of the room in a grey shirt they'd cut one sleeve off, and she put her weight even on both feet, and she put the arm where it would be seen, and she waited for the door.

It opened, and he filled it.

She watched him see her. The eyes first, going over her face and finding it working. Then down, to the shoulder, and the metal, and all the way to the long still fingers at her hip, and she watched his face try to decide what the arm was and give up and come back to her eyes.

He crossed the room before anyone spoke and put his arms around her, with care, on the side that was still hers. Her good hand came up at once and gripped the back of his shirt.

The metal hand came up too. A heartbeat late. She felt the shoulder say it, and then felt nothing, and then felt the weight of the arm lift and pause in the air behind him, as if checking its own permission, and settle flat between his shoulder blades with a care she hadn't had time to decide on. It had read her. It had gone and been gentle with her brother before she'd finished meaning to.

"Lost an arm," she said into his shoulder. "Found one."

He didn't let go right away. She felt him feel it: the weight of the hand on his back, how still it was, how big. His breath changed under her ear. He'd worked out, with no one telling him, how easily it could have closed.

Ben was in the doorway, grinning and wiping his face with his sleeve, and not coming in, and she looked over Gage's shoulder and said, "You too. Come here," and he came, and she had them both, one hand of each kind.

"Does it hurt?" Gage said.

"No." She let Ben go. She lifted the arm and turned it, watching the elbow, and the elbow turned a fraction behind her eyes. "It's late. Half a breath behind me, every time. They say it listens to the shoulder, what's left of the nerves there, and it'll learn my timing in a few days. Right now it's guessing." She flexed the fingers, one after another, and they followed in order, each one a little behind her thought. "It guesses well."

"They put that on you overnight."

"They offered. I said yes." She gave him the look, the dinner-table one, the one for when he was about to be slow about something. "What would you have done?"

He had no answer. She held the metal hand out to him, palm up, the way he'd once held a copper tag out to her across a room full of charcoal and said she read the system better than he did. He touched it. She felt none of that, and watched all of it: his thumb finding the seams, pressing the palm, the palm giving a little under the pressure. His face did the arithmetic. Cool. Seamed. Gave like something, and like nothing he'd ever handled.

"It's stronger than me," he said.

"Much."

She thought *close*. The fingers waited their heartbeat and then shut all at once over his hand, and Gage let out a short sound with no dignity in it and pulled, and the hand went with him, and he didn't come free. Ben laughed out loud. She couldn't remember the last time she'd heard him, and then she could: the shack, a radio, before the gates. Nothing since the mine.

"Cami."

"Half a breath late," she said, holding on, "and still faster than you." *Open.* The fingers opened. She winked at him. "It's learning what I want to do. Give it a week and it'll learn what you need to be told, too. Which is usually the same thing twice."

He shook his hand out and looked at it. The fingers had left no mark. She saw that bother him more than a mark would have.

"And it's learning you," he said.

"Yes." She laid her good hand over the metal wrist and held it there, the way she'd held every machine she had ever taken apart, listening before she touched anything. There was nothing to hear. It was warmer than the room. "I'll learn it first," she said. "I'm faster."

She watched him believe her.

It went through his face like water going into dry ground, the way it had when she told him the number on his chest was a key and he'd stood there with the knowledge in him and no idea yet what it would cost. Right behind the believing came the other thing, the one that tightened the skin around his eyes and set his jaw an inch forward: a brother hearing his sister say she'd win a race he couldn't follow her into. He'd carried her out of two rooms. He couldn't carry her out of this.

He put his own hand over hers, over the metal, and left it there. Three hands on one wrist. Ben came and put his on top, because Ben would.

---

The medic had left the door open. It gave onto the corridor, and the corridor lights were still up, all of them, the whole run of them back to the observation room, lit the way they lit for someone walking.

Nobody was walking under them. The three of them stood in a room built for four, and the lights outside stayed on anyway, holding a place for a boy who hadn't come down the hall, and she stood with her brother's hand and Ben's hand on a wrist she couldn't feel, and counted.

Three.

She'd count again tomorrow.
