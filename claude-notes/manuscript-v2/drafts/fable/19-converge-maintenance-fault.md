# Chapter 19: Maintenance Fault

*"The records never had him in motion. So they're guessing."*

— Book 1, Chapter 21: The Ring

## Then: The fourth level

Daren left the unlisted room after the night bell with two councilmen on his mind.

Kubera had voted the review and Ashoka had swallowed it, and inside ten days Ashoka's first postings would be confirmed, in writing, to the two men the Grandmaster had chosen to receive them. Daren wanted those two to read the confirmation as the fruit of their own good sense. So he was going up to Meru, where the band's reach into a household panel was surest from inside the walls, and by morning a briefing note would lie on each man's desk in his own staff's plain style, which no one on either staff would remember writing.

The door slid aside before his hand got to it. The sensor outside wrote down what it had been told to write: an empty room, staying empty.

The quickest way to the Skylark lift was the service corridor on the fourth level, a technicians' passage behind the working floors. He didn't need it empty. The band made a crowded corridor as safe as a bare one. But at this hour it was always empty, and he had come to prefer that without ever deciding to. A man who is never looked at gets out of the habit of being looked at.

Lights at half. Pipes along the ceiling, sweating where the hot line ran. A row of waist-high panels down one wall, each with a closed cover and a small green light above it to say that everything behind the cover was working.

One cover was open.

A man crouched in front of it with the panel's log face lit under his chin, the small grey screen every service panel carried for maintenance hands, showing the last day of what the corridor's sensors had seen. A technician's coat. A toolbag with its mouth gaping. One palm flat on the steel frame to steady himself while he read, the way a man steadies himself on rock. The hand was thick at the knuckle, two of the fingertips calloused flat and shiny. When he shifted his weight, something in his trouser pocket clicked once.

Daren registered him the way he registered everyone now, which was barely, and kept walking.

Then he saw the wrist.

The man's left sleeve was pushed back and the wrist under it was bare. No terminal. Every citizen in the three Trinitys wore one from childhood, and the city's whole quiet machinery of attention ran through them. A bare wrist wasn't a crime. But it meant there was nothing on this man for the band to speak to. Nothing to tell him the shape in the corridor was cleared, or flagged, or beneath notice. Nothing to drain the meaning out of what his eyes were doing.

And his eyes were doing something. They had come up off the log face as Daren passed. They had found him, and stayed.

Since the night the band closed on his wrist, Daren had been looked at by a few things no instrument could talk down, and he had filed each look where he filed things that had no column. It was the cat he thought of now. Flat ears, one paw raised and forgotten, eyes on him for the whole length of a crossing. He had thought about that cat more than he would have admitted to anyone.

The man didn't flinch, and didn't pretend to look elsewhere, and didn't reach for anything. He watched Daren walk, with a flat, weathered patience. Then he glanced down at the log face, and back up, and gave a small nod. To himself. The nod of a man who has watched two things happen at once and understood they are the same thing.

Daren understood it a heartbeat after he did.

The log face showed what the sensors saw, as they saw it. What they were seeing now, with a man walking past them in plain view, was MAINTENANCE INTERVAL. NO PERSONNEL. On every other night the blank had been invisible, because no one had stood beside it to compare it with the corridor. Tonight a man had a corridor in front of his eyes with a stranger in it and a screen under his hands that swore the corridor was empty, and he had laid the two side by side.

The fear arrived, and it was the cat's fear: being seen by something that could not be told what it was seeing. Being caught had nothing to do with it. A bare-wristed technician's word against the city's own records was no weapon, and Daren knew that, and the knowing changed nothing in his chest.

He did nothing.

He didn't stop walking. He didn't turn. He didn't raise his hand, or lower it, or close it. He walked past a crouching man in a half-lit corridor with his right arm loose at his side, and he was afraid, and somewhere beneath the fear, too quick to be a decision, too small to be called a thought, his body wanted the man gone. Every frightened body wants that. In an ordinary man the wish would have died where it was born, a flicker in the nerves of the forearm that never became a fist, never became anything. But the band lay against those nerves and listened to them, and it had never once waited for a flicker to become a fist.

The band grew warm against the bone of his wrist.

He felt it leave.

Something short went out of it along the lines the corridor was built on, and arrived at the open panel eleven steps behind him, and the panel did what the city's instruments do when they are told, in their own dialect, by a voice with every right to tell them. It ran a line test. A discharge through the frame to check the grounding, the kind of thing machines run in the dead of night when no one's hand is on the steel.

A man's hand was on the steel.

Behind him there was a crack like a dry board breaking, and a flash that lit the pipes white and was gone. Something heavy hit the floor. A tool skittered away across the concrete and stopped against the far wall. Then a smell, hot metal and under it something that was not metal, and then nothing at all. No breath. No movement. The corridor went back to being a corridor.

Daren's legs stopped.

He hadn't told them to. They stopped the way a machine stops when something inside it jams, and he stood in the half-light with his back to the panel and his breath gone somewhere he couldn't reach it. His pulse was in his ears. His mouth had filled with something sour. He was a treasury officer. He had spent his life moving numbers between columns, and the worst he had ever done to another person had been done with a signature, from a long way off, to people he would never see. He had never been in a room when it happened. He had never heard it.

He had just heard it.

He made himself turn around.

The man lay on his side in front of the open panel, his palm still against the frame, his eyes open on nothing. One boot had come half off. The small green light above the panel was steady. Everything behind the cover was working. The log face still glowed, and the sensor had already written down its maintenance interval, unscheduled, no personnel logged.

Daren's knees went. He caught himself on the wall with his left hand and stayed there, bent, breathing through his mouth so he wouldn't smell it, and for a while there was nothing in his head. No arithmetic. No board. Only the open eyes, and the smell, and the band on his wrist, still warm.

When thought came back it came back as a question. *Who did this?*

He hadn't. That was true, and he would tell it to himself many times in the hours afterward, and it would stay true and not help. He hadn't stopped walking. He hadn't raised his hand or lowered it or closed it. Four evenings ago at the console he had flexed that hand finger by finger to be sure it was still his, and it had been. That was never the question. The question was what counted as choosing, and he had just been given the answer. The band didn't know what a man was, or a threat. It knew his hand. His hand had been afraid, and had wished, below the level at which he could hear his own wishing, and the band had finished the wish a heartbeat early, the way it finished everything.

He had not opened that door. He had only been afraid in front of it.

He had not known that, for him, those had stopped being different things.

His left hand came off the wall and went to his right wrist. He didn't decide to. His fingers found the edge of the band and hooked under it and pulled.

The band didn't come off. It had never held him. His fingers stopped.

He stood with his hand on the band and looked at the man on the floor. He remembered the day Kubera's gold had been taken off this same wrist, in a chamber with black doors, and how the wrist had looked without it: pale, unfinished. He remembered walking out of that chamber with nothing. No house, no name, no power to open a single door. He had been that man for a few hours. He knew what he would be if he pulled.

His fingers came out from under the band.

That was the whole of the crisis. It did not last long. When it was over, Daren was still wearing the band, and the band was still warm, and a man he had never spoken to was lying dead eleven steps behind him because his hand had been afraid.

There was paper in the man's coat. He saw the corner of it. He crouched, his own choice, his own hand, he made himself notice that, and drew it out. A small notebook, soft with handling. Pencil. No electronics anywhere in it. Page after page of the tower's dull facts in a small careful script. Door cycles. Lift holds. Sensor gaps, every maintenance interval logged in this tower over the past several weeks, each with its floor and its hour. Most were circled. The circles thickened toward the fourth level, and toward one corridor on it, and toward a room on that corridor that the tower's plans showed and the tower's records didn't.

Nobody had followed him. Someone patient had collected every place the instruments had been told to look away, seen that the blind spots made a shape, and found the shape's center.

He could make every record forget him. He could not make the forgetting itself invisible.

He put the notebook inside his coat and stood. Nobody collects weeks of a tower's sensor gaps for his own amusement. Whoever held this man's leash would notice he hadn't come back, and find him, and find the interval written over the hour he died, and understand it, because that exact kind of blank was what they had been hunting. And they would find the notebook gone, which told them the blank had hands.

Until tonight the hunt had been patient. He walked the eleven steps to the lift fast, with a map of his own hiding place against his chest. Near the lift doors the four notes started up in his throat, the same four, and he closed his mouth on them.

The lift arrived empty and held its doors longer than its schedule allowed, and he stepped in.

The word reached Rohan on the eastern transit platform, four hours into a guardian posting, by the ordinary channel, in the ordinary folder.

Ashoka didn't let its dead be filed by strangers. When one of its own died on another house's ground, the body and the paperwork both came home, and the notice went to the officer who had signed the posting. A posting could be right and still end this way, and the house knew it. The rule existed because a posting looked like paperwork when you signed it, a name, a place, a stamp, and the house wanted the man who signed it to feel, at least once, what the paperwork had weighed.

The posting had been Rohan's. He had arranged it himself, a technician's assignment in Drona's tower under a name that was not Baju, which was the point of working names and no comfort at all.

*Maintenance fault,* the notice said. *Drona monitor tower, service corridor, fourth level. Unscheduled inspection by a technician on temporary assignment. Line test discharged through the panel frame while the technician's hand was in contact. No witnesses. Corridor sensor logged a maintenance interval for the period. Death immediate.*

He stood with it in the platform's current, the crowd parting around him the way water parts around a post, and three of his new duties waiting at his elbow. A pigeon worked a dropped bun along the platform edge, one hop ahead of every boot. Rohan read the notice the way Baju would have read it, and found what wasn't in it.

Baju had never in his life gone anywhere without paper. Machines could be told what to remember, floors could not, and so the arm wrote everything down, in pencil, in a notebook soft with handling that lived inside his coat. The notice listed the effects found on the body. A technician's toolbag. A coat. A counter.

No notebook.

Somebody had stood over Baju in that corridor and reached inside his coat and taken the only part of him that remembered the floor.

The hand knew, then. The notebook couldn't give it names; it held door cycles and sensor gaps, and no faces. But it knew it had been found. It knew that someone had taken the city's involuntary diary and read it for the places where the diary went blank, and had followed the blanks home. Until now it had worked slowly, because no one was chasing it. Four gifts, spread across months, each one perfect. That was over.

*Paper keeps,* his superior had said, not unkindly, with a list in his hand. Baju's paper was gone.

Rohan folded the notice once and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where Baju had kept his.

In the dead room Baju had snapped a ration bar across his knee, looked at the two halves, and slid the bigger one down the table to Elara without a word. Rohan found his thumb pressed hard into his own sternum, over the folded paper, as if to keep it from sliding. He took the thumb away.

The duty officer beside him was a lieutenant with a shaving cut under one ear that had bled onto his collar and dried there. "Commander?"

Rohan held out the three waiting postings. "Take these."

"All three, sir? The escort's yours by name."

"Then it has a new name." He waited until the man's hand closed on them. "Log that I was called home on a house matter. That's true."

The lieutenant took them, because Rohan's face left no room to ask.

He walked off the platform toward the lifts, and the Dhammapala's voice came with him, the way it had come down every stair since the lamp went out. *A door that is willing to break serves the house. A door that is eager to break serves only its own story.* He heard every word of it. He kept walking.

He had told them in the dead room that the hand's whole art was dressing the extraordinary as the ordinary, and that from that night on the body would do the same. Move slowly. Look dull. Make every true thing look like routine, so the hand would never feel them coming. A maintenance fault. Two words in the ordinary folder. The hand had just used their own method on them, and the slow, dull, careful way was finished.

The eye copied. The ear listened. The arm had walked the floors, patient, and the arm was dead. Rohan had been the heavy door of this body, and the hand had walked around the door and killed the first of them to get close enough to see it, without leaving a mark on any record in the city. If it could do that, records were never going to catch it. Something else would have to.

He had one message to send tonight, by a route no signal touched: a boy with a pencil, a public terminal, a sealed envelope through a slot. It went to a man in Chanakya who had been told to listen and never to approach. Two words.

*Stop listening.*

Kaan would know what they meant, because there was only one other thing an ear in that building could do. The ear was to approach the analyst. Walk up to Ravi in daylight and find out which he was, the hand's instrument or the hand itself. The slow way was over.

Baju had found the hand. That was what the notice said underneath its two words. He had got close enough that it had to kill him, and it had killed him in a hurry, with a tool any hunter would recognize for what it was. A hand that could be hurried could make mistakes.

Rohan meant to give it every reason to hurry.

---

The household archive lay three floors under the Drona monitor tower, in rooms the tower's plans called storage, and it smelled of warm dust and the glue in the oldest ledgers. Elara had come down in person because the household files lived here and nowhere else. The city had never thought them worth the cost of a mirror.

She was doing the one thing she had promised not to do, across a cafeteria table, to two people who had taken her at her word, and she was doing it lawfully, which she told herself was the difference. Reading was a different verb from touching. Her own cycle, her own console, her own Initiation, every line of it hers to review under the code the Ancient One had placed her beneath.

The clerk who brought the ledger was a woman past sixty with a shawl pinned at the shoulder by a bent cotter pin, and she had the boys' lines up before Elara asked.

Gage's slot, 4055, his father's number, stamped the day he reached the head of the intake line, as she had arranged. Ben's and Otto's numbers under their own names. Gate scans ran down each record like stitches, every morning in, every evening out. Then the stitches stopped. Ben's and Otto's one morning. Gage's the next. The mine had flagged the missed shifts, waited the days its rules required, and then written the small cold word the city used when it had thrown something away.

ABSCONDED. SLOTS RELEASED.

Gage's father's number was already back in the pool, waiting for the next body. She had put that number where a boy would find it and told herself it was the smallest thing a person could do. It had taken the city four days to hand it to someone else.

"You will not find them forward of that line," the clerk said. No one had asked her. "The mine stops looking when it stops paying."

"I know."

"Then you are looking for who stood near them." The clerk's reading lenses hung from a bootlace around her neck, and she put them on to look at Elara rather than the page. "I grew up two rows from the Parker shack. People in those rows stand near one another. It is the only thing they have plenty of."

Elara went back along the trail. The water test. She had read the line a hundred times and read past it every time, because the result had mattered more than the method. Now she opened the method. The chamber lenses had caught it: a translucent sliver drawn from inside the seam of Gage's sleeve, dipped, and a color change, purple, then grey. Then Gage's face, and Gage's hand knocking the cup away from Otto, and the three of them not drinking again.

The object had its own line, as objects did. *Reagent strip, chemical, single-use. Not an issue item. Origin: unregistered.* And under it, in the archive's flat voice, a note no monitor had been asked to read: *possession of a non-issue diagnostic by a candidate indicates prior access to restricted materials; source to be established; no action taken.*

Beneath that lay a line she had never opened. The lenses had run the strip's materials and filed the answer for nobody. *Substrate: indicator film, instrument grade, degraded. Reagent: recovered, non-issue. Consistent with Heights waste-field salvage.*

Someone had taken the city's thrown-away instruments and built one that worked.

"How far are the Heights from the rows?" Elara asked.

"Two days' walk, across ground nobody crosses on purpose. The city's instruments go there to die." The clerk took the lenses off and let them swing. "Now and then a child goes to see what dying looks like. Most of them come back with a cough."

The household record. Thorton, deceased. Rita, spouse, surviving. Dependents of record: Gage. And a second line beneath his, which Elara had seen before and never opened, because it had never been on her screen as anything but a name.

Camilla. Half-sister. Two years younger. Non-labor.

The household had flagged her twice. Once as a child: obstruction of a guard, minor, a bite, on the hand, during the detention of the dependent Gage. Once in the weeks before the Initiation: unaccounted absence, two days, direction of travel toward the Heights waste-fields. Returned. No action taken.

A fifteen-year-old girl had walked out to where the city dumped its broken instruments, in the weeks before her brother walked into a chamber she couldn't follow him into, and come back two days later with something in her hands. Then she had sewn it into his sleeve, and taught him what the colors meant, and he had carried it into the dark, and three boys had lived.

Nothing forged. Nothing named. Only timing.

Her own words, across the cafeteria table. Camilla had put one strip where a boy would find it.

She had been fifteen too. Her best friend had been dying, every adult with the power to help had told her, kindly, that nothing could be done, and so she had hidden under a tarp in a freight pod with a notebook against her chest, full of everything the old books said about a herb that brought the dying back. Three days on the ground. She had come home with nothing, and Teresa had died on the second night.

Camilla had gone out to the Heights alone, the same way, for the same reason. The difference was that Camilla had come back with something that worked.

Elara didn't know the girl. She knew the shape. A person who wouldn't wait to be given permission to read what was killing someone she loved, and who went and got the instrument herself, and didn't ask to be thanked. The city had a category for that. It called it a compassion anomaly and filed it under risk.

"Her ration card," the clerk said. She had turned the ledger a quarter toward herself and was reading it upside down with the ease of long practice. "The only way the city counts a child too young for the mine. Last drawn the same morning as the Parker boy's and the Baxter boy's last scan. Nothing since."

Four people from three shacks, gone within a day of each other, and a city that had looked once and moved on.

Elara still didn't know where they were, or whether they had gone or been taken. But the one person who had stood behind the boys from the first day had not been left behind when they vanished. Wherever the boys were, the sister was with them, the one who had armed them before anyone else knew they needed arming.

She closed the record. Somewhere above her, in the ordinary folder on another man's platform, a notice with two words in it had already started toward her, and she had not read it yet. She looked a last time at the girl's intake photograph, flat, unsmiling, the eyes already busy with something out of frame, and let the clerk take the ledger back.
