# Chapter 9: Two Levels Down

*“Don’t draw me a forest, Gage.” Her voice hardened, and for a moment she sounded like Pappy. “Draw me a way out.”*

— Book 1, Chapter 17: Mirror Mirror in the Box

## Now: Two levels down, third door

The third door had a handle.

Camilla stood and looked at it before she touched it. Brass, worn pale on the top where hands had gone, a thumb-latch under it, and a key plate with no key. Someone had built this door for people who'd want to open it themselves. She put her good hand on the brass. It was warm, the way metal gets when there's heat running behind a wall.

Inside, the room was long and low, and it smelled of oil that had gone still.

Benches ran down both sides under sheets of grey cloth, and the cloth had settled into the shapes beneath it the way snow settles on scrap: a vise here, the hump of a drill press, something tall and round that might be a lathe. Lamps hung on chains. Along the far wall somebody had painted outlines, white on green, one for every tool, and the tools hung in them. Hammers in the hammer shapes. Wrenches by size, little to big, a staircase of them. Pliers with their jaws open the exact width of the paint.

She counted. Sixty-one shapes. Sixty tools.

The empty one hung at shoulder height between a mallet and a pair of tin snips: a long outline with a curve at one end and two prongs at the other, the shape of nothing she'd ever held. The hook under it was bare. The paint inside the shape was cleaner than the paint around it, a shade whiter, as if the tool had hung there long enough to keep the dust off and then gone.

Gage had already pulled the cloth off the nearest bench and set the flat wooden box on it. He didn't look round. His hands were in the matchsticks before the lid was off.

Ben stood in the doorway with his right hand flat against his hip, over a pocket. The facility greys had no pocket there. He pressed anyway, and found cloth, and took the hand away, and a few breaths later it went back.

She'd seen him do it four times since the briefing room. She didn't say anything. She filed it.

---

The shelf was at the end, past the last bench, where the light from the chain lamps ran out.

Books. Forty, maybe, lying on their sides because the shelf was too short to stand them, spines out. Grey cloth over board, gone brown at the corners. A number stamped low on each spine in ink that had bled a little into the weave. The edges of the pages had been cut all at once, so they lay flush, and the top edge had a blue stripe painted across it.

Camilla stopped walking.

She knew those spines. She had learned to read off them, sitting on the floor of the shack with the boards up and Pappy's lamp turned low so Rita wouldn't see the oil going. The same grey cloth. The same bleeding stamp. The same blue stripe across the top of the pages that came off on your thumb if you were sweating. She'd thought the blue was something Pappy had done himself, to mark which ones were his.

She pulled one. It came out heavy and opened on its own to a page it had been opened to before: a pump section, cut in half, every part lettered. The paper smelled of the same thing the floorboards had smelled of.

*Salvage*, Pappy had said, when she'd asked where. *Junkyard. Where else.*

She held it a while. Then she slid it back into its gap and squared the spine to the others with one finger, so that nobody looking would know it had been out.

Behind her, Gage said, "Cami. Does this look right to you."

It did. She came back and moved one splinter anyway.

---

The engineer came in without knocking, which she decided she liked, and stood at the end of her bench with his hands behind his back.

The pencil stubs were still behind his ears, sharpened to nothing. He'd brought no case this time. No tools. He looked at the arm lying on the cloth in front of her and then at her face.

"You said down to the last wire," he said. "There is no wire."

"Then show me what there is."

"I will not touch it. If I touch it while it is open it will learn my hand, a little, and you do not want that." He nodded at the forearm. "A thumb's width below the elbow, on the inside, and the same on the outside. Both at once."

She looked at the two places. Then she looked at her good hand, which was one hand.

"It won't open for one," he said. "That is on purpose."

So she did it with the other. She laid the metal arm on the bench, palm up, and brought the metal fingers round, slow, until the thumb sat on the inside of its own forearm and the first finger on the outside, and then she thought about pressing, and half a breath later it pressed.

A seam she hadn't been able to find came open along the whole forearm with a sound like a cold lid lifting. The cover didn't fall. It rose a hair and sat there, and she lifted it off with her right hand and set it on the cloth, and looked in.

Nothing moved.

That was the first thing. She'd been braced for the inside of a machine: a tendon, a rod, something that pulled when she pulled. The forearm was a trough of the same grey metal, and lying along its floor, where the long bones would have been, was something she had to lean in to see at all. A web. Finer than hair, finer than the seams on the fingers, grey on grey, so fine that she saw it first as a change in the way the light sat and only afterward as lines. It lay in the trough the way frost lies on a window: all over, touching everything, weighing nothing. Where the arm met her, up under the shoulder cup, it gathered and ran in, thickening into something she couldn't see the end of.

It lay exactly where her nerves had been. She knew the map. She'd read it in a grey book with a blue stripe.

"Close the hand," the engineer said.

She did. The fingers closed on the bench, late. The web didn't move. Nothing in it moved. Only, for the length of the movement, the grey of it went a shade darker along one line and then paled again, the way a wet mark pales as it dries.

"It's not pulling anything," she said.

"No."

"So what's moving the fingers?"

"The fingers are. Each joint has its own small strength, and takes its orders from that." He tipped his head at the web. "That does not move. That listens. Your shoulder speaks, in the old language, and this hears it and tells the hand what was said. It is the ear of the thing. The arm is only the arm."

She put her face closer. Her breath didn't stir it. She wanted to touch it worse than she had wanted to touch anything since the copper lamp, and she didn't, because he'd said *learn*, and she understood what that meant now in a way she hadn't an hour ago. The thing in the trough was taking notes.

"Where does it decide?"

"Nowhere you would call a place. Everywhere along it, a little." He paused. "That was your question, in the treatment room. I did not have an answer you would have accepted. I am not sure I have one now."

She followed the web up with her eyes to the shoulder cup, where it gathered, and there, on the inside curve of the metal, just before the point where the cup met the seal and became part of her, there was a mark.

Pressed in, not painted. A circle, and inside the circle something that had once been a shape. It had been rubbed almost flat. Hands, or years. She tilted her head and the lamp caught it: a line, maybe two, a curve going the wrong way for any letter she knew. She tried it as a number and it wasn't one. She tried it upside down.

"Who made this?"

"This ring."

"Not the arm. This." She put the tip of her good finger on the bench beside the stamp, not on it. "That's a maker's mark. It's older than the metal round it. You pressed a new arm round an old mark."

The engineer didn't move for a count of three.

"The listening core is older than this ring's charter," he said. "We copied it. We did not invent it. The copies are very good; that is as much as I will claim for us."

"Who did invent it?"

"I asked that, once. I was told the ring found it and kept it, as the ring keeps a great many things. I was not told where it found it." He looked at the stamp. "I have never been able to read that either."

She looked at the circle until the lamp swam. Then she picked up the cover.

It went on the way it had come off: lay it along the trough, think about the two points, and press, and the seam closed itself with the same cold lid sound and was gone. She ran her thumbnail down where it had been and felt nothing. She opened the hand and closed it. Half a breath. She did it again and the half was shorter, and she caught the engineer watching the gap too, and counting.

"Right," he said, and she heard it was the highest thing he had.

---

Gage had the Lake Wall up. Three splinters, and the middle one split with his thumbnail, and he was sitting with both hands flat on the bench on either side of it, not touching.

She came and stood at his shoulder. The clay was still damp. The turbine feeds were two matchsticks laid close together down the right side, and he'd scored a line across both of them with a fingernail where the valves would sit.

"They can shut those from here," she said. "That's what we didn't have. That's all we didn't have."

"That's all." He didn't look up. "Nineteen nights. Eighteen, by the time we sleep."

"And somebody at the wall. To say when."

The split splinter sat in the row like a bad tooth. He'd worked the run from the Hub to that junction. He'd told her once he could walk it in the dark by the props, counting, left hand on the rock. Ben had never been past the Hub. Otto was two levels up in a tank of blue.

She didn't say it. He didn't either. His right hand had started to close on the bench and he watched it and made it open, and she laid her metal hand flat on the wood beside his, where he'd see it, and left it there until the wrist stopped lagging.

"I'm going to go ask about the switch," she said.

"Ask him about the flowers while you're there."

---

The engineer had told her the way without her having to ask twice: up one level, along the curve until the floor changed from grey to something with a grain in it, then a door with no number. She heard the music before she saw the door.

Strings, and something under them like a slow heart. It came through the wood.

The door opened before she knocked.

Shelves. She'd known there would be and it still stopped her on the threshold. Floor to ceiling on three walls, and on every shelf the painted figures, ranged by height, each face smaller than the last and each one looking at a point a little to the left of her. Some wore the gold. Some wore the green-grey, the red and black. Some wore nothing she could name, long coats, robes, hats like chimneys, old-world shapes out of Pappy's stories, and she caught herself trying to count them and stopped at a hundred and eleven because the room went on round a corner.

Flitzberg sat at a long table with his back to her, drawing. Paper everywhere, clean and white, and a drawing on each sheet, and his hand moving over the one in front of him in short quick strokes. His own face, when he turned. She checked the jaw.

"Camilla." He set his pencil down parallel to the paper's edge. "You have been two hours in the workshop and you have not yet opened a bench. I had a wager with myself."

"I opened my arm."

"Ah." He turned his chair to face her. "Then you have come about the switch."

"Who can turn it off?"

"The medic. The engineer. Myself." He said them like items on a list he had made ready. "No one above this ring. Not because they would not wish to, but because they do not know it exists, and a thing that is not known cannot be commanded. You asked the medic, and she gave you two names and one door she would not swear to. I am the door. There is no one behind me."

"That's three people who can switch me off."

"Yes."

"I don't like three."

"No. I would not, either." He folded his hands. "I will not tell you to trust it. You have been inside it now, and you will go inside it again, and when you have taken it apart to the last wire, you will know whether I lied. That is the only proof I have to offer, and it is the only kind you would accept."

She stood in the door and let that sit. The music went on. The slow heart under the strings came round again.

"The faces," she said. "Pappy's. Gerald's. Why those?"

Flitzberg looked at her for a long count. Then he reached to the shelf beside the table without rising, and took down one of the figures: a man in a miner's coat, painted grey with soot, the face lined and tired and kind. He turned it in his fingers and found the seam at the waist and twisted, and the top came off, and inside was the same man, smaller, with the same face and younger. He set the two halves on the table and twisted the inner one, and there was a third, smaller again, and the paint on its face had been laid on with a brush of one hair.

He didn't open the third.

"This office keeps the faces of the people it owes," he said. "I do not wear them to frighten. I wear them so that I do not forget whose debt I am carrying when I walk into a room. Your grandfather is one. The man with the leg is another."

Her mouth had gone dry. She wet it.

"What did you owe Pappy?"

"A great deal, and we paid him nothing he would have called payment." He looked at the three figures in a row. "That is a longer account than I can give you tonight, and a part of it is not mine to give at all. I will say this much, since you have made me promise: it is why the books on that shelf two levels down will have looked familiar to you."

She did not let her face do anything. She had learned that at a dinner table.

"You open the little one too?"

"No." He began to fit the figures back together, the second into the first. "There is a point in every set past which I do not go. One must leave something closed."

"Not in my arm," Camilla said. "Not anywhere I'm standing. You've got sixty tools hanging in sixty-one shapes down there, and I'm going to find out what's missing, and then I'm going to find out why, and when I'm done with that I'm going to find out what you owed my grandfather and whether you've paid it yet." She put her hand on the door frame. The metal one. It settled half a breath after her face did, and she let it. "Keep your own face on when you talk to me. I'll know it from the jaw."

She went before he could answer. The music followed her halfway down the curve and then the floor changed under her feet and it was gone.

---

Ben had found the window.

It was in the workshop, at the far end past the shelf of grey books, a panel in the wall at head height that she'd taken for a plate over a vent. The engineer had opened it for him; he was standing a few paces back with his hands behind him, the way he'd stood at her bench, watching Ben and not the wall.

The panel showed the gates.

She knew them before she knew what she was looking at. The iron, the rust runs down it, the little guard box with the lamp that never went out. Daylight up there, grey and flat, a wind pulling the dust along the ground in low sheets. It was afternoon above. She hadn't known that. There were people along the fence as there always were, a line of them, shawls over their heads, waiting the way a stone waits.

Gretchen stood apart from the line.

Small, a sack over one shoulder, both arms folded tight across her, standing with her feet set the way Ben set his when he didn't want to be moved. Her face was turned to the tunnel mouth and didn't turn from it. Someone passed behind her and spoke, and she shook her head without looking, and the person went on.

Ben's hand was on the wall beside the panel, not on it. He slid down the wall until he was sitting on the floor with his knees up and his back against it, and he stayed there, looking up at her, his mouth a little open as if he had started to say her name and run out of breath.

Camilla looked away from him, because he hadn't given her leave to look.

She scanned the line instead. Left to right, each shawl, each bent back, the way she'd read the ledgers on the briefing room wall. Twenty-three along the fence. A woman with a baby. Two old men sharing a lamp. She went back along it right to left, slower.

Rita wasn't there.

She stood and looked at the place at the end of the fence where Rita would have been, with a wrench held across her like a bar, and the place was dust and a stretch of iron. Rita had stood at those gates for Thorton for three days and stopped. She had stood there for Gage once and he had come out. Camilla didn't know whether an empty fence meant a woman who had given up, or a woman who was sitting in a shack with the lamp turned low to save the oil, keeping it burning past dark. She didn't know which she'd have chosen for her. She found that she couldn't work it out, and she had never not been able to work a thing out, and her metal hand had closed at her side without her.

"Can we send anything up," Ben said from the floor. He wasn't asking her.

"No," the engineer said. "I am sorry. Nothing goes up that the ground could read."

Ben nodded. He pressed his right hand flat to his hip, over the pocket that wasn't there, and left it.

On the wall, Gretchen shifted the sack to her other shoulder and went on standing.

---

The engineer closed the panel when Ben let him. Then he left, and it was the three of them and the lamps on their chains, and Gage's hands moving in the matchsticks at the far end without a sound.

Camilla went along the painted wall until she found what she wanted. A horseshoe magnet, red paint flaking off its arms, hanging in a horseshoe shape. Beside the lathe, a tin of needles with a lid you had to pry. In the drawer under the first bench, a cork, a stub of one, with a knife mark through it where someone had cut a slice and never come back for the rest. The dish she took from under the drill press: a shallow tin lid, grey with old oil, and she wiped it with the hem of her shirt and filled it at the tap in the corner, which ran brown for a count of five and then ran clear.

She sat on the bench with the dish beside her and the magnet in her lap.

She held the needle in the fingers of her good hand and drew it along one arm of the magnet, point to eye, one direction only, and lifted it clear, and brought it back round, and drew it again. She counted. Pappy had said fifty; she'd done it with a nail at nine years old and got a nail that would pick up filings and nothing else. Twenty. Thirty. The metal hand held the magnet still in her lap, and she was aware of it doing that, its own small strength in each finger, taking orders from an ear that lay in it like frost. Forty. Fifty.

She cut a slice off the cork with the knife from the drawer, a round the thickness of her thumbnail, and pushed the needle through it sideways so it lay across the top like a bar, and set the cork on the water.

It turned. Slowly at first, and then with purpose, and overshot, and came back.

"Ben."

He got up off the floor. He came and stood over the dish with his hands at his sides, and she saw him understand what it was before he was all the way across the room, because his step changed.

"It only knows one direction," she said. "I don't know which one. There's no north down here that I'd trust. But it knows, and it'll keep knowing, and if you turn the dish it'll turn back."

She lifted the dish in both hands, the metal one under it, steady, and held it out.

He took it. The water trembled and the cork rode it out. He looked down into the lid for a long time, and his thumb moved along the tin rim, back and forth, a gesture so worn it went on without him.

"Yeah," he said.

That was all. He carried it to the bench by the door and set it down where the lamp fell on it, and sat beside it with his shoulder to the wall, and didn't press his hand to his hip. At the far end, Gage set the Junkyard on the shelf, high and dry, and sat back.

Camilla stayed where she was. From there she could see the sixty-one shapes on the wall, and the one that had nothing in it, and the grey spines in the dark past the lamps, and the dish.

The cork turned a last few degrees. The needle swung past the place it wanted, and came back, and swung a smaller way past, and came back, and settled.
