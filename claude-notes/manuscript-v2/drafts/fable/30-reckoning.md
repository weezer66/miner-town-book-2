# Chapter 30: Reckoning

*Flitzberg's eyes moved from Gage to Camilla to Ben to Otto. Four faces. Three looking back.*

— Book 1, Chapter 25: Don't Do It

## Now: Reckoning

The lift came down wet.

Camilla heard it before the door opened, a long sigh in the shaft, and then the door drew back and lake water ran out over the sill in a thin brown tongue and stopped at her boots. The light inside was the same white light as everywhere. The floor of the car was an inch deep in it, and the inch was moving, still finding its level, grit turning slowly in it.

The grey-haired medic came out first, backwards, with one end of a board.

Camilla had stood at this door since the line went quiet. She'd stood here while the ring's people went up past her with ropes and a board and the engineer carrying lamps, and while the lift sat at the top of the shaft for longer than any shift change she'd ever counted. Ben had the line. Ben would say it if there was anything to say. Her good hand was in her pocket with the splinter, and she'd held it so long the crack had printed itself on her palm.

They set the board down on the plates. Water came off it on all sides.

Gage lay on his back with his eyes open. Someone had cut his left trouser leg off at the thigh and the leg under it was wrong, swollen dark from the knee down and lying a little away from the other, and a splint of two lamp poles had been strapped along it with the straps off somebody's lamp. The coal was back in his clothes. The deep had put it there in an hour, all the grey the ring's laundry had washed out of him, black mud in the seams of his shirt and in the scar on his brow and in the creases of his open hands.

"The leg is held," the medic said, to Camilla, before Camilla could ask. "He has not lost it. What he has lost of it I cannot tell you tonight. The back I like better than the leg." She didn't lower her voice for him. "He has been conscious throughout. He counted the lift floors aloud. There are none marked."

"Twenty-two," Gage said, to the ceiling.

Then his head turned on the board and he found her.

He looked at her for a while. His eyes went to her face and then to her metal arm and then back, checking that both had made it. The water had washed the lamp-black off round his eyes and left it everywhere else, so that he looked at her out of a paler mask.

"Clean?" he said.

It came out dry. He'd meant it dry. She could hear the fourth run in it, the model on the bench, his finger lifting off the split splinter, and she could hear the question under the question, which was whether she was going to tell him.

Flitzberg had never lied to them. The ring's people had never lied to them. She wasn't going to start on her brother on a wet board.

"No," she said. "Not clean. It worked."

He waited.

"The wall went. The Hub's under. The turbine floor, the silver, the pods, all of it, the way you drew it." She made herself say the next part at the same speed. "And the pump house. It went over the door and the lamp went out under the water and it's gone, Gage. Miner Town's ration pump. There'll be no water in the row tomorrow that didn't come out of a barrel."

The medic's hands kept moving on the straps. Somewhere up the shaft, the lift cable ticked as it cooled.

Gage shut his eyes. He opened them again.

"I saw it," he said. "Through the gap. I watched it go." His throat worked. "It wasn't on the model."

"No."

"Nothing was on the model but the line."

"I know."

She crouched, which put her metal shoulder level with his face. Then she took her good hand out of her pocket, and the splinter with it, and she laid the splinter in his right palm where it lay open on the wet board, and put her fingers down over it.

His hand didn't close at once. It lay under hers, big and cold and open, the way he'd kept it for weeks so nobody here would mistake it for anything else. Then the fingers came up round hers, one at a time, the little finger last, and shut, and held, and she felt the splinter's edge between their two palms.

"Rita's kettle," he said.

"I know."

"Say it again. The true one."

"It worked," Camilla said. "And it's not clean. Both. I'm not taking either one back to make it easier."

Something moved in his face under the black. She'd heard those words said once, over her own stump, by a man with her brother's hand-print on his throat, and she hadn't known then that she'd keep them.

"Lift him," the medic said. "Now, please. His sister may hold on. Nobody is going to make her let go."

They carried him down the corridor, and the lights woke ahead of the board, a step beyond it each time, and Camilla went beside it with her good hand shut in his and the metal one swinging at her side, and water dripped off the board's edge and left a line behind them on the white floor all the way to the infirmary door.

---

Two hours after first bell, Ravi opened the door of Room Four, and this time there was someone in the chair.

Kaan sat with his back to the wired glass and his hands on his knees. The nick through his left eyebrow was white in the policy corridor's light. He didn't stand. His eyes came onto Ravi's coat, where the folio lay flat against his chest under his crossed arm, and then up to Ravi's face, and he waited, as he'd waited in a doorway with his heel against a door.

His terminal had ticked against his wrist all night: a water event in Sector One, extent unknown; the Hub lift suspended; Kubera's allocation council to sit at the sixth bell on ration transport. None of it signed with two letters. He had no idea what had.

He took the folio out of his coat.

"The custody ledger says I'd return this the next morning," he said. "That was nine mornings ago. The clerk with the paper-clip chain will have drawn a line under my name by now in red." He held it out. "You said bring what I took. I'm bringing it. I want to give it to the person who's going to read it, and I want to be there when she does."

Kaan took the folio in both hands. He stood, and went to the door, and held it, the heel of his boot against the wood.

Three flights down into Ashoka's old stone, and a door with *dead room* painted on it in plain white. Ravi had never been anywhere in this city that the city couldn't follow him into, and he felt it the second the locker drawer took his terminal with its soft sigh: the silence on his wrist, like a hand lifted off.

She was standing at the table with her back to him, in grey-blue. A red crease across her forehead where she'd leant it against glass. Both wrists bare.

"Monitor Nathan," he said.

"Analyst." Elara turned. She looked tired in a way he recognized from the Initiation watch, the third day of it, when they'd all stopped pretending to sleep. "You logged an objection, on the first day. I've thought about it a lot since."

"So have I."

Kaan laid the folio on the table and stepped back to the corner beside the locker, and Ravi watched Elara's eyes lose him there, and find him again.

"Third page," Ravi said. "The underlined line. Don't read the rest; I was nineteen and I used too many words."

She turned to it. He watched her find the line. The pencil under it had gone grey and furred; he'd gone over it so many nights that it looked like a seam.

"Read it out," he said. "Please. I've only ever read it to myself."

"*Continuity Reserve transfer, Kubera to Chanakya, received fourteenth of the month. Purpose: survey of archive wing, preliminary. Founder-tier channel. Signing authority given as two letters, O.G., which match no registry held by this house. Analyst recommends the transfer be held pending identification of signatory.*" She stopped. "Dated three years ago."

"Three years and a season. First year, first quarter. I'd been at my desk eleven weeks." He kept his hands where she could see them, flat on the table edge. "I flagged it because the signature was wrong, and because the money was for a survey of the archive wing, and nobody had asked for a survey of the archive wing. My section head sent it up. It came back in a sealed case, findings restricted above my grade, and nobody ever told me what it meant. I've carried the number three years. I thought it was a mistake I'd made."

Elara didn't look up from the page.

"A survey of the archive wing," she said. "For the Annex."

"For the Annex that Kubera refused for eleven years and then granted in a week, with a petition under it that has my name on nine pages I didn't write." His voice stayed level. He'd practised that for three years too. "The hand didn't start with Daren. Somebody wore the band three years ago, before the box was ever opened for him, and spent Kubera's money on Chanakya with it, and a first-year analyst flagged it, and the flag was sealed. That's what's in the case above my grade. Who sat on it."

Her eyes came up.

"If this goes on a table," she said, "the Annex comes apart. The nine pages. Everything they've put your name on."

"Yes."

"They'll say you knew."

"I wrote that the record should say who sat on it." He heard his own voice and found it came out at twenty and not at nineteen, which was something. "It was me. For three years, in a coat pocket. And it was my house, above me, in a sealed case. The record can say both. I'd rather it said both than said neither."

He reached into his coat.

The black king came out warm from his pocket, dark stone, the pale seam through its base, one shoulder of the crown worn smooth under three years of his thumb. He'd carried it to a conservatory and a review console and a bench with a short leg. He set it on the table, beside the folio, upright, facing nothing.

"That came to me the day somebody stopped a checkmate on my behalf," he said. "I kept it to mean I hadn't decided. I've decided."

Elara looked at the king for a while. Then she closed the folio over the third page, and laid her bare hand flat on the grey sleeve.

"It goes down with me," she said. "To the table. Today. And you don't come, because the room is one no one can be brought to, and I'd be lying if I said I knew the way myself." She picked the king up and held it out to him. "Keep this. You'll want something in your hand this afternoon when the Annex clerks come to your desk."

He took it. In the corner, Kaan came off the wall, crossed the room, and held out his right hand, palm open, the first time.

Ravi shook it. The hand was dry and hard, and it let go first.

---

The table had not risen.

Elara came down to it by the blind car and the brass-plated lift, with the folio inside her jacket and Anandi's cloth left folded over her terminal on the shelf upstairs, and the woman in grey took her, this time, through the door on the left.

The lamp over the round table had burned all night and into the day, and the clean circle it threw on the pale floor had a smell now, hot glass and old bread. Someone had brought bread. The crusts lay on a board at Flitzberg's elbow, and nobody had touched them since.

The four Grandmasters sat where they had sat. The Arthashastri, upright in green, had taken his cuffs off and laid them on the stone beside his chair. The Dhammapala's hands were on her knees, the fingers curled. The Ancient One had not changed in any way Elara could see. The Master of the Reserve sat with his bare hands on the wood and his rings in their row, every seal shut.

In front of him lay the band. Black, dull, drinking the lamp. Beside it Baju's notebook, its cover gone soft as cloth, the pencil stub still pushed down the spine.

Elara laid the folio down beside the notebook and opened it to the third page.

She went back to stand at the wall where the woman in grey had put her, between the two doors, and found Rohan already there in his red-black with no insignia, his right hand hanging open at his side, the glue on the knuckle cracked white. He didn't look at her. He moved half a step along the wall so that she had the place beside him, and that was all.

Across the room, by the far door, Daren stood in his buttoned black with his right wrist bare. Pale, ridged, unfinished. He'd stood all night. There was a chair beside him and he hadn't used it.

The Arthashastri read the third page without touching the folio. His lips moved once on two letters.

"Three years," he said. "Before the box was opened for this officer." He looked at Daren, and then, as Elara hadn't yet seen him do all night, at the Master. "A first-year analyst of my house flagged this, and the finding was sealed from above. I was not told."

"Your house sealed it," the Master said. "Not mine. Kubera sends money. Chanakya decides what to do with questions about it."

"On whose instruction?"

The Master didn't answer. He looked at the folio as he had looked at the band last night, and said nothing, and the nothing sat in the clean circle of lamplight with everything else.

The Dhammapala had it said once more, in order, "in a room that keeps what it hears." The register slip, Room 4-12, never released. Nayan's four columns, unrolled across the far side of the table from a tube, the hand's three gifts marked in pencil and the forty-day line in small squares. The notebook, with the hole worn through its last page. The band. The folio, with its grey furred line three years old. The paper road, end to end, under one lamp.

Then Daren spoke, from the far door, without being asked, as he'd read figures at THRESHOLD: flat, in order, nothing added. The first night. The ledger. The lamps and the bottle. Whose purse. The fourth-level corridor and what his own hand had been afraid of. A ridge on an old wrist, a finger above where gold sits.

"I wore it," he said at the end. "He wore it first. I have no paper for that. The analyst has."

The room held it. Elara felt Rohan's weight change beside her, onto the balls of his feet, and stay there.

And then nobody moved.

The Dhammapala spoke first. "Ashoka holds this door. Ashoka will hold it. My house does not act on a Grandmaster of another house alone. It has never done so, and it will not begin tonight on a paper and a struck officer's word, however true. The four of us act, or none."

"Kubera's council will not meet on this," the Master said. He said it as a fact about weather. "It will meet at the sixth bell on ration water for Sector One, which is a matter this room has made, and it will not unseat the Master of the Reserve on the word of an officer it struck and a case its own house closed. I know my council. I have sat in it for forty years."

"Drona will say what is true," the Ancient One said.

"Drona," said the Arthashastri, very evenly, "has watched every house's instruments for a century, and told none of us. Drona will forgive me if I weigh its word tonight at what it has been worth." He turned his cuffs over on the stone with one finger. "My house will read this folio. My house will ask why it sealed it. That is what Chanakya will do, and I will not promise you more than I can carry up that lift."

"And this office," said Flitzberg, "does not vote. It never has. It keeps, and it answers, and it has answered tonight more than in the century before it." He looked at the bread, and at the Master. "Everyone at this table knows what this room is now. That is the price this office has paid, and it is not a small one."

Four houses. One table. Nobody willing to be the hand that moved alone against a hand that had moved alone for three years. Elara stood against the wall and watched the balance she'd been taught at twelve fail to do anything at all.

The Master of the Reserve reached out and took up his first ring.

He put it on. Then the second. He did it slowly, one at a time, as he'd taken them off, and each one closed on its finger with a small sound in the quiet room, a seal shutting. When the last was on he stood, an old man in muted gold, and looked round the table at the other three as he had when he sat down.

"I came without escort," he said. "I will go without it."

He walked to the far door, the one nobody opened for him. Daren was standing beside it. The Master didn't look at him, or at the band, or the folio. He went through, and the door shut with its soft sound. Every person in the room knew what he was, and he was going up to a council at the sixth bell as Master of the Reserve, to decide how much water Miner Town would get.

Rohan moved.

He didn't cross the room. He took one step off the wall, toward the far door, and Daren, who had been looking at the shut door, turned and saw him come, and didn't move either way.

Baju's commander, and the man whose hand had killed him. Four body-lengths of clean stone floor. Elara didn't put herself in the way. She'd done that last night. She stood where she was and let him have the step.

"His name was Baju," Rohan said. "He had a mechanical counter he clicked when he was thinking. He hated lifts. He'd been in my unit six years." His voice didn't rise at all. "You'll say it to his people. I'll take you to them, and you'll stand there, and you'll say it, and you won't say *afraid*, because they don't need to carry that too."

Daren's bare wrist turned, a little, toward the room.

"Yes," he said.

Rohan stepped back to the wall. His right hand was still open. It stayed open.

The Dhammapala rose. "Kubera has struck this officer from its rolls, and he has stood in my house's custody since he laid that thing on the table. Ashoka will keep him. He will be a witness for as long as there is anyone to witness to." She said it to the room and to Daren both, and Daren bowed his head to it and did not sit down.

The Ancient One had not moved. Elara went to her.

She didn't know she was going to until she was standing at the old woman's chair, in the light, with the four Grandmasters' silence round her and her bare wrists at her sides. The Ancient One looked up.

"The map," Elara said. "The one on your table with the stones. You told me what was in the blank was something you'd sworn never to name, and I didn't ask again. I'm standing in it. I've been standing in it since last night." She kept her voice low, and it held. "What's it called?"

The Ancient One looked at her for a long time.

"Patal Loka," she said. "The world beneath. You are the first Drona monitor in a hundred years to be told that word by a Grandmaster, and you will be the last for a while." Her hands stayed folded in the shawl. "You asked me once why you and not one of us. Here is a better answer than the one I gave. We swore not to look. You did not swear. Go up, Monitor. Watch the ground. Nobody else is going to."

---

The band came to the workshop in a cloth.

Flitzberg carried it in himself, in his own face, with his collar still buttoned to the top and a plain square of grey linen folded over whatever he held, as if he'd caged a small animal for the night. He set it on the end of Camilla's bench, well away from the lathe, and stepped back from it, and only then unfolded the cloth.

Black. Dull. A thick ring of something that wasn't glass and wasn't stone, with a seam she couldn't see and a cold coming off it she could feel on the back of her good hand from a foot away.

"The table could not agree who should hold it," Flitzberg said. "Kubera's Master walked out before the question was put. Ashoka would not take it into a house of guardians. Chanakya does not keep relics. The Ancient One said that Drona had kept enough." He folded the cloth into a square. "They agreed that no one should wear it. I offered this office. No one objected, which in that room is consent."

"Is it dead?"

"It has been de-keyed, by a method I did not know existed and the Ancient One did. The Master's seals are closed to it. It should hear nothing." He looked at it. "I am not going to tell you it is dead. I do not know."

She looked at the band. Then she laid her left arm on the bench beside it, palm up, and let the sleeve ride back off the forearm, where the plate slid aside and the web lay under its cover, dark lines in a grey field, waiting for her shoulder to say something.

Two halves. One that listened a beat behind, and learned you. One that listened a heartbeat ahead, and ran on what you were afraid of. Somebody a century ago had drawn both on one sheet of paper and the sheet had been torn in half, and here they were on a bench in a room under the mountain, a finger apart.

The arm's fingers curled by themselves, late, toward the band, and she let them stop where they stopped.

"Who's allowed to switch this one off?" she said.

"No one, now. That is the point of it." He paused. "You will be the first to look inside it who was not its maker. I would ask you to write down what you find. This office writes nothing. I am asking anyway."

"I'll write it." She didn't look up. "I don't work with anything I'm not allowed to take apart."

"I know. That is why it is here."

On the shelf above the bench, past the lathe, where the lamplight ran out, the box sat on its bed of rag with the lid open.

They'd found it in the Hub wash at the foot of the drain sill, the engineer said, wedged under a prop with the strap still buckled. The clay had gone back to mud and run out through the seams. The coil was green along one side. The card had come away from its pins and lay in the bottom of the box with the ink lifted off it in a grey wash, and there was one mark still readable on it, near the top: a short hard stroke, the kind the needle made for rock.

It had worked. Once, on the one night it needed to. Someone had still had to carry it in.

She picked up the smallest driver from the rack, the one she'd ground herself from a nail, and looked at the band's seamless black, and set the driver down again, point toward it, and left it there for the morning.

---

The infirmary lamp had been turned down to its night setting, and nobody had gone to bed.

Gage lay in the long bed with the left leg splinted from hip to ankle in something white and hard the ring's medics had poured on warm and let set. The bed had been made for a ring's man and he overhung the end of it. His hands lay open on the blanket, one on each side of him. The splinter was under the right one. She could see the end of it.

Otto stood at the foot of the bed.

He'd been standing there since she came in, and he hadn't moved, and the chair the medic had set for him was beside his knee with nothing in it. His arms hung loose. His eyes were on Gage, and they stayed there, open, and his chest went up and down so slowly she'd checked twice.

The door opened and Ben came in from the screens.

He had the look he got off the headset man's wall, grey round the eyes, his lips still moving on something. He sat down on the edge of the second bed, the empty one, with his hands on his knees, and looked at the three of them, and let his breath out.

"Queue's started," he said. "At the standpipe by the gates. It's not light yet and there's forty in it. Barrels coming down from Trinity on the lift road, the big grey ones." He rubbed his face. "It's going to be long, Cami. Weeks, maybe. It's going to be the queue."

"I know."

"I counted past that pump house every night because nobody was ever in it." He looked at his hands. "I'm not going to say it was fine."

"Nobody's asking you to," Gage said, from the bed.

Ben looked at him. Then he said the other thing, the one he'd come down the corridor with, and his voice changed on it.

"She's there. Gretchen. Same patch, scarf on. She's second in the queue." He swallowed. "And there's a monitor going to the gates at first bell. Drona. The one who sat with me last night. Her name's been on us since the Initiation, on the record, so she can carry word and nobody can stop her. She asked Flitzberg what she was allowed to say. He said, *the truth, Monitor, and nothing short of it.*" He stopped. "Gretch is going to hear my name from a Trinity girl in grey-blue with no band on. I don't know what she'll do."

"Sit down," Otto said. "In the dirt, probably. Then get up."

Ben laughed, one short wet sound, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"And the crystal," Camilla said.

"Blue. The last quarter. Steady." Ben nodded at nothing. "Hasn't moved since he woke. I stood in front of it on the way here and it didn't do anything. It's just blue."

Nobody said what that meant. Nobody could check it.

Camilla stood where she was, at the head of the bed with her metal hand on the rail, and looked at them.

Gage, in the bed, his leg in its white shell, his hands open on the blanket, his eyes open too. Looking at her.

Otto, at the foot, very still, the chair beside him empty. Looking at her.

Ben, on the edge of the other bed, grey round the eyes, his hand come to rest on his hip over nothing. Looking at her.

Her.

She counted them, because counting was what she did when nothing else would hold still, and it came out at four, and she went round again to be sure, and it came out at four again, and every one of them was looking back.

Nobody said anything. The lamp hummed on its night setting.

On the screen in the corner, which Ben had left on, the ring's grey picture of the city's platforms turned over slowly, one and the next and the next. On one of them, high up, a lit window, and in it a figure in grey-blue standing with something small in her hand, a strap and a dark face, not buckling it on. She was looking down. The picture turned over and showed the gates, and the queue, and a thin girl in a brown coat second in it, and turned over again.

Gage's right hand opened a little further on the blanket, and the split splinter lay in the middle of his palm where she could see all of it.

He left it there. So did she.
