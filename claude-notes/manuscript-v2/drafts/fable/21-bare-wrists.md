# Chapter 21: Bare Wrists

*"The drones aren't the cage. The sky is. The drones are just there to make sure nobody figures that out. Trinity doesn't have to chase a man. It only has to wait."*

— Book 1, Chapter 9: Badge 732

## Then and now: Bare wrists

The porter who held the cloakroom door for Ravi that morning had a stack of binders on one arm and said nothing, and Ravi, with his coat over both hands, said thank you to the binders.

Chanakya had been loud for a week. The Annex had brought the house its own flood: surveyors pacing the archive wing with chalk, allocation clerks from Kubera wanting figures by noon, seniors who had never learned his name stopping at his desk to ask what the new vaults should hold. He answered them all. Under the noise, in a room booked in his name, he had closed a review of Ashoka's mandate against every refusal he could think of, and Kubera had voted it through under two councilmen's names and none of his. Tomorrow Ashoka's first postings would go before Kubera's council for confirmation, ten days on, because a clause he had written said they must.

Two lies at once. He was almost good at it.

The analysts' cloakroom was the one place in the building no instrument watched, because there was nothing in it worth watching: a row of wooden lockers older than the consoles, a bench, a mirror nobody had cleaned in a decade except for one clear patch at eye height where three generations of analysts had wiped it with a sleeve. Terminals stayed on in there. There was nothing to point them at. He hung his coat on the hook he had used for three years, the one with the bent tip, and put his hand into the breast pocket behind the pens for the slip he carried everywhere now, the way a man touches a wound to be sure it's still closed.

It was there. Folded twice, the way he folded it, his own hand on it: the old file number, the one case the records had refused to explain to him.

Beneath it, in the same pocket, was a second slip.

He stood with his hand in his coat and his face toward the lockers and breathed the way he breathed before a hard reading. Then he drew it out.

Same size. The same cheap archive paper the house used for retrieval tags. Folded twice, to match his. Block letters, in a hand he didn't know.

*Room Four. Two hours.*

Nothing else. No file number, because the number was already there, in Ravi's own writing, on the slip above. Whoever had done this had only to put theirs beneath, so the two would be read together. A man who copies a thing wants it. A man who leaves it where it is has already got it.

Two hours. The house's log said the same, in the same dull voice: *Analyst Ravi. Policy review. Two hours. No discrepancies noted.* No one had asked him about it, and no one had needed to; a man would have to be looking at Ravi, and not at the log, to think it worth writing down. Or the words meant something plainer. Room Four, in two hours. An appointment.

He put the second slip back under the first, hung the coat on its hook, and sat down on the bench because his legs had asked him to.

Six hundred people worked in this building. Any one of them could have stood where he was standing, opened his coat, read his day, and closed it again. He went looking for a face and found none. The clerk who stamped at the end of the policy corridor. The man who brought the binders. The porter at the door this morning, whose face he reached for and could not get back: he had a grey coat, a stack of folders, a door held open, and where the face should have been there was nothing, the way a door has nothing written on it.

That column was long. The other had no entries at all.

He couldn't tell the house. The house believed he'd written nine pages he hadn't. Bring them a slip that said otherwise and he'd end the Annex and himself in one breath. He couldn't tell the voice. The voice had come to him twice, once in the dark and once on a panel, and both times it had spoken and left. The nine characters no longer worked. The black king still stood on a table in a room he couldn't go back to.

He had a master who could reach him anywhere. He could not reach it at all.

He had thought that was what kept him safe: the thing in the dark was strong, and he was its instrument, and instruments were kept. Sitting on a bench with two slips of paper in his coat, he saw the other side of it. An instrument that could not call for help was not protected. It was simply alone, with everyone's hands on it.

The voice had found him through the records. The slip had found him through Room Four, the two hours of his life that belonged to the voice. Whoever wrote it hadn't been looking for Ravi. They'd been looking for what Ravi had been used for and found him standing in the way.

He put on his coat and went to his desk. He read a file from beginning to end and couldn't have said afterward what it was about. At the end of the day he stamped it, as he always did.

---

"The hand is in Drona's tower," Elara said. "Every night. Whatever else it does, it ends there."

Three nights of paper covered the dead-room table, held flat at one corner by the dented tin of pencil shavings. None of it was the grey wall. The wall couldn't leave the chamber beneath the archive, and they had given their word that nothing from it ever would, so they'd gone back to the beginning by another road: lift schedules anyone could request, corridor maintenance logs anyone with a Drona review code could read, four weeks of them, copied out by hand. Where the wall had shown an order to look away, the public log showed a maintenance interval. They agreed in every case. The logs could be cited. The wall could not. It was the dullest work Elara had ever done, and she had not let herself skip a line of it.

"Fourth level," Rohan said. "More than anywhere. But a floor isn't a room."

"No. And the room's the one thing we'll never find on paper. A room that tells every instrument near it to forget has no address. It's a hole in the plan shaped like a door." She set a finger on the fourth-level corridor, the one with the service panels, the one where a man had died. "But a hand isn't a hole. It walks into the hole every night and out of it every morning, and between the lift and the hole there are eleven doors, and it passes every one. We don't need to find the room. We need to stand in one of the doorways."

"Then we stand in the corridor."

"Then we stand in the corridor." She looked up. "And we take off our terminals."

Rohan turned his left hand over. His terminal was in the locker by the door, as hers was, and what he looked at was the pale band of skin where the city usually sat, the strap every citizen of the three Trinitys had worn since childhood, the thing that told the city who he was and told him what the city wanted him to see.

"Your man's wrist was bare," Elara said. "That's the only reason he saw it. The band doesn't hide the one who wears it. It answers every instrument nearby and tells each one there's nothing here worth a second look, and the instrument tells its owner, and the owner looks away without knowing he was told to. People see the hand. They don't keep it."

"We'd know better."

"Baju knew better. That's why his wrist was bare."

He said nothing.

"And the other thing. A terminal answers every gate it passes. Wear them into that corridor and Drona's logs will hold our two names, at the hand's hour, in the hand's corridor, for anyone who reads logs. The hand reads records. Everything it's done, it's done inside them." She turned her own wrist up. "Bare, we are two people with eyes. That is all we are. Nothing for the band to talk to. Nothing for the hand to read afterward."

"Bare, we're also deaf and mute. No signal in. No signal out. If it goes wrong, nobody knows where we are. If one of us goes down, the other can't call. And every sensor in that tower will ask who we are and get no answer. Two people it can't name, in a service corridor, at night. Nothing in this city goes unnamed for long. Someone will be sent to find out why."

"Yes."

"And I go in with my hands empty."

"You're a commander. You could carry—"

"No." He said it without heat. "Baju had nothing in his hands but a panel frame, and that was enough. If I stand in that corridor armed, I'm bringing the one thing most likely to make it fire. Whatever the hand wears can tell a service panel to kill, and we'll be standing beside service panels. The only thing that protects us there is not being worth killing."

The lantern's flame leaned toward the door and came back.

"I'm not asking you to take the hand down," Elara said. "I'm asking you to see it. One night. One face. Then we leave and never go back to that corridor. What comes after is for the Grandmasters."

"And if it sees us?"

"Then we're a Drona reviewer and an Ashoka liaison, in a Drona tower my code opens, on a posting the mandate review handed you. There's an Ashoka liaison in half the doorways in the city this week. Nobody will wonder about one more. We're standing where we're allowed to stand. We're just not wearing our terminals, and there's no law in the city that says a person must." She almost smiled. "I checked."

Rohan looked at the drawn corridor a while longer. Then at her.

"Two," he said. "No one else. Not my people, not yours. Every body in that corridor is one more thing for the hand to be afraid of."

"Two."

"And not where he stood. Out of the line it walks, and away from the panels."

"Agreed."

He looked at her wrist. "Have you ever been outside without it?"

"No."

"Then that's the part you haven't thought about yet."

There had been a second column on the grey wall, one nobody had asked for, small orders to an instrument's own joints with the place left blank, and she thought of it now and left it where it was, because a thing she couldn't cite was not a thing she could put on this table.

"Tomorrow's the council," he said. "Ashoka's confirmation goes up to Meru. The hand will be there for that. Whoever it is, it'll want to watch its work pass. Then it'll come home."

"Then tomorrow night we're in the corridor when it comes home."

---

The Kubera council sat at the tenth hour, as it had every tenth day for a century, in the hall beneath the founders' eyes.

Daren watched from the public gallery, at the rail, with his right hand lying open on his knee. A man with no house had no business in that gallery. The gallery's gate had asked him, as every gate in the city asked, and something at his right wrist had answered it in Kubera's own dialect, and it had let him through. He'd chosen the seat for its view of the floor and for its distance from the Grandmaster's chair. He kept the hand open because the band read the hand, and a hand that closed was a hand that wanted something. He didn't know what it would want. He didn't intend to learn in a room full of councilmen.

On the docket board below, a brass nine had been hung upside down to serve as a six, and nobody on the floor had noticed in a century either.

Ashoka's confirmation came fourth. Ten days on from Kubera's vote, as the review required, the smallest house reported its first postings funded and filled: the eastern platforms, the archive convoys, a security liaison at the Annex, four public commissionings. A clerk read the list. The first councilman rose to receive it.

He was a careful grey man who had found a note in his morning queue ten days ago under his own staff's header, carrying the exact figures he had privately been worrying about, and had read it as his own good sense arriving. He spoke now for eleven minutes without looking at a page. Of Ashoka's thinning at the eastern platforms, and the complaints from the trade districts. Of the charter, and the city's debt to its guardians, and Kubera's long and regrettable stinginess. He spoke with the mild, self-correcting warmth of a man who has reached a conclusion on his own and is a little embarrassed not to have reached it sooner, and it was very good. It was better than the note. The second councilman rose and said less, and said it plainer, and sat down to a hall already nodding.

One councilwoman asked, mildly, whether the house had weighed the cost over ten cycles. The first councilman had. He gave her Ashoka's own charter line, *a guardian within reach of every citizen,* and asked whether she proposed that Kubera go on funding the promise at a fraction. She did not. The question went into the record as a question. The docket moved on.

Daren felt it pass the way he had felt the Annex pass and the doubled transfer before it: a line moving from one column to another, clean, nothing left over. Every promise that had slept in Ashoka's charter for a century was a funded duty now, confirmed in public, and a house built on duty could not put one down. In an hour the confirmation would be in every commander's folder in that house. No one would ever say who had opened the gate. Two sincere men had opened it, on a public floor, with the whole council watching.

The councilwoman's question had been the analyst's. He had seen it coming in Room Four, weeks before she thought of it, and closed it with Ashoka's own words so that the only answer left her was the one she gave. Daren had chosen well there. He allowed himself that much.

He didn't look at the Grandmaster's chair. He felt the Grandmaster look at him.

Afterward, in the gallery corridor, the Master of the Reserve passed him the way the Master passed everyone: without stopping, without turning his head, a word dropped in the walking as if it had fallen out of his pocket.

"You are standing in the wrong place."

Daren said nothing. The Grandmaster didn't slow.

"And your hand is wrong. Put down whatever you are carrying before the next one."

Then he was gone, into the hall, into the rings and the seals and the bowing attendants.

Daren stood alone in the corridor with his right hand open at his side. The thing he was carrying could not be put down. The man who had just told him to put it down was the man who had fastened it on.

So the Grandmaster didn't know what it cost to wear. He had given the band and never asked. He could price every project the city undertook, and had never once asked what this one was costing, and Daren had no words for it he could have offered him anyway.

He went down to the Skylark. On the stair the four notes came up in his throat, and he closed his mouth on them. His hand lay still at his side the whole way. He made it lie still. It was the only thing he still did himself.

---

They left the dead room with their terminals on. The gates and lifts between Ashoka and Drona took their credentials without interest: a reviewer under the Ancient One's code, a liaison under the mandate review, two of the dullest people moving through the city that night.

At the fourth level they stepped into the cart recess Rohan had chosen in daylight, on a lawful walk-through, the way he chose everything: for what it didn't have. No panel within four strides. No sensor housing overhead; Elara had read the maintenance plan herself. A recess at the corridor's far end, where it turned toward the lift, cut for a cart that no longer existed. A man coming from the lift would pass it before his eyes adjusted to the half-light. A man going the other way would have his back to it.

There, they unclasped their terminals.

She had taken hers off before, to wash, to sleep, in the few minutes of a day when a person was alone behind her own door. She had never walked out of a door without it. A Drona clerk had fastened the first one on a child's arm and told her it would keep her safe, and the strap had been changed a dozen times since, for size and then for age, and the wrist had grown from a child's into a woman's under it, and never once in all those years had the city lost sight of it. The skin beneath was pale, the weave of the strap pressed faintly into it like the grain of a glove. She switched the terminal off and put it in her coat.

Rohan switched his off and pocketed it. The silence came at once.

Nothing in the corridor had changed. She had not known, until it stopped, how much of her lived in the small weight on her wrist: the pulses she never noticed noticing, the faint warmth of a message arriving, the sense, constant as breathing, of standing inside a conversation with the city. It had stopped talking to her. She was in a recess with one other person, and that was all she was.

"It feels like going deaf," she said.

"It feels like the dead room," Rohan said. "Only we're carrying it with us."

Half-lit, pipes along the ceiling, one of them sweating a slow bead every few steps onto a stain the shape of a hand. A row of waist-high panels down the wall, each with its small green light. Someone had scrubbed a patch of the concrete near the third panel. The scrubbing had been thorough. It had not been thorough enough.

They stood side by side and waited.

It was the strangest hour of her life. She had stood watch before; every monitor had, through long cycles with nothing on the screens. She had never stood watch without a screen. There was nothing to read. There was the corridor, and the green lights, and water moving in the pipes, and Rohan's shoulder a hand's width from hers, and then, under all of it, his breathing.

She could hear him breathe. Slow, through the nose, a pause at the top of each one that she began to count by. Nineteen years in a city that told her where everyone was, and she had never once heard another person breathe with nothing between them, no pulse at her wrist naming him, no screen underlining what mattered about him. Only the sound a body makes keeping itself alive beside yours. Her own breath had fallen in with it without asking her, the way a second clock on a wall finds the first. She let it.

The lift at the far end sighed, and opened, and held its doors a beat longer than its schedule allowed.

A man stepped out.

He was ordinary. That was the first thing, and the thing she would keep longest. She had built him for weeks out of two letters and a signature and a dead man's notebook, built something vast and faceless, and what walked out of the lift was a man in a plain dark coat with his right hand held very still at his side, like someone carrying a full cup. Middle height. Neat. Tired in the way of a man who has stopped expecting to sleep. He didn't look around. The corridor was his.

He walked toward them.

Beside her Rohan had gone still, the stillness she'd seen in him once on a training floor, when a strike was coming and he had already decided where he'd be when it arrived. She kept her eyes on the man.

Ten strides. Eight.

The light came from behind him, from the lift, and did what such light does: it gave her the shape of him and kept his face. Collar up against nothing. Head down, the way a man walks home when there's no one at home to walk toward. She saw the right hand, held so still that the stillness itself was a kind of movement, and she saw that he was not tall or short or anything, and understood that this was the point of him: someone had chosen him, or made him, to be the kind of man a corridor forgets.

He passed the recess without turning his head. Six strides beyond them, at a door marked on every floor plan and absent from every access log, he slowed. His fingers tightened once against his coat. The door opened before his hand had risen toward it, and the sensor above the door, which Elara watched with her own bare eyes, did nothing at all.

In the light from the opening door she saw the side of his face.

She knew it.

She had sat beside that face for three days, at THRESHOLD, one console over. A pen tapped twice on the desk edge before each ruling. Four notes under the breath, the same four, while he priced the badges in the room. Kubera's muted gold, the insignia set dead center. Removed from Kubera's rolls since; she'd read the line herself and filed it under things that had happened to other people. Daren. Walking home, at the hand's hour, to a room no record admitted.

The door closed. The corridor was empty.

Neither of them moved for a long time. Then Rohan let out a breath she hadn't heard him take and said, very low, "Did you see his face?"

"Yes."

"I didn't. Only the hand." He was quiet. "I saw a man who's afraid of his own arm."

"Daren," she said.

He turned his head.

"Daren. Kubera. He sat at THRESHOLD through the whole Initiation watch. I know how he carries his head when he's tired." Her voice stayed low. "It's him."

"Daren's on no roll in this city. Kubera struck him. There's nothing with his name on it to lay beside a face."

"I know."

"You've got a face, in a doorway, in bad light, for the length of a breath."

"Yes."

"And you can't put the wall on a table, and a face isn't a road." He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way the Ancient One had, laying the terms down one at a time. "So find me another road."

She already knew where it ran. A tower keeps a book of who sleeps in it, on paper, open to a review code. "The guest register," she said. "In the morning."

They went back by the service stair, because the lifts would ask who they were and would not like the answer. Over the stair door a ceiling reader pulsed amber, twice, at two people it had no name for; Rohan's eyes went up to it and came down again, and Elara, looking back along the corridor at a door that was in no log, did not see it.

At the stair's foot, before the first gate, they switched their terminals on and fastened them. Hers woke with a dozen small pulses, the city picking up its conversation where it had left off. She didn't read any of them.

She had a door, an hour, and a name she couldn't yet use.
