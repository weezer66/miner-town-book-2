# Chapter 28: Feeds

*"You haven't worked the deep." Gage didn't look up from the model. "I have — two months of it. The water's going to come fast and the lamps will go. Whoever plants this has to know the corridors by hand. You don't. I do."*

— Book 1, Chapter 12: The Splinter Architect

## Now: Feeds

The sand-glass stood on the desk between the headset man's tin of crackers and his elbow, and nobody had turned it yet.

Camilla stood in the middle of the screen room, where she could see both rows and the glass. Top row, the mine in grey: the guard post at the head of the Hub lift, the turbine floor, the Hub itself with its rails and its props and its string of lamps going away into the dark, and at the mouth of the run a small light that was Gage. Bottom row, the ring: a corridor with a boy standing in it, and a long room where four old people sat at a table and a man with a soft face stood at the foot of it, and on the lacquer in front of the fifth chair a row of rings lay on their sides.

Ben was on his stool with his eyes shut. The stool had one leg a hair shorter than the others, and he'd hooked his heel round it so it wouldn't rock.

"Board's going over," he said. "Neck. Now nobody's looking at the gauges."

The headset man took a cracker without looking at the tin.

"Cage is loading. Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one, he's the one who always goes back for his flask." A pause. On the top row a man went back for his flask. "Twenty-two."

Camilla's good hand was on the desk. Her metal one hung at her side, and she let it. The arm had been quiet all evening, a beat behind her and no more.

"Turbine floor," Ben said. "He's talking to the new man. He laughed at the start." His voice did something small. "Kid's well. He's going home on time tonight."

The man on the turbine floor clapped the new man's shoulder and walked toward the cage. Camilla watched him all the way into it.

"Twenty-six. Gate's shut. Cage is going up." Ben's eyes stayed closed. "That's all of them. The Hub's empty, Cami. There's nobody down there but ours."

Ours was one light at the mouth of a tunnel.

"Gage," she said into the line.

"Here." His voice came out of the desk speaker flat and close, nearer than he was. Under it, a hum she knew, the fans in the Hub ceiling. "Setting it down at eleven. On the lip."

On the screen the light went into the run and stopped a little way in. She saw it dip as he crouched. The listener had gone up the mountain in a tin case with a strap, the coil and the clay and the needle, and the engineer had padded the inside with grey rag so the clay wouldn't crack on the stairs.

"Needle's up," Gage said.

"What's it hearing?"

"Cage going up the shaft. Fans. The gate." A breath. "Me."

She waited. The sand hadn't moved. The engineer stood with his hand on the brass cage of the glass, ready, his pencil stubs behind his ears, looking at her.

"It's deaf to the wall from here, Cami. It's got the whole Hub in it. It'd hear the wall if it was the only thing talking, and it isn't. Not out here."

"Fourteen," she said. "Where the water comes in on the left. Try it there."

Nothing on the line. The light didn't move.

"Gage."

"Thirty-one," he said.

Her metal hand closed. She hadn't told it to, and she found that she had, that the telling and the closing had happened in the same breath, and she couldn't say which had come first. On the top row the light lifted and went on into the run, and the hum of the fans in the speaker faded as he walked away from them, and after a while under his breath she heard him counting.

She'd known since a clean room with a box of splinters on the table. She'd known standing in a door with egg yolk drying in her knuckles. It went through her anyway, down the good arm and the other one, and she stood in it.

"Twenty-five," Gage said, far off. "Twenty-six."

"Clear," Ben said.

The engineer's eyes were on her. The glass waited under his hand.

"Thirty-one. I'm here." A scrape, the case set down on stone. "Needle's up. It's quiet. It's just the wall and me."

Camilla breathed in.

"Feeds," she said.

The engineer turned the glass over.

"Closed," he said.

Nothing happened on any screen. Rust-colored sand ran in a thread. On the top row the Hub stood empty under its lamps, and the light at the far end of the run was still, and the headset man's hand had stopped on the way to the tin.

Then Gage's voice, low and certain, out of the rock a mile away.

"Landed. Again."

"Again," she said.

"Closed."

---

They traded the words over the sand as they'd traded them over clay, except that now the gaps between them were real and had a boy in them.

"Landed. Again."

"Again."

"Closed."

Ben had started counting the blows under his breath, in case Gage lost them. Four. Five. Camilla watched the top row and the glass and the top row, and because she was watching everything she saw the bottom row change.

In the long room the Grandmasters sat as they had sat. The man with the soft face hadn't moved. But at the back of the room, behind the fifth chair, a door she'd taken for part of the wall had opened a hand's width, and along the bottom edge of that screen the ring's own writing had come up in small grey letters, the kind that came up when a lift held or a corridor sensor woke. *Door. No hand.* The ring had asked who'd opened it and been given nothing.

Four Grandmasters turned their heads toward it. The fifth chair's man didn't. He sat with his bare right hand resting on the table beside his rings and looked at the soft-faced man across the lacquer, and the set of his shoulders was that of someone whose guest had arrived when he'd said he would.

"Landed. Again."

"Again."

"Closed."

On the next screen along, the boy in the corridor had started walking.

He didn't hurry. Otto never had, since the tank; he walked like someone being polite to the floor. But he was walking toward the long room, and she knew the ring's corridors well enough now to know that the door at the back of that room and the corridor he was in met.

Six, Ben said. Seven.

She had the line to Gage in her good hand and the ring on the screen in front of her, and one mouth.

The screen-room door opened behind her.

She knew every tread in this place by now, the headset man's, the engineer's, the medic's soft-soled ones. These were none of them. These were boots that had come down a great many stairs tonight and weren't used to stairs. Camilla turned her head and kept the rest of her where it was.

A young woman in Drona's gray-blue, nineteen or so, with her hair coming down on one side and her left wrist bare. She'd stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame. The wrist had a pale band on it where something had always sat, and as Camilla watched she turned it up to read it, and found nothing there, and let it fall.

The woman's eyes went over the room once, the screens, the glass, Ben on his stool, the headset man half risen, and came to rest on Camilla. Then on the arm.

"That door," she said. She came in. "At the back of the table room. Nobody touched it."

"You're meant to be upstairs."

"I was put behind glass to watch. I've watched." She was at the bottom row now, close, her breath moving a hair on Camilla's cheek. "I know that door. I've been counting it for six weeks on a wall you've never seen. It opens before the hand rises. There's a man coming through it with a thing on his right wrist, and the thing opens doors for him and does worse than that, and he can't take it off."

"Landed. Again."

Camilla's mouth said "Again" while her eyes stayed on the woman.

"Closed."

Nine, Ben said.

"Who's the boy?" the woman said. On the next screen Otto had reached a turn and taken it.

"Mine." Camilla heard her own voice, the one she used on the bench when a thing was going wrong in two places. "You walked out of wherever they put you and came down here. Why here?"

"Because this is where the screens are, and I'm no use behind glass." She looked at the top row, at the empty Hub, at the thread of light in the run. She didn't ask what it was. Camilla watched her not ask, and understood that she already knew most of it. "Listen to me. I'll say this once because you don't have time for twice. The city above isn't watching your wall tonight. Ashoka is standing in every doorway of every tower because a man I used to sit beside put it there. Drona's wall is turned the other way. Nobody up there is looking at the ground. I've been in the room where they'd be looking from. I know."

Camilla took it like a reading off a gauge she didn't own.

"You're sure."

"I'm never sure. I'm telling you what I saw." The woman's eyes dropped to the metal arm again, and this time they stayed. Something in her face went still, the stillness of a person finding a number she'd carried a long way written on something real. "Open," she said. "Close. Half a breath late, and the gap closing. Then a fist, one night, held. No house. No floor."

Ten, Ben said.

"That was you," the woman said. "Your arm talks to Drona's wall. It has since the day they fitted it."

The glass had a third of its sand left.

Camilla looked at the arm. It hung at her side, grey, the fingers a little curled, as they always were when she wasn't thinking about them. Every cup, every egg, every dent in a tabletop. A fist at her side on the night Otto woke. Somewhere above the cloud a column on a wall she'd never see, filling up with her.

One breath. She let it in and let it out and the arm came with the second half of it, late.

"Later," she said.

"Yes."

"Landed." Gage's voice. "Again."

"Again."

"Closed."

On the bottom row the door at the back of the long room had opened the rest of the way, and someone stood in it, a dark shape against darker stone, and the four Grandmasters had risen, and the man with the rings had not. On the next screen Otto was two turns from them. On the top row, a mile of rock away, a light stood at a wall that the ring was hitting from the inside, and the boy holding the light was hers too.

Two fronts. One mouth.

She knew which of her brothers could do without her voice tonight. He'd told her so at a bench, with his finger on a split splinter, every night for nineteen nights.

She put the line down on the desk in front of Ben. He opened his eyes.

"You've got it," she said. "Call it if I'm not here."

He looked at the line, and at her, and his hand came off his hip and took it.

"Eleven," he said into it, in the same voice, so Gage wouldn't hear the change. "Landed?"

"Landed," said the speaker. "Who's that?"

"Me. Again."

"Closed," said the engineer.

Camilla was already at the door. The Drona woman had stepped out of her way without being asked, and was watching her go with the look of someone who'd have to find her again.

"Where's he going?" the woman said, to the screen, to Otto.

"Where I'm going," Camilla said, and went.

---

The tunnel took his voice and gave it back smaller.

"Landed," Gage said. "Again."

"Again." Ben, now. Cami had gone off the line without a word to him, and he knew what that meant, that something in the ring had wanted her more than the wall did, and he put it where he put things and left his left hand on the rock.

The wall was talking to him through the palm.

He'd known it would; that was the whole of his plan, two months of the deep in one hand. What he hadn't known was how it would sound. Each time the ring shut the feeds the water came back up the pipe along the lake wall with nowhere to go, and the rock took it. Through the hand it was a thud with a shiver after it, the way a prop talks when a cart's gone over it too fast. In the tin case at his boots the needle jumped on every blow and stood up trembling. Between blows it lay flat. The listener could hear the wall now. It had only needed someone to carry it to where the wall was the only thing speaking.

"Landed. Again."

"Again."

"Closed."

Twelve, he counted. In the rehearsals it had always been twelve. On the thirteenth something changed under his hand.

The shiver didn't stop.

It went on after the blow, a fine grain in the stone like a held note, and a little way along the wall from his fingers a thread of dust let go of the ceiling and came down through the lamplight in a straight line. He'd seen dust come down like that once before, in his first month, from a roof that had come down an hour later on an empty gallery. Men who'd worked the deep said the rock told you. It told you if your hand was on it.

"It's going," he said. "Ben. The wall's talking."

"Say again."

"It's going. One more."

"Again," Ben said, and his voice hadn't moved, and Gage loved him for it.

"Closed."

The fourteenth blow landed and he heard it with his ears.

That was wrong. All night the wall had been a thing in his palm. Now the sound came out of the rock itself, a long low groan with grit in it, the sound a mountain makes clearing its throat, and the floor of the run pressed up through his boots and his ears filled as if he'd gone down the shaft too fast in the cage. The lamp nearest the junction swung on its string.

Water came through the stone in a sheet.

There was no burst to it. It arrived, a dark skin over the wall from his hand to the roof, and then it was at his boots, and then the sound was everywhere and had no direction.

He picked up the case. He turned round.

"Thirty," he said into the line. "Through. I'm coming out."

"Thirty," Ben said. "Go."

He ran the run backward by hand.

Left hand on the rock going in; right hand on it coming out. The props went by under his fingers and he counted them down, because counting was what kept the dark from being the dark. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight. The water was at his shins, cold enough to take the feeling out of them, and it was running where he was running, toward the Hub, and faster. Behind him the mountain said something long. The lamp over twenty-six went out, and then twenty-five, not in a row, the string taking them as it pleased.

"Twenty-four," he said. He didn't know if the line still carried. "Twenty-three."

The case under his arm was heavy with water. He'd have liked to drop it. His hand didn't.

At fourteen the water that had always come in on the left came in on the left and on the right and from above, and he went through it with his mouth shut and his eyes shut and his right hand reading the props. Thirteen. Twelve. The beam. He ducked for it, the whole of his height folding, and it took the top of his head anyway, a hard flat kiss, and he kept his feet.

"Eleven," he said. "Lip."

He stepped up over the drain sill onto the Hub floor, and the Hub was lit.

The last string of lamps along the mouth still burned, and in their light he saw the whole of Sector One's great room filling.

Water came out of the run behind him, and out of two other mouths along the far side that he hadn't known joined the same rock. It crossed the rails. It took the turbine floor the way the model had said it would, lake to pods along the fall, the line his finger had drawn, and the turbine housings stood in it up to their shoulders and then didn't stand. He heard the pods go in the dark below, a sound like a tray of cups dropped down a stair. The silver galleries were already under. Their straw. He'd drawn it a hundred times.

Then he saw the pump house.

It sat on the far rim of the Hub, where it had always sat, a squat stone box with a tin roof and a lamp over its door that the pump crew never turned off. Every cup of water in Miner Town came up out of the lake through that house, through its one old engine, into the ration tanks and the standpipes and the trough where the women queued at the shift bell. It was Trinity's pump. It was their water. He'd never put it on the model, because the model was the straw, and the pump house wasn't the straw. It was a hundred strides from the fall line. He'd measured it with his thumb.

The water came up its step.

It came up its step and its door and its lamp, and the lamp burned under the water for the length of a breath, a small gold smear, and went out.

He stood on the lip with the case under his arm and his mouth open, and nothing he knew how to do was any use. He'd run the deep by hand to be here. He'd counted thirty-one props so a wall would break on the right night, and it had, and the mountain had taken the straw and the pump with one hand, because the mountain didn't know what a model was.

"Ben," he said.

"I'm here. Where are you?"

"The pump house." The water was at his knees on the lip. "Ben, the pump house's gone. The ration water. It's gone under."

Nothing from the line. Then: "Get out, Gage. Get to the cage."

The cage was across the Hub. The Hub was a lake with lamps in it. He turned for the ladder-way instead, the old one along the wall, the one that nobody used, and the roof over the mouth of the run spoke.

He knew the sound. He'd heard it in his first month. He got his shoulder under the last prop out of habit, a hand put up against a falling thing, and the prop was the only one in the run he'd never counted, because it wasn't in the run. It was the one that held the lip.

It came down across him, and the rock behind it followed, and the lamps on the mouth went out all at once, and the water took the light.

"Ten," he said, to no one, under it. He couldn't find the line. He couldn't find his right hand. "Nine."

The water was warm now, which was wrong, and the count went on without him.

---

The speaker on the desk had been quiet for a long time, and the boy on the stool was still counting into it.

"Eight," Ben said. "Seven."

Elara stood where the girl had stood. Nobody had told her to leave, and nobody had told her to stay. The headset man had gone grey and gone out, and come back, and sat, and his hand lay on the tin of crackers without opening it. The engineer had left the glass where it was, upside down and empty, and stood with his two pencils behind his ears looking at nothing. On the top row every screen that had been the mine was dark but one, and that one showed water with lamplight on it, moving, and no one in it.

She didn't know the boy in the tunnel. She knew his light. She'd watched it go in, and stop, and stand, and come back along the run with the lamps going out ahead of it, and she'd watched the last string go dark all at once, and in that dark something had come down.

"Six," Ben said.

"How many were there?" she said.

He didn't look up. "Thirty-one. He counts them going in. He counts them coming out." He swallowed. "He was at eleven. Then he was at ten. He's counting down to the lip. I'm counting with him so he's not on his own."

She pulled the headset man's second chair across the floor, and sat in it, beside the stool, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched and didn't. The chair had a cracked seat that pinched. She left it.

"Five," Ben said.

She listened with him. The speaker gave back a hiss with the shape of a tunnel in it and nothing else.

"What's his name?"

"Gage." Ben's thumb was moving on the side of the line, rubbing a worn place on it. "He's hers. Her brother. He's the one who built it, all of it, out of matchsticks, and then he's the one who carried it in." He breathed. "Four."

Elara had spent six weeks counting things on a wall. Door cycles. A column with no house and no floor. A boy named Baju dying in a line of grey letters. She'd never counted beside anyone. She found that she was doing it now, under her breath, a beat behind Ben, and that her bare wrist had turned itself up again to read what wasn't there.

"Three," they said.

Nothing from the speaker.

"Two."

On the bottom row the long room was lit and full. The four Grandmasters stood. The man with the soft face had come round the table to the foot of it and stopped. The boy from the corridor, Otto, stood in the room now, a little apart from everyone, with his hands open at his sides, and the girl with the metal arm was in the door behind him, breathing hard, her hair stuck to her face.

And in the far doorway, the one the ring had asked about and got no answer, Daren stood. Camilla's screen showed him as the register had, a name and a room: coat buttoned to the throat, right arm held a little out from his body, fingers spread. He was looking at Otto. Otto was looking at nothing in particular.

Between them, on the lacquer, the Master's rings lay in their row where he'd set them, each one on its side, and the old man's bare hand rested beside them, and he had still not turned his head.

"One," Ben said into the silent line, and waited in the gap, the same length as always, for the answer that would make it a count.
