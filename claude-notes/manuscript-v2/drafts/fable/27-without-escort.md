# Chapter 27: Without Escort

*"Not yet. I need you to be silent until we bring your friends back to consciousness. All of us need to talk. I have been waiting over twenty years for this day."*

— Book 1, Chapter 20: Mr. Flitzberg

## Now: Without escort

The screen room had never held this many people.

Camilla counted them from the door, by habit. The man with the headset, in his chair under the grid, the cracker tin at his elbow with its lid on. Two women she'd passed in corridors and never seen in here, at a side bench that had worn a dust sheet last week, a row of small dull plates in front of each of them. The engineer, with two whole pencils behind his ears, sharpened, the first unbroken pencils she'd ever seen on him. The grey-haired medic in the corner with her hands folded, because somebody might get hurt tonight and she'd rather already be in the room. Flitzberg, standing, in his own face.

Seven, with her. Ten when the boys came down.

The sand-glass sat on the end of the long desk in its brass cage, all the rust-colored sand in the bottom. Nobody had touched it.

On the top row of screens, the city: platforms and gateways and stairheads up in the light, and in every one of them a figure in red-black, standing. Ashoka had put a guardian in every doorway the city had, because a council had voted it should, and tonight the whole house was on its feet above the cloud with its back to the mountain.

"Less watched than it has been in a century," Flitzberg had said, when the screens came up. "I did not arrange it. I am going to use it."

The box was on the desk beside the glass with its lid off.

She'd built the lid from a ration tin, the ring's own stamp still on it, and the thing inside was what weeks on a bench had made of a coil, a plate of clay and a needle: smaller than it had started, denser, with a strap for a shoulder. She laid the metal hand flat on the desk a foot from it, and the needle shivered, and then she laid her good hand down the same way, and it shivered less.

"Rock," the engineer said. "This morning. The lift shaft on the lower level, when the cage went by. It jumped for the rock and not for the cage."

"And last night it jumped for you dropping a bolt three rooms off and called that rock too."

"Yes."

"Sometimes," she said.

"Sometimes."

She put the lid on. Sometimes was a needle that kicked, and a needle that kicked went in the log and you waited on it, and there wasn't any waiting left. It would go up the mountain tonight with a man who could tell a landed blow from a missed one with the flat of his hand, so that if the box lied he'd know. She'd built it so nobody would stand at the wall. Someone was going to carry it in.

---

Ben came down first, in the greys, with graphite still in the creases of his knuckles from a floor a long way up. He went to the stool beside the headset man and sat on it and turned it a quarter toward the door, and put his right hand on his hip, and took it off.

"Said I'd be back," he said.

"You said by the third night."

"I was. Nobody checked." He looked at the screens as he had for weeks, at the holes between the people. On the row second from the top, the mine: the Hub lift's head, a guard post with a board on a chain, the turbine floor under its lamps. Men still on it. "Shift change in forty. The turbine man's kid's well. He was laughing on the way down."

Then Gage filled the door, and Otto was beside him.

He'd walked down. Camilla could see it in the way he stood, a boy whose legs had agreed to the stairs on terms. He'd put on the greys and they hung on him, and there was a hen's feather caught on the sleeve from the level with the hens, and he hadn't noticed it, and nobody told him.

"You're not coming up," Gage said. He said it to the room, having already said it to Otto on every landing.

"I know."

"Then go lie down."

"No." Otto didn't raise his voice. He never had. "There's a room down here where the table is. I'm going to stand near it."

"Why?"

Otto thought about it honestly, which was his way. "I don't know. Something in me says that's where I'm wanted." He looked at Camilla, and at the box under her hand. "You've got your ear. I've got that."

Gage opened his mouth, and shut it. Camilla watched him look for a handle on it and not find one. You could argue with a plan. Nobody in the room had ever once found a way to argue with the stillness, and Flitzberg, who had built a test around a thing he didn't understand, only inclined his head.

"The gallery corridor," he said. "Outside the table room. I will have you shown there. You will not go in."

"I wasn't going to go in," Otto said.

---

Gage came to the desk.

He took the box by its strap and put it over his head and settled it on his chest, on the left, where Thorton's wrench rode in the belt loop under it, and the two of them clinked once and found how to sit together. He stood there with it. His hands were open at his sides. He'd kept them that way so long that she'd stopped seeing it, and now she saw it again, two big hands held open in a room of screens so no one would mistake them for anything.

Then they closed. Both, slow, on nothing.

He didn't look down at them. She did.

He reached into his pocket and took out a splinter. Pale wood, thin as a fingernail, split a hair down the middle by a thumbnail. He'd kept one back every time he swept the box, then. She'd never seen him do it.

"Hold that," he said.

He put it in her metal palm. The fingers came round it half a breath behind her, and closed, and she felt the wood through them as she felt everything through them, at the far end of a long pipe, and it held.

"You've got the count," he said. "Ben's got the men. I've got the run."

"I know what you've got."

Neither of them said the rest. The ring could close the feeds. It couldn't see the wall. Her box could, sometimes, and sometimes needed a hand on the stone beside it.

She'd known since a clay basin on a grey table who would stand there. So had he. Saying it would have made it a thing one of them had decided.

"Go on," she said.

He went. He didn't duck the door; the door was tall enough. He ducked anyway.

Ben got up off the stool and sat down again.

---

Flitzberg had put on a coat.

That was how she knew he was leaving. Plain grey, no house in it, buttoned to the throat over the place where her brother's fingers had once left their marks. He stood by the door with his hands folded, in the face she had checked.

"Four Grandmasters," she said. "And you."

"And a fifth chair that has never had anyone in it who did not belong to a house." He looked at the screens, at the men in red-black on their stairheads. "Tonight it will."

"You said we'd never feel ready."

"I said no one ever has." He was quiet. The plucked thing wasn't playing; there was no music anywhere in the ring tonight. "I had assumed, when I said it, that I was describing other people."

It cost him. She saw it go out of him and watched him let it.

"Sooner than you're ready," she said. "You said so."

"I was right." He unfolded his hands. "I would rather have been wrong by a month. Call it when Ben says clear. Not before, and not for me." Then the door, and the grey coat going through it, and the door closing on him without a sound, the way every door in this place did.

---

The car had no windows.

Elara knew that was the point, and still her eyes went looking for one, the whole ride, round the grey felt lining and the two facing seats and the small brass hook above the Ancient One's head with nothing on it. The old woman sat in the darned shawl with her hands in her lap and her eyes closed. She might have been asleep. Elara didn't think she was.

Her wrist was bare. The terminal lay in the dead room's locker, in its slot, beside the empty one.

Rohan had walked her up the three worn flights to the street door, because it was the last thing he was permitted to do. The Dhammapala had gone an hour before, from a different door, alone. He'd told Elara that on the stairs in the voice he used for postings.

"I walked her to the car. The door shut while my hand was still on it." He'd looked at the hand. "Without escort cuts both ways. I should've heard it."

"You heard it."

"I heard it." At the street door he'd stopped. Beyond it, under the lamps, the closed car waited with its one door open and a figure in plain grey beside it, no house anywhere on her. "There's a way down. The runner's lift, in Drona's lower stacks. The boy came up by it and I walked the other one back to it." He'd said it without looking at her, to the car. "I won't use it unless the night breaks."

She hadn't believed him. She'd known him long enough now to know the difference between a thing he'd decided and a thing he was telling himself, and this had been the second. She hadn't argued. There'd been one door open under the lamps, and it had one seat left in it, and the Dhammapala had been right about the axe.

"Keep your hands empty," she'd said instead, and gone down the step.

The car stopped. The door opened onto stone, and a woman in grey led them to a lift with no floors marked on it, no panel, nothing on its back wall at all, and it went down for so long that Elara's ears closed and opened and closed. The Ancient One kept her eyes shut through all of it.

"Where you can see the work," the old woman said, when the lift stopped, "and no one can see you doing it. I told you."

"Yes."

"Then go and see it."

---

The gallery was a stone shelf behind glass, high in the wall of a round room, and it had a bench and a ledge and a single lamp turned low, and on the ledge there was a ring where a cup had once stood, the stain gone brown and set into the stone. Somebody had sat here before her. Somebody had sat here often.

Elara sat where they'd sat.

Below the boundary. That was all she could lawfully say she knew about where she was: that the lift had gone down for longer than any shaft in Drona was deep, and the stone around her had been cut by hands and then left alone, and on the Ancient One's old paper map there was a blank where every street stopped and the pen had lifted. She was sitting inside the blank. It had chairs in it.

Five. Round a table with no head and no foot, under a lamp that hung from a chain so long it vanished into the dark above the lamp's own light.

The Ancient One was the first to come in, below her, from a door Elara couldn't see, and crossed to the chair facing the gallery and sat in it with her shawl pulled close. She didn't look up. Elara understood that she wasn't going to.

Then the Arthashastri, in Chanakya's green-grey, a narrow man who took in the room in one unhurried pass and chose his chair like a man choosing a move. He laid both hands flat on the table and left them there.

Then the Dhammapala, alone. She came in without a sound in red-black, and she had the look of a woman who had walked a long way inside a small space, and when she sat, she set one hand on the table's edge and looked across it at the Ancient One, and the two old women held each other's eyes a while, and nothing in either face moved, and something passed anyway.

Then a man Elara had never seen.

He came in from the Ancient One's side and took the fifth chair, and he belonged to no house. She looked for it as she'd been taught to look at everyone, uniform first, and found a plain grey coat buttoned to the throat and nothing else: no color, no crest, no wrist. A man with a soft face. Full cheeks, smooth skin, a stillness laid over the features so even it looked made. He folded his hands on the table in front of him and looked at the empty chair.

The empty chair was gold's. Kubera's. It stayed empty long enough that the Arthashastri's hands lifted off the wood and came down again.

Then a door at the far side of the room opened on its own, before anyone's hand had reached it, and the Master of the Reserve walked in without escort, as he'd said he would.

He was smaller than she'd thought. Close-cropped grey hair, muted gold, rings on every finger, and he walked to the chair in the manner of a man walking to a chair in his own house. He sat. He looked at the four faces round him in turn, and last at the soft-faced man, and the corner of his mouth lifted, and he began to take off his rings.

Elara's hand had closed round a pencil that wasn't there.

*No house. No floor.* The right-hand edge of a grey wall, a column nobody had asked for, with its location field blank. She'd carried the blank out of that chamber like a stone in a shoe. She was sitting in a blank now. She didn't know if they were the same blank. She knew she'd ask.

---

The door before the Phoenix opened for Daren as they all did now, ahead of his hand, and he let it, and that was the whole of what he let it do.

He'd made the rule on the maglev and kept it down the Great Hall and under the founders' eyes. The band would open doors. Nothing else. No gate asked who he was; no reader woke; no ledger behind any door gave him what it held. He walked through the city with the instrument on his wrist cool as the room and asked it for one thing, and it gave him that one thing, and it waited with its whole black attention for the second.

He didn't give it the second.

The Master had said he knew the way. *That is a thing one learns, wearing it. Among others.* Daren had turned the sentence over on the narrow bed for a night and found the hinge in it. The band had been down this road before, on a different wrist. It remembered roads as it remembered dialects. So he didn't choose his doors tonight. He walked, and let his hand hang open, and at every turning where two doors could have opened, one did.

Below the Great Hall, below the vaults, in a service level where the lamps were a generation older and buzzed, a steel door in a wall of plain brick slid aside onto a stair.

He went down it.

The brick stopped after one flight and dressed stone began, blocks the size of a man's chest, each with a mason's mark in one corner, a small bird or something like one. The stair turned on itself. The marks stopped. Then the stone was only stone, cut with tools that had left ridges you could feel through boot soles, and then the ridges stopped too, and the walls were rock, dark and sweating, and his breath came back to him off them.

A rail ran down the right-hand side. Dust lay on it thick as felt, except in a single strip along its top, at the height of a short man's hand, where it had been wiped bright by one hand, going down, over years.

Daren didn't put his hand on the rail. He kept it open at his side and went down beside the shining strip, step after step, a man walking a road someone had already worn.

The notebook was against his chest. He felt it at every step, the soft cover taking his warmth, the corner pressing its ribbon of pressure along the rib. He still didn't know the name. Ashoka knew it. Somewhere on a roll in that house, a clerk had written it down beside *technician, temporary assignment*, and a woman in red-black who was somewhere beneath him now would know it without looking. He had come down with no words ready. He'd tried, on the bed, to make a sentence that began *I* and ended somewhere, and every one of them was two-thirds of the truth, and he'd stopped. He would be in the room. He didn't know past that. He knew the man's paper would be in the room too, where somebody could say the name over it.

The stair gave onto a corridor, and the corridor onto a lift.

No floors. No panel. The car took him without asking, and the doors closed, and in the black of the back wall a man looked out at him.

He knew him from a maglev, at the dark end of a carriage. Coat buttoned to the throat. Right arm held a little out from the body, the fingers spread, as if the hands were wet. Something black at the wrist that drank what light there was. The eyes gave nothing back; the wall was too dark for that. He looked at the man a cat had once looked at, and for the length of a breath he was the cat, ears flat, body low in its carrier, watching something come down a lift that none of the instruments could feel.

Then the car stopped and the doors opened and the man in the wall was gone.

He stepped out into a corridor of the ring's plain grey, lit even and low, and the air had a smell of washed stone and under it something warmer, like a kitchen a long way off. The band was awake on his wrist. He felt it reach as it always reached, out along the lines the corridor was built on, feeling for whatever was there to read: a door, a panel, a wrist. It found doors. It found, far down the corridor, a lens the size of a fingernail, and asked it nothing. He kept his hand open.

At the far end a boy was standing.

Seventeen, maybe. Small, in grey, with a feather on his sleeve, facing the wall beside a closed door with his hands at his sides and his weight on the balls of his feet, and so still that the corridor seemed to have been built round him. He didn't turn his head. He didn't do anything.

The band reached for him. Daren felt it go, the whole of its attention swinging to the one living thing in its path, out along the bone and across the forty paces of grey, feeling for the flinch, the grip, the wish, the small frightened signal every body sends ahead of itself.

It came back with nothing. A hand closing on air.

Daren stood with it, not understanding. He had never once in all these weeks felt the band find nothing where a person stood. It had found the man in the corridor, and the councilmen, and a cat. The boy at the end of the corridor stood with his face to a wall, breathing, and gave it nothing to read at all, and the band lay against his wrist as cool as an empty corridor.

He turned down the side passage the band had chosen for him, toward the room where the table was, and the boy didn't watch him go, and Daren felt him not watching all the way to the door.

---

On the fourth screen from the left, the table.

The man with the headset had brought it up without being asked. Round room, round table, five chairs and no head to it. Camilla had seen the shawl before; she'd never seen the woman in it. She'd never seen the other three. The narrow one in green-grey with his hands flat. The one in red-black who'd come alone. Gold, with his rings off. He'd laid them on the wood in front of him in a row, each one a thumb's width from the next, and he'd squared the row with one finger before he sat back. Ten rings. She counted them, because she counted everything.

Flitzberg sat in the fifth chair with his hands folded and said nothing.

High in the room's far wall, behind glass, there was a lamp turned low and a shape sitting beside it. Camilla looked at it, and filed it, and moved on, because there was nothing on that screen she could do anything about.

On the screen beside it, a grey corridor, and Otto with his face to a wall. He hadn't moved since they'd shown him there. The feather was still on his sleeve.

Her good hand lay on the desk beside the glass. The metal one lay beside the box's empty place, closed round a splinter, and when she thought about the splinter the fingers closed harder, late, and she heard the wood creak and told them to stop, and they stopped a beat after.

"Lamp," Ben said.

Second row. The Hub mouth, a tunnel into black rock with a drain sill across it, and in the sill, a point of light. It swung as the man carrying it walked. It stopped. It lifted, a little, the way a lamp lifts when a man straightens his back under a strap.

Nobody said his name. Saying it would have made it a question.

"Guard post," Ben said, in the voice he'd used for the man with the crackers, flat, in order. "New man's at the board. Old man's looking at his boots." A pause. "He's scratching his neck."

The man with the headset wrote something down.

"Turbine floor's up. Six, seven, eight. Last two on the lift." Ben's hand went to his hip, pressed flat over the pocket that wasn't there, and stayed. "Lift's gone. Hub's clear of the floor crew. Nobody on the Hub but him."

The engineer's hand was on the brass cage. He hadn't turned it. He was waiting for her, and she was waiting for Ben, and Ben was waiting for a man on a turbine floor a mile above them to finish signing a board, and under all of them the two women at the side bench sat with their hands a finger's width above their row of dull plates and didn't touch them.

On the fourth screen, the Master of the Reserve said something, and the soft face across from him didn't change.

On the second, the lamp went in. It went into the black of the Hub mouth and got small, and stayed, a star in a well, and then it moved left and dropped a hair, and she knew without hearing it that he'd stepped high over a lip that was eleven props in, and three strides later it dropped again for a beam that wasn't there.

The feeds were open. All of them. Somewhere up in the mountain the fastest water in it was going where it went every night, down pipes nobody in this room would ever see, past a junction where a boy had once put his thumbnail into a splinter. The wall was dry. Ben hadn't said clear. The glass hadn't turned.

She lifted her hand off the desk.

It was the metal one. She hadn't chosen it; it had come up because the other was flat on the wood, and it rose with the splinter still in its fist and hung in the air over the plate the engineer had shown her on the first night, the one that was hers to touch and nobody else's. It hung there half a beat behind where she'd meant it to be, and then it was where she'd meant it to be, and it waited. She could feel what it could do. All that strength, over a plate the size of a coin.

On the fourth screen, ten rings in a row. On the second, a lamp going down a run she'd heard counted a hundred times in a corridor with no doors. On the one beside it, a boy who hadn't moved.

*Feeds.*

Not yet.
