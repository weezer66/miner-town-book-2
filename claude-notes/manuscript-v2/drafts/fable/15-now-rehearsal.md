# Chapter 15: Rehearsal

*The crystal had pulsed three sections green, one still cold blue. Above the last door, the word waited where it had waited from the start. SACRIFICE. The other rooms had asked what they could do. This one asked what they would give.*

— Book 1, Chapter 24: The Green Room

## Now: Twelve nights

The engineer had brought eggs.

Six of them, brown, in a dented tin lined with a rag. He would not say where in the ring a hen lived. He stood at the end of the bench with his pencil stubs behind his ears and said, "Pick one up. Put it down. Then the next."

She'd done bolts the first day. A week of washers, until she could feel the difference between one and the next through metal that had no feeling in it. Then the matchsticks from Gage's box, which he had counted when they came back. Eggs had an opinion about being held.

The first came up clean. The fingers closed round it and she felt nothing, which was still the strange part, and the egg sat whole in the grey palm. She counted the gap between thinking *close* and the hand closing, the way she'd counted it all week: a third of a breath. *One-and.* Half had been *one-and-two*, and she'd lived with that for ten days and called it slow and hated it.

Now it was a third, and she hated something else, and it was harder to find the name of it.

Second egg. Third. The hand was learning her. That was the engineer's word, and he used it the way Pappy had used *seating* for a bearing that had worn itself to fit: a good word with no praise in it. She'd caught herself at the sink that morning turning the tap with the metal hand without looking, the way you'd use any hand, and had stood there with the water running and not liked how easy it had been.

Fourth egg.

She thought *close* and it closed on the thought, no gap she could count, and the egg went.

A wet give, then yolk between the grey fingers and running off the knuckle onto the rag, and the smell, stronger than one egg had any right to. The hand stayed shut. She had to tell it to open, and the shell fell out in two halves and a web of white.

"Ah," the engineer said.

"It went early." She was wiping the fingers on the rag, hard. "It's meant to lag."

"It is meant to listen. Today it heard you well." He took a pencil stub from behind his ear and drew a short line in the bench dust, beside three others. "Early when you are sure, late when you are not. You will have to be sure less loudly."

"I wasn't sure."

"Your shoulder was."

She picked up the fifth with her good hand, because she wanted to feel it, the grit of the shell and the warmth it had kept from wherever it was laid, and set it in the metal palm, and closed the fingers slow, counting, and they stopped where she stopped them.

The egg sat whole. The hand had done it. She had done it. She couldn't say any more where one of those ended.

"Again," she said, and he made no mark, and she was grateful for that.

---

The corridor on the lowest level ran out of lamps after the third door, and Gage walked into the dark part with his left hand on the wall.

Camilla stood at the lit end and didn't follow. She'd found him here twice this week by not finding him anywhere else.

His lips moved. She could see the shape of the numbers, and his hand lifting off the wall and coming down a stride later, flat, the way it would come down on a timber prop. The ring's walls were poured and bare and one grey all the way along, and he laid his palm on them one by one, forty strides into the dark, and each was a prop he'd put his hand on under the Hub, coming out at shift's end with the lamp dying.

At one place he stepped over nothing. A lift of the knee, a longer stride, his weight coming down soft on the far side. The drain channel. He knew where it was to the inch, in a corridor it had never been in.

Then he stopped, head down, hand on the wall, and stood there until she'd counted to thirty and quit.

The junction. He'd walked the run and arrived.

He came back not counting, one hand trailing the wall and the other open at his side. When he reached the light he looked at her, and she saw he'd known she was there from the first stride.

"Forty-one," he said. "The one before the junction's bad. Pappy flagged it the year he retired. They'll have left it."

"They'll have left it."

He went past her. His hands were open; she checked. He checked too.

---

Ben had a stool in the screen room now. The man with the headset had brought it in on the second day and set it beside his own without a word.

"There," Ben said. The man nodded and didn't look at the screen Ben meant, which was how Camilla knew Ben was right. "Him. Looks down at his boots when the bell goes, and doesn't look up till the lift's full." His hand had gone to his hip, over the pocket that wasn't there. "That's eleven seconds nobody's watching that door."

"Nine on a cold night," the man said. "He stamps."

"Nine." Ben's lips moved; he was putting it somewhere. "And the woman at the top of the lift turns her back to count heads. Twice. That's the long one."

"That is the long one."

He was good at it. He wasn't watching people now. He was watching the holes between them, where a man looked at his boots and the city stopped looking at the ground, and he found them faster than the man who'd sat here for years.

He turned on the stool. He'd felt her there the way Gage had.

"I know what every one of them does at shift's end," he said. "The eleven that watch the Hub. Which one's got a bad knee. Which one's going to be sick before winter." He pressed his hip. "I didn't want to know that about anybody."

"You'd have found it in a queue."

"That's not better, Cami."

"No."

On the bottom row, the gates. A brown coat, a scarf, a pale worn patch under two feet. The first bell hadn't gone, and she was already there.

---

Flitzberg brought the glass himself.

Two bulbs of old glass gone faintly green, in a dark wood frame worn pale at the top where a century of thumbs had turned it. Grey sand. Ten minutes, he said, and set it at the end of Gage's model, unturned.

"I am the ring," he said. "I answer on Camilla's word and no one else's. I do not think. I do not hesitate. I do not correct. If she says it, it is done."

The model lay where it always lay. Clay basin. Splinter wall, the middle one split a hair. The feeds down the right side; she'd learned to call them the feeds, because the valves were the ring's and the feeds were theirs. The Hub, the veins, the pods. The shelf high and dry on the far side with a scrap of grey cloth on it for the Junkyard.

Gage stood over it with his hands flat on the bench on either side. Ben had the stool, pulled round to the end where the screen room would be. Camilla stood where she could see all three of them, and the glass.

"Ready."

Flitzberg turned it. The sand began.

Ben's eyes went flat and far. He was watching eleven men she couldn't see. "Bell. Lift's loading. Boots." His lips moved. "Lift's up."

Gage's finger had gone to the Hub and was walking up the run. One prop, two. She could count faster than he could walk it, and she made herself not.

"Heads," Ben said. "She's counting. Twice." A breath. "Clear. Hub's clear."

"Feeds," Camilla said.

"The feeds are closed."

Gage's finger reached the wall and rested on the split splinter. "Strike. Hold. Count forty off the kick—" and his lips went, and she watched the sand. "Thirty-nine. Forty. Again—"

"Feeds."

"Closed."

"Strike. Hold." The finger pressed. "Count—" His lips stopped, and started again a number lower than they'd stopped at. "Lost it. I lost one."

"Say it anyway."

"No." He took the finger off the wall. "In the dark I'd have lost it the same. Go again."

Flitzberg laid the glass flat and the grey ran back, and nobody spoke until it had.

---

Second run, Ben hesitated.

"Heads. She's counting." A breath. Two. Gage's finger waited at the thirty-second prop. "There's one still at the lift. He's gone back for—" His hand pressed his hip. "He's on. Clear. Hub's clear. Sorry. Clear."

"Feeds." "Closed." The finger went on up the run and arrived at the wall and pressed, and the count held this time, forty and forty, and the sand ran out with his finger on the splinter.

"Late," Ben said, before anyone could. "Four seconds. I saw him get on and I still waited."

"You weren't sure."

"I was sure. I waited anyway."

Gage took his finger off the wall. "Again."

---

Third run was hers.

She'd heard *twice* and the breath after it, and Gage's finger was at the thirty-ninth prop and all of it lined up the way a bearing lines up when the race is true, and she said, "Feeds."

"Closed," Flitzberg said. He didn't look at Ben or at her. He'd said he wouldn't think, and he didn't.

Ben's mouth was still open round the *clear* he hadn't said.

Gage's finger stopped two props short of the wall.

"I jumped it." The metal hand had closed at her side, and she opened it. "I called on the shape, not the word."

"The ring answers on your word," Flitzberg said. "Right or wrong. That is the whole of what it does, and why the word is yours."

"I know whose word it is."

Gage's finger finished the run anyway. It went the two props and sat on the split splinter, and pressed, and the sand ran.

She'd stopped watching the glass. She went back over three runs and found what she'd known the first time and let herself not know: lost count, late word, early call, and every one finished with her brother's forefinger on the one piece of wood with a crack in it, pressed, waiting for the kick.

He wasn't doing it on purpose. The finger went where the run went, and the run ended at the wall.

Ben had the stillness on him, the one that came when a pattern finished.

Neither of them said anything, and Gage said, "Again."

---

Fourth run.

"Bell. Boots. Lift's up." Ben's voice had gone level. "Heads. Twice." The breath. "Clear."

"Feeds."

"Closed."

"Strike. Hold." Forty, clean. "Again."

"Feeds."

"Closed."

"Strike. Hold." Forty. The sand at the neck. "Again."

"Feeds."

"Closed."

"Strike—" and the last grain went through. "She's through. Wall's through."

Flitzberg set the glass on its side.

"Clean," Camilla said. It was. Every beat had fallen where it should. Her voice did what it was meant to do.

Gage lifted his finger off the splinter. There was a mark on the pad of it, a small pale line where the split edge had pressed. He looked at it and put the hand flat on the bench, open.

A clean run meant it worked. A clean run meant that in twelve nights, somewhere under the Hub, someone would stand where that finger had stood, and say *struck*, and *again*, and the ring would hear them and the water would come.

They had never once talked about who. They didn't now. Ben went to the sink and ran the green tap longer than anyone needed to. Flitzberg took his glass away under his arm. Gage began to take the model apart, the wall first. He always did the wall first.

---

The crystal stood in the room where they'd woken, and the room had been left as it was.

She hadn't gone back, and had found there was a kind of need in not. Now Ben had been missing an hour, and the door with glass in it stood open, and he was sitting on the floor inside with his knees up, looking at the crystal. Flitzberg stood with his hands folded. He inclined his head to her and said nothing, and she took that as the room's rules and sat down on the floor a yard from Ben.

Three quarters green. The last quarter pulsing blue. The floor was the same cold it had been.

"Don't talk yet," Ben said. He was counting it the way he counted faces, fingers spread on his knee, pressing them down one at a time and starting over.

She counted it herself. Six beats between brights. Six. Seven. Seven. Eight.

It was slowing.

"Nine," Ben said. "It'll go to twelve. Then it comes back, and down again. About as long as it takes to walk from here to the tank and back." He looked at her. "It's warm and cool, Cami. It's a long slow in and out. It's breathing."

The blue went to ten.

She had it before he'd finished, the way she'd had the pump's fault before Pappy pointed: warm and cool, slow, a tide going in and out over the whole length of a boy's body. She'd stood in that doorway for eighteen nights and watched the gel go pale and deep and pale, and never once timed it, because it had been the tank's business, and the tank was a machine.

"It keeps time with the tank."

Ben's count stopped.

"Or with him," he said. "The tank's set to him. The medic said." He stopped. "I don't know which one's the clock."

They both looked at Flitzberg.

"Nor do I," he said. "This office has owned that crystal since it was found, and this office has never known what the fourth quarter answers to. It has been blue since before I was born. I will not offer you a mechanism I do not have."

"It's not a machine," Camilla said. She didn't know she thought it until she'd said it.

"No. I have never believed it was."

The blue went to twelve, and held, and came back faster.

Ben watched it come. Then, in the voice he used when the first subject had got too big: "I was looking at your books. The wall in your rooms. The four houses."

"I noticed."

"Every leak bends round the same place. There's a point in the middle of all four that nothing touches, and every line that gets near it goes round."

"An old astronomers' lesson," Flitzberg said. "A black hole is found only by the way light bends around what cannot be seen."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It is a description. It is the one I have."

The blue was at six. Camilla got up, and the metal hand pushed her up a shade before her good one had decided to, and she went to find out which clock it was.

---

Ben's hour was the late one.

The tank room kept its light down. The gel went pale, and deeper, and pale, and Otto lay in it with his eyes open and his hair floating off his forehead in a way that would have made him laugh if any part of him could see it. The old medic sat at the side with a flat grey slate on her knee. The screen above the tank showed its one green line, flat, and under it the lines that were his heart and his breath and were not.

Camilla stood in the doorway. The frame had a place that fit her shoulder now.

"Gage did the run today," Ben said to the tank. "Clean. Fourth go. You'd have hated the glass. It's got sand in it, you can hear it."

The gap. The exact length of a sentence.

"Cami jumped the call on the third one. She'll tell you she didn't."

Gap.

"I was late on the second. Four seconds. There was a man at the lift and I knew he was on and I waited anyway. You'd have said—"

The line moved.

A rise, one, the height of a fingernail, a small round hill in the middle of a flat green road, and then the road again. The gel was going from pale to deep when it happened, or from deep to pale, and she would spend the rest of the night unable to say which.

Ben had stopped on *said*. His mouth was still shaped for it.

She'd seen it because she'd looked at that screen every night for eighteen nights, the way you check a gauge that's never moved because the one night you don't is the night it will. She had counted it, and it had been one.

She looked at the medic.

The medic had not looked up. Her hand moved on the slate, once, a short stroke, and came to rest, and she sat as she had sat and said nothing, and Camilla understood that *exact* meant you wrote down one rise and did not call it anything.

Ben breathed in.

"You'd have said," he began again, and said the whole sentence over from where it started, *I was late on the second, four seconds*, every word in its place, and left the gap, and Camilla counted it and it was the same length as every gap he had ever left.

The line was flat.

He waited past it. He had never done that. He sat with his hands between his knees and his eyes on the gel, and waited through the length of another sentence and another, and nothing came.

Then he went on. The next sentence. The gap.

She stepped back out of the doorway.

The corridor woke ahead of her, one light and then the next. Two levels down, Gage would be at his bench with the box open, building the wall. He'd have the middle splinter in by now and split with his thumbnail, and his finger would be resting on it without his sending it.

Twelve nights. One rise in the green.

She could go down and say *the line moved*, three words, and watch his hands do whatever they'd do. Or she could sit on the stool and lay her metal hand flat on the bench beside his until the wrist stopped lagging, which took no time at all now, which she hadn't told him either.

It was one. You didn't call a pattern on one. She'd told Ben that herself, over a dish of water, about a needle. You waited for two.

She went down. She sat on the stool. He looked up, and she looked at his finger, which was on the wall, and she said nothing, and he went back to building, and she counted the gap she was leaving and could not find the end of it.
