# Chapter 20: The Proof

*"The answer is in front of you, like it always was. Don't fight it. Embrace it and channel its energy as your own. Adapt, son. Adapt."*

— Book 1, Chapter 24: The Green Room

## Then and now: The proof

Rohan told her standing, with his back to her, because he hadn't been able to make himself sit.

At night the dead room had no shaft-light to arrive late. A lantern stood on the table with a true flame behind its glass, and the four panels on the wall had gone brown at their edges in it. The brass pin Baju had pushed into the fourth panel with his thumb still sat a hair crooked, the way he'd left it. Rohan stood in front of that one.

"Baju is dead," he said.

He said it to the wall. Then he said the rest, in the order the notice had given it to him, in the voice of a man reading from a page: the Drona tower, the fourth-level corridor, the open panel, the line test, the hand on the steel. No witnesses. A maintenance interval written over the hour. And the one thing the notice hadn't listed, which he'd found by its absence.

"It took his paper," he said. "It stood over him and reached inside his coat and took the one thing that could remember what it had done."

It. Even now. He'd made the rule in this room, no he, no she, nothing that would let the hunt settle early on one shape of person, and he was keeping it with his friend dead, because a rule you drop when it hurts was never a rule.

Elara's throat shut on the word that wanted out of it, and she let it stay shut. The panels swam and steadied. He hadn't sent for her for that. His own people would comfort him. He wanted someone who'd do something.

"He had a counter," Rohan said. He still hadn't turned. "Brass. He timed everything. Steps. Lifts. How long a door took to close behind him. He'd have known to the second how long it took me to read the notice." His thumb moved against his forefinger, the way a man works a thing that isn't there. "It's on its way home in a bag with his coat."

She crossed to the wall, stood in front of the fourth panel beside him, and read Nayan's level hand-copy of the Annex approval until her voice was her own.

"Tell me what the notebook held."

Baju had reported twice since the dead room, by paper, by hand. Sensor gaps. Lift holds. Door cycles that logged nothing. Weeks of them, each with a floor and an hour, pulled from maintenance records nobody but a maintenance hand ever read. He'd been collecting the places where the city's instruments had been told to look away, believing the blind spots would make a shape, and the shape would have a center. His last report had said he was close. It hadn't said where.

"And he found it."

"He found something. He died in a service corridor with his hand on a panel and his notebook gone. I don't know what else that means."

Elara nodded slowly. She was thinking about the method: a man who had understood that you can't find a thing that erases itself by looking for the thing. You look for the erasing. It was the best idea anyone in this hunt had had, and it had cost a life, and it had gone into a stranger's hands.

"Then we are not going to do it again," she said.

Rohan turned.

"We're not sending another person to crouch beside a blank and wait for it to happen to them. It knows that's how it was found. It'll never let itself be found that way twice." She went back to the table and put both palms flat on it. "Baju's method was right. The blanks are the trail. He was reading them one panel at a time, by hand, in a tower with a thousand panels. I want all of them. Every instrument in the city that's ever been told to look away, in one place, at once. Then the shape isn't something a man has to die to see. It's a map."

"No such record exists. If an instrument's told to forget, it forgets. That's the whole of the problem."

"The instrument forgets. I'm not sure Drona does."

He waited.

"The witness copies. Anandi taught me the old path in, one file at a time. I don't know if the mirrors keep what an instrument is told. I do know the whole archive, opened at once across every house, isn't something a mentor can grant. One person in Drona can, and she's the same person who put me under a review code and never said how far down it reached." Her nails pressed white against the wood. "I think she knows exactly how far. I think she's been waiting for me to ask."

"Your Grandmaster."

"Tonight. And you're coming with me."

Rohan's face didn't change, but something behind it did. "An Ashoka commander doesn't walk into Drona's inner chamber."

"Then tonight one does. Listen." She picked up the lantern and set it down between them so he had to look at her through it. "Whatever she gives us, she'll want it never to leave that room, and she'll want the word of everyone who'll act on it. I can give her mine. I can't give her Ashoka's. If I bring it back to you, you're bound by a promise you never made, to a face you never saw, and the first time you have to decide whether to break it, you'll be deciding about my word, not yours. That's no way to hold a secret that gets people killed. If Ashoka's going to be bound, Ashoka gives its own word. To her face."

She let go of the lantern.

"And there's Baju. You chose him, briefed him, sent him, and read his death. I'm not standing in front of my Grandmaster and telling that secondhand. You're the only door in that body. She hears it from the door."

He said nothing for a long while. Then he reached past her and pinched out the true flame between his thumb and forefinger, the way the Dhammapala put out her lamp, and the dead room went back to being dark.

"Lead," he said.

---

The Ancient One received them in the chamber beneath the archive where she had once stopped Elara's breath with a lifted hand.

The note had gone by Anandi's own hand, sealed, three lines long. *An officer of Ashoka is dead on Drona's ground. I request the witness archive, whole. Commander Rohan of Ashoka comes with me or I do not come.* Elara hadn't expected an answer before morning. It had come in an hour.

Three people at the long table. The Grandmaster sat at its head, small and upright, her hands folded. Sarvajña sat at her right with the seven stones at his wrist. Anandi stood behind them both at a console Elara had never seen lit, its keys worn into shallow dishes by thumbs that had been dead a hundred years. Her face was blank, which with Anandi meant she had argued for something and lost.

Nobody asked why there was an Ashoka officer in the room. Nobody offered a chair. Elara stood beside Rohan, not in front of him.

"He'll tell you what happened," she said. "Then I'll tell you what I want."

Rohan told it without names, without the body's working names, without anything the wall held. A man of his had found a way to read the city's blind spots, and had followed them to a corridor, and had died there with his hand on steel, and the record of his death was a maintenance interval. In order, without ornament, to the end. When he finished, the Ancient One was looking at Elara.

"And what you want."

"The witness copies. All of them, at once, instead of one file at a time through the old prompt. The whole archive, searched for one thing: every order ever given to an instrument in this city to look away. If the Sākṣī kept those orders, the hand's been drawing its own map for months and leaving it in Drona's basement. If it didn't, I've cost a commander of Ashoka a night, and we go back to paper."

The silence that followed was the silence of people deciding how much to say.

Sarvajña broke it, and he spoke to the Grandmaster. "She has asked the question. We agreed that if it was ever asked, we would not lie."

"We agreed that if it was ever asked, we would answer it. We did not agree on how much." The Ancient One unfolded her hands. "Monitor Nathan. Commander. What I am about to tell you is forbidden because it is dangerous to Drona. If it leaves this room in any form that can be traced here, this house falls, and the balance between the four falls with it. You will use what I give you. You will never cite it. Anything you find by it, you will find again by some other road before you act on it. Are those terms understood."

"Yes," Elara said.

The Ancient One did not look away from Rohan. "The question was for you, Commander. The monitor is mine; her oath to this house already binds her. You owe this house nothing. Your house will act on what you hear tonight, and your house will never be told where it came from, and if that is ever discovered, it will be Ashoka's commander who kept Drona's secret from Ashoka. Can you carry that."

"My own Grandmaster told me there are safeguards in this city older than Ashoka, and that she wouldn't name them, and that if the balance was kind I'd never need to know what they were." He held the old woman's eyes. "I've carried that from her without asking. I can carry this from you without telling. Yes."

"Then sit."

They sat.

"What Drona teaches its own about the mirrors is true and incomplete," the Ancient One said. "A scholar's caution: one copy kept apart from the hand that wrote the other. The archive did not grow to the size of a city to settle disputes. The disputes came later, and were a convenient thing to say."

"Then why did it grow?" Elara said.

"As the price of something else." Sarvajña took it up the way a man takes up a weight he has carried before. "After the six days of fighting at the rebuilding, Kubera came to Drona with a commission. It wanted a guard. Men can be bribed, men can be seen, men take sides. It wanted one instrument that could walk the city in a man's hand and be obeyed by every door, every ledger, every sensor, in every house, without any house being able to say it had been commanded."

"Who built it?"

"Kubera wrote the authority. The founder-tier permission that every instrument in the city still honors, beneath every rule laid over it since. Drona built the reader."

"The reader."

"The part that listens to the hand." His fingers rested on the seven stones. "A band that lies against the wrist and reads the nerves of the forearm, the small signals a body sends before it moves, and turns them into commands in whatever dialect each house's instruments use for trust. It does not read a mind. It reads a hand, a heartbeat before the hand knows what it means to do."

"And Kubera called it what?"

"The office it served was the Obsidian Guard. It wore a black band."

"OG," Rohan said.

"OG."

Elara's hands had gone cold on the table. "It's existed since the founding."

"It existed for one generation," the Ancient One said. "Then the four houses saw what they had made. A guard that cannot be seen cannot be answered. Whoever wore it was accountable to no one, not even to the house that wrote its permission."

"So they sealed it."

"The four Grandmasters of that generation locked the band in a box of four parts, struck its dialect from every instrument's instruction, and swore to each other that it was finished. Kubera swore it kept the box only as a relic. Drona swore it had destroyed the plans for the reader, and had kept no record of the Guard's commands."

"Then why does the archive still work?" Elara said. "Why's there anything for me to search?"

"Drona lied," Sarvajña said.

The two words sat in the room, and nobody picked them up.

"We kept the plans," the Ancient One said. "That is the smaller lie. The larger is this. When our engineers built the reader, they built it the way Drona built everything in that century: with a witness. Every command the Guard ever gave, every door it opened, every ledger it moved, every sensor it told to forget. The instrument obeyed and wrote nothing. The command itself was mirrored. The order, written in parallel into the Sākṣī by a channel the Guard did not know existed. For one generation Drona kept a ledger of everything the Guard was ever told to do. When the Guard was sealed, that channel was not. It has been listening to silence for a century."

Elara understood a heartbeat before the Ancient One said it.

"It is not listening to silence anymore."

Anandi touched the console.

The wall behind the Grandmaster lit with a plain grey archive screen, rows and columns and timestamps, the dullest thing Elara had ever been glad to see. Each row was an instrument somewhere in the city, named by house and floor. Beside it a time. Beside the time, the order it had been given. The orders were all one order, written four ways. *Look away. Record nothing. Maintenance interval. No personnel.*

Months of them.

The oldest were all inside Kubera, dozens close together: someone practicing. Then they moved. Down into Drona's monitor tower. To a floor Elara knew. To a room on that floor she had walked past a hundred times and never once thought about. Then out across the city in lines: a Chanakya conservatory one night, at nine; the archive wing another; transit platforms, service lifts, corridors. Each line was a walk. Each walk had been invisible to every eye in the city except this one, and this one had been shut in a basement and forgotten.

"Four evenings ago," Rohan said, and pointed, and didn't need to say the rest. The fourth-level corridor of the Drona tower, night after night. And tonight, the same corridor, the same hour, and beside it an order unlike any of the others. *Line test.*

That was Baju, dying in a column.

Rohan looked at it until Elara thought he would never stop. Then he said, in a voice that was almost conversational, "It is not a blank. It never was. It is a signature."

"Yes," the Ancient One said.

"Every time it tells an instrument to forget, this remembers that it asked."

"Yes. And now you understand why no one may ever learn that this room exists." The Ancient One let the grey wall speak before she went on. "Look past the Guard's walks. For a hundred years, every order given to an instrument in Kubera, or Chanakya, or Ashoka has come here, to Drona, without their knowing. The balance between the four rests on each believing the others are blind in the same places. We have not been blind. A house that has watched the other three in secret for a century has stopped being a house. It is a tyranny that has not yet been noticed, and the day it is noticed, it falls." She folded her hands again. "You asked me, Monitor Nathan, how far down your review code reaches. This far. Use it."

"One thing more," Sarvajña said, "because you will need it. The band reads a hand a heartbeat before the hand would move. That makes it fast, and quiet, and it makes it one thing more, which the four who sealed it understood. It is only as calm as the hand that wears it. A careful hand wearing the Guard is invisible. A frightened hand wearing the Guard is a weapon that fires itself. The makers wrote it down a century ago, and I have read it: the band acts on fear as readily as on intent, and faster, because fear is the louder signal. If your man came close enough to be truly seen, then the hand did not need to decide anything. It only needed to be afraid of him." He looked at Rohan. "You will not find this thing while it is careful. You will find it when it is afraid."

Rohan's jaw set along the old scar and stayed set.

Elara turned back to the wall. The columns were still filling; the query was live, and Anandi had left it running. As she watched, a new line appeared at the bottom, with tonight's date and an hour only minutes old.

Meru. A household panel on the upper terraces. *Look away.*

A second, two terraces over. *Look away.*

"The hand's up there now," Elara said. "Right now. It's walking."

Nobody in the room moved. On the grey wall, in the ugliest font in the city, the Obsidian Guard went about its evening's work, and for once someone was watching it do so.

Then she saw the other column.

It had begun filling at the far right of the wall while everyone's eyes were on Meru, and nobody had asked for it. She counted before she understood what she was counting: nine entries in the time the Meru lines took to make two. None of them said look away. They said almost nothing. *Open. Close. Open.* Orders to an instrument's own joints, not to any door or sensor in the city, each one paired with a second stamp that followed it half a breath late, and the lateness shrinking as the column went down, as if whatever was asking and whatever was answering were learning each other's pace. Someone was learning a hand. And where every other row on the wall carried a house and a floor, the location field on these was empty. No house. No floor. Nothing.

She opened her mouth.

Across the table the Ancient One's eyes went to the column a breath after Elara's had, and something in her face closed, like a door someone has remembered to shut. She said nothing. Anandi's hand did not move toward the console. Sarvajña had not looked up at all. Nobody explained it, and Elara understood she wasn't going to be told. She couldn't cite the wall; whatever she found by it she'd have to find again by some other road. So she did the one thing the terms left her. She counted the column to its end, fourteen, and kept the number where she kept things.

Meru at night was the quietest place Daren had ever stood, and tonight the quiet was unbearable.

He stood in a public gallery above the lifted-water gardens. The water went up before it came down here: pumped to the top terrace and let fall in steps toward the lights of the three cities below, so that on Meru even water was made to climb before it was allowed to rest. The stewards' households sat behind their long windows, each with its panel glowing in a study or a hall, and the two he wanted were in his sight. The closer he stood, the fewer places the band had to ask to look away. And nobody on Meru looked at anyone else. A man standing alone at a railing was the worst kind of manners to notice. Tonight that was cover.

The work was simple. Open the first household's panel through the lines the band kept warm. Place a briefing note in the councilman's morning queue, under the staff's own header, in the staff's own dull phrasing. Close. Leave nothing. Two terraces over, again.

He couldn't make his hand be still.

He'd put his right hand on the railing to steady it and it wouldn't steady. A fine tremor had started in the corridor and had not stopped on the Skylark or the maglev or the long walk up through the terraces, and every time his eyes fell on the black band at his wrist he saw a man's open eyes.

He'd made himself turn away from them. The turning hadn't worked the way turning away usually worked, because the eyes had come with him, up the lift, across the dark between platforms, into this garden full of climbing water. He saw the man exactly as he'd left him: on his side, eyes open on nothing, one boot half off his foot. Of all of it, the boot was the thing he couldn't stop returning to. Such a small, undignified thing. It made the man a person.

The band lay warm against his wrist, and it was listening to the tremor.

That was worse than the tremor itself. He knew now what the band did with a frightened hand. He was standing in a garden on Meru with a frightened hand, and every nerve in his forearm was telling the band the same confused story, flinch, grip, get away, and the band was reading all of it a heartbeat early, the way it read everything. If he let the fear run, it would act on something. A steward's terminal. A garden pump. A lift. Someone else's hand on someone else's steel.

So Daren stood at the railing and did the hardest thing he had done since a chamber with black doors: nothing at all, on purpose.

He breathed. He made his shoulders drop. He unclenched the hand on the railing one finger at a time, the way a man opens a hand that has been holding something too long, and thought of nothing, not the councilmen, not the vote, not the eyes, only the hand, and the air around it, and the band growing cooler against his wrist as the nerves beneath it quieted. It took a long time. A moth found the nearest panel's glow and beat at the window between them and couldn't understand why. Somewhere below, a steward's household dimmed its lights for the night and didn't know that a man in the gallery above had just spent all his strength making sure nothing in their house did anything it wasn't told to.

When the tremor was gone, he did the work.

He did it differently than he would have four nights ago. He didn't let the band carry a wish and finish it. He keyed each step himself, slowly, holding the stillness like a weight: the household line, the panel, the queue, the note, the close. The band carried what he gave it and nothing more, because he gave it nothing more. The first panel took him a quarter of an hour. It should have taken a breath. He didn't care. Then the second.

When it was done he stayed at the railing, because he wasn't sure his legs would carry him and because there was nowhere he wanted to go.

He'd wanted to be the force the city arranged itself around. The Grandmaster had promised him that, and the band had made it true. He'd never once asked what a force did to the things it moved. A force didn't get to choose. It moved what was in its path. He had called that power. It had only ever been the absence of anyone able to tell him no.

He looked down at the band. It was cool now. It was waiting. It would do, tonight and tomorrow and for as long as he wore it, exactly what his hand told it, including the things his hand told it that he never heard.

He couldn't take it off. Without it he was a man with no house, no name and no door in the city that would open for him, a man the Grandmaster had emptied out and then filled with this. He'd made sure of that himself, one correction at a time, until the instrument was the only part of him that still did anything. Take the band away and there was nothing underneath.

So he could only be still. Still enough, every hour of every day, that it never again heard anything he didn't mean.

Below him, on a terrace he couldn't see, a councilman's panel accepted a routine briefing note from his own staff and filed it for the morning. Further below, in a chamber beneath Drona's archive that he had never heard of, two lines appeared on a grey wall with his hour on them, and someone read them.

Daren turned from the railing and went to find the Skylark, slowly, with his right hand held still at his side, like a man carrying something that could spill.

The butterfly came the way it always came, from nowhere.

Blue on blue. He nearly missed it. Then its wings opened and closed, slow, the way they'd opened and closed on an old man's shoulder in a yard behind a shed, and the yard built itself around them before Otto had finished knowing where he was: packed earth, the shed's leaning wall, the heat of a Miner Town afternoon that had been dead for years. Master Chan sat cross-legged in the dust with his back straight and his hands open on his knees. The cup of not-tea stood beside him with its skin on. A line of ants crossed his shin and he let them. The butterfly settled on his shoulder.

"You are very still," Master Chan said.

"You taught me to be."

"I did. You were a loud boy. All fists. I taught you the fist is the last thing, and you learned it better than I did." The old man's eyes were kind, the way a good teacher's are, which has nothing to do with gentle. "I have watched you hold those stones. I have never seen anyone go so deep."

"I can't come back up."

"No."

"I keep doing what you taught me. I stop fighting. I match it. I let it carry me." His voice cracked, which it hadn't done since he was twelve. "That's how I got out of the green room. You said adapt. I adapted. Now there's nothing left to adapt to. It's just me, and I'm the room, and I can't find the edge of myself to climb out of."

Master Chan was quiet a while. The butterfly opened and closed.

"I told you to adapt because the room was stronger than you, and a man who fights a stronger thing head-on breaks. That was true then." He leaned forward, and Otto saw that the old man wasn't calm. He was urgent. "Look where you are, Otto. There is no room. There is no stronger thing. You have adapted to yourself so completely that you have disappeared into it. You cannot match this. It is already your own. That is the whole of the trap."

"Then what do I do?"

"What I never taught you, because you never needed it." The open hands closed, slowly, into fists on his knees. "You fight yourself. The stillness you built is a wall, and you built it well, and you are on the wrong side of it. The only way through a wall you built is to break it. It will feel like dying. It will feel like betraying everything I taught you. Do it anyway."

"I don't know how."

"Yes, you do. You have known since you were a loud boy with fists. Start small. One thing. Do not try to wake. Do not try to breathe differently. Do not try to find the way up." He turned his right hand over on his knee. "When this hand locked on me, years before you, I did not force it. I opened it in my mind, one finger, an hour at a time, until the hand believed me." He held the hand up, and slowly, deliberately, moved one finger. "This. Everything else is the room. This is you. Find it. Fight for it."

The yard was already going. The shed's wall thinned. The heat went out of the afternoon. The butterfly rose off the old man's shoulder and hung in the air between them, blue in the failing blue, and Master Chan's voice came from farther and farther away.

"It is not time to adapt, son. It is time to fight."

Then there was only the blue, and the pull in his arms that came and went, and the sound that might have been his heart.

Otto fought.

There was nothing to fight against, so he fought the nothing. He wanted his hand. One hand, either hand, wherever it was. He wanted it the way he'd wanted the stones to stay up, with everything, and the stillness took the wanting and made it part of the calm, because that was what he'd built it to do, and he was more still than before, and further down, and the blue was warmer.

He understood, somewhere in it, that this was how he would die. In peace. Adapted all the way to the bottom.

He thought of Ben's hands on glass, flat, fingers spread. He didn't know how he knew about that. He knew it. He thought of Gage standing in a doorway with his hands open on purpose. He thought of Camilla with one arm, saying get back to the panel. He thought of a rope let down a shaft, taut, a knot at every arm's length, going somewhere above him. And of Gage at the hole with his voice breaking on it: *Promise me.*

He'd said he would. He hadn't quit.

An old man in a yard, holding up one finger. *This is you.*

He stopped trying to break the whole wall. He found one brick.

One finger. The first finger of the right hand. He didn't try to feel it. He didn't try to find it. He poured everything he had left into the single, stupid, animal demand that it bend, him and nothing else, and he held the demand the way he'd once held ten stones over a floor of fire, past the point where anything in him said he could, and past that, and past that.

The stillness held.

He held.

The stillness held.

Blood from his nose, somewhere far above, in a body he couldn't feel. He held. The butterfly, blue in the blue, wings opening and closing. He held. An old man's voice: fight. He held, and held, and held—

And something gave.

It was very small. It was the smallest thing a body can do.

In the observation room, the medic on the night watch looked up from her reading because one of the lines on the panel beside the tank had done something it had done only twice before, and never like this. The two rises she had written down and called nothing had been a fingernail thick. This one climbed, and stayed up, and while she was still looking at it, it climbed again.

She stood. She crossed to the glass. The boy hung in the gel as he always hung, eyes closed, mask in place, arms a little away from his sides. The cycle was in its cool phase; nothing was asking his muscles to work.

Then she looked at his right hand, and stopped breathing.

The first finger had curled. On its own. Against nothing. And as she watched, a single thread of red left the boy's nose and unspooled upward through the blue, the way smoke rises in still air, and the finger, slowly, with what she would later swear was effort, curled again.

She watched it curl a third time. Then she did what the office had trained her to do. She marked the time on the panel, noted the phase of the cycle, and keyed the night alert for the room two levels down. Her eyes went to the three names printed beside the alert key, the three who had asked to be woken for anything, and stayed on them a second longer than the key needed.

Then she went back to the glass. She had sat with enough of the unconscious to know the order things came back in: a finger first, then the eyes, sometimes within the hour, sometimes not for days. There was no telling which this would be. But someone should be standing there if it was the hour, and there was no one else awake to do it.
