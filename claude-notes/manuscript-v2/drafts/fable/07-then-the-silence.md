## Then: Day Four

The packet was waiting on the study table when the door closed behind her.

She bathed because the schedule said to. She drank the nutrient intake the dispenser had measured for her. The room lowered its lights on its own, and she sat in the half-dark with the restricted folder and broke the seal.

*Cohort 3A. Behavioral Correlation Assessment. Elara Nathan.*

Auto-generated at 02:13. At 02:13 she had been pinned at VARIANCE under the shift lock with the Guard's engines idling in her ear, and somewhere below her feet a routine had finished writing a report about the thing she was watching and addressed it to her. Gage's pulse profile. Ben's stress spikes. Otto's proximity data. A line near the end, in the system's flattest voice, noting synchronized response. The system had seen it before she admitted it. It had only lacked a category, and she had given it one, and then gone down into the vaults and taken it away again.

She turned the page. The letters stayed where they were. She turned another.

Vishnu had taught her karma at his scripture cabinet, one hand lifting the warped door so it would close: the shape a choice makes after it leaves the person who made it.

Her head dipped. She jerked it up. The room smelled of cold metal and broth, and the clock had moved. Her forehead came down on the table, and the folder slid half-open under her hand.

She woke with her cheek on the cool surface and the tip of her nose against the paper. A thin line of saliva had cooled along her jaw. She didn't feel it.

At 5:00 a.m., three measured strikes sounded against the door.

---

She stayed still.

No name from the reader. No clearance tone. The knock came again, harder, and then a third time, somebody who had been told how many and was counting.

Elara pushed herself up. The sleeve of her sleep shirt carried a pale crescent of table dust. She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, crossed the room, and put her palm to the reader.

Two Trinity guards stood in the corridor, dark uniforms, collars fastened to the throat. The one on the left had the trained blankness of a man delivering orders he hadn't written. The one on the right was younger than she was. His collar sat one notch too tight and had left a red line under his jaw, and his eyes went to the floor seam, the wall panel, anywhere that wasn't her face.

"Get dressed," the older one said.

Elara kept a hand on the door. "Why?"

"Drona review committee summons." The younger guard said it to the panel beside her head. "Conference room. Eight hundred hours."

The older one held out a thin black card. White text surfaced on it as her fingers closed.

08:00 - DRONA REVIEW CONFERENCE ROOM

"You will report directly to the committee. You will not return to the monitoring floor before the review."

Her thumb left a damp print on the card. "Is this an inquiry?"

Neither answered. The younger one looked past her at the open folder and the dent her cheek had left in the papers, and swallowed, and the red line at his throat moved with it.

"Get dressed, Monitor Nathan."

The seal closed.

She dressed without the full lights. Uniform. Collar. Seal. She tried sentences on under her breath, *procedural judgment, incomplete evidence, candidate safety*, and each one sounded like a door being held open with a foot.

At 7:00 her fingers missed the collar hook on the second pass and caught on the metal edge. She stood with her hand at her throat until it stopped shaking, seated the hook, and went out.

At the turn into the Corridor of Knowledge the architecture changed without getting grander. Drona didn't need height. The corridor ran narrow and pale between sealed glass cases: a cracked lens, bone-handled tools used to read the stars before Trinity owned a sky, portraits of scientists hung between shrines to the old sanyasis, and the oldest books lying open in pairs a casual eye would call careless, an astronomical text against a religious epic, a medical manual against a field notebook on plant behavior. The disorder was the point. Knowledge had never advanced in a straight line.

She slowed at the third case.

The brass measuring arm lay inside on faded velvet, its hinge worn bright where a dead man's thumb had worked it, one thumbscrew long ago replaced with a bent length of wire. She had drawn it as a student, three sessions a week, standing where she stood now with a pencil and a board, until she could render the hinge from memory. Her right hand curled on its own, index finger and thumb closing on a pencil that wasn't there, and the small familiar weight of nothing in her grip steadied her more than the sentences had.

She knew the rest without looking. Which scientist died before publishing. Which monk copied a manuscript through three winters. Every object behind the glass asked the same thing of her: what did you do with what you knew?

The corridor narrowed before it ended and opened into the conference wing, where the rooms still kept the old shape of the gurukul. In the first, young researchers stood around a suspended river model while an elder put questions to them and gave back none of the answers. In the second, two students were restoring a damaged painting, and their mentor guided the angle of a brush with two fingers laid on the back of a hand, and said nothing at all.

Elara didn't slow. Truth in those rooms was being made the long way, corrected and earned under an eye. She had made hers in one unauthorized minute at an unattended terminal.

The door of the review room was clear glass. The silhouettes were already seated.

She stepped in, and the relief that went through her was so sharp it nearly bent her at the shoulders. Elder Anandi sat at the long table, whose lectures on observational ethics had shaped Elara's first year. Beside her, Mentor Kavik, who had taught her to tell pattern from impulse, his hands folded on the wood the way they'd been folded above her exercise sheets. For one breath she believed they'd see the shape of what she'd been carrying.

Then the room widened past them.

Three strangers held the chairs nearest the center. The first wore a long robe of saffron and ash, wrong in a house that had always kept learning out of doctrine's hands. The robe she could have argued with. The hand she couldn't. His right wrist rested on the table, the cuff threaded with seven small stones in a narrow line: four for the Vedas, three for interpretation, silence, and living transmission. He wore his discipline where a record would have carried it.

The rehearsed language went thin in her mouth. She took the nearest chair and sat before the table could decide whether she was meant to stand.

The second stranger was a woman holding cards older than manufacture, thick and hand-beveled, the corners of the top few worn softer than the rest. She shuffled them the way a clerk squares a ledger. Trinity didn't permit the future to be decided by symbols. The cards sat in her hands anyway, an instrument.

The last sat at the far end of the table, and was unlike the others in a way Elara felt before she could name it. A plain grey shawl, darned once at the shoulder in thread a shade too dark. A cotton tunic. A face lined by years of patience, and no mark of office or lineage anywhere on her. Her eyes were the trouble. They held a steadiness older than the room, the look of somebody taught to watch a person until the pretense ran out and then to go on watching. Her stillness was the instrument, and the room had arranged itself around it.

Elara drew breath to begin.

The woman in the shawl lifted one hand. The palm turned outward, fingers loose and open, a gesture so still it seemed older than speech.

The words stopped behind Elara's teeth. Her breath stopped under them, half-drawn. Her mouth wouldn't open. Her chest wouldn't move.

No one at the table spoke. Her ribs locked around the air she had and refused to trade it. Heat climbed her neck. The edges of the room went soft and bright, her hands found the table and pressed flat, and still nothing came in or went out. The one thing she had always owned, the answer, had been taken before she could use it.

The hand came down.

Her lungs seized and opened. She folded over the table, one hand at her collar, dragging air in through her teeth, drowning and being hauled onto a deck at once. Her eyes ran. She forced herself upright with her hands still shaking against the wood, and found the committee seated exactly as it had been, and the woman at the far end with her patient face unchanged.

They'd called her here to learn the terms of the question before she was allowed an answer.

Then the woman spoke, and the words came in Sanskrit, the oldest of the carried tongues, the language of the scriptures behind Vishnu's warped cabinet door.

"मोनितोरा नातन, अधुना वक्तुं अर्हसि। अपि च, यानि अस्माभिः परिकल्पितानि प्रश्नानि तानि एव तेओभ्युपगच्छन्तु, न तु स्वस्यात्मनः परिकल्पितानि।"

She inclined her head to Kavik. He rose into the silence without any change in his face.

"Monitor Nathan, you may now speak. Furthermore, let only those questions that have been devised by us be taken up by you — not ones devised by your own self."

The meaning had stirred before he opened his mouth. Some part of her, the part that had sat on Vishnu's floor with a book she couldn't read, had reached toward the syllables and nearly closed on them. A sense sitting just past the edge of the hand.

Anandi had warned her, in the first year, in a voice that left no room for argument. *Just when you think you have seen it all, life will throw you a surprise and remind you that you still have a very long way to go.* Elara had laughed. She'd been young enough to think she already knew the shape of the world.

The woman in the shawl lifted her chin.

"प्रवृत्तेः पूर्वम्," she said, "अद्य अस्यां सभायाम् उपविष्टेभ्यः आचार्येभ्यः उचितम् आदरं दर्शयत। आचार्याः एकैकशः स्वपरिचयं कुर्वन्तु।"

"Before getting involved," Kavik said, "show due respect to the teachers sitting in this meeting today. Let the teachers introduce themselves one by one."

One by one, they turned their faces to her.

"I am Sarvajña, the all-knowing," said the man in saffron. The stones at his wrist caught the light as he spoke. "I am the elder entrusted to keep Drona steady upon the line of righteousness. Drona stands on three pillars: knowledge, disciplined inquiry, and the duty to serve truth before power. We do not permit them to be reduced to procedure alone."

"I am Draṣṭā, the seer." The woman with the cards had none of the theatre Elara had braced for. "My duty is to know the three times: the past, the present, and the future. Nothing is certain, and I do not pretend otherwise."

The third let the silence lengthen before she filled it. Sarvajña carried knowledge; Draṣṭā carried perception; Anandi and Kavik had built the mind that sat here. The woman at the end of the table stood above those distinctions, as if the room's laws had gathered in her rather than the other way round.

"I am the Grandmaster, known by many names depending on who you are and what you believe in," she said. "But today, the Ancient One sits well with me."

She said it in Elara's own language, and the shift unsettled her more than the Sanskrit had. The two tongues came out of one breath.

The Grandmaster's eyes moved to Anandi and Kavik, then back. "We are gathered to share grave news, graver than you can imagine, and perhaps, in some way, news that will bring you relief. We are not here to reflect on your choices during the Initiation. There is no right or wrong until the impact of a choice is felt as just or unjust. That conversation is reserved for another time."

A small signal to Draṣṭā. The seer gathered the cards and spread them across the wood in a long uneven fan, dark backs up.

"Choose one."

*Why?* It rose and stopped behind her teeth, where the rule now lived. She had never felt the cost of a question until she was holding one she wasn't allowed to set down.

Draṣṭā smiled faintly. Nothing amused in it. The look of somebody who had heard the swallowed word cross the table and had decided not to answer it.

If the news was as grave as the room's faces, the danger already existed. A card wouldn't make it, and refusing one wouldn't unmake it. Elara reached.

Draṣṭā turned the first card over. A king suspended between a dark sky and the curve of the earth, neither rising nor falling, and a single name in old lettering: *Trishanku*.

"Pushed out of heaven and prevented from falling back. Fixed between the two, belonging to neither." Draṣṭā touched the name. "The word survived because some outcomes refuse to resolve."

UNASSIGNED. Three names flashing on a grid that had no row for them.

"The second."

A warrior under a ruined sky, weapon lowered but not surrendered. *Ashwatthama*.

"Conflict that cannot be terminated. He loosed a weapon at an unborn child after the war was already decided, and was cursed to survive without relief. The battle ended. The conflict did not." Draṣṭā's smile shifted its meaning without moving. "He is also the son of Drona. You have chosen a son, while his father's house now stands by you."

Three boys holding one another up in a tunnel while a system wrote *coordinated containment* over them. Nothing about it was finished.

"The last."

A severed head rising against a darkened sun, mouth open on the light. *Rahu*.

"The asura who drank the stolen amrita. Vishnu cut him in two with the nectar at his throat, and so he lives without a body. He swallows the sun and the moon at eclipses and cannot hold them. They always emerge." Draṣṭā laid a fingertip beside the name. "Rahu is danger that keeps coming and never ends. The old word is *graha*, that which seizes. If Trinity wanted a label for a boy nearing a dangerous limit, it might call his energy a graha reading, and never know what it had borrowed."

Otto's force markers, flicking up and down like a held breath.

"The past cannot be changed by regret, Elara. The present must be met by action, and the future often warns us before it arrives. Prepare while preparation is still possible."

Elara looked at the three cards side by side. Suspension. Conflict. Danger closing and reopening.

Sarvajña leaned forward. The mystic went out of his voice entirely; what was left sounded like a man reading figures that had been checked.

"The animosity between the ganas did not begin with you. For generations the elders have spent themselves balancing the scales between them: what was meant, what was done, what each act set in motion. That work has kept old grievances from becoming open war. It is not resolution. And while we have watched the scales, something has begun to form beneath them."

He let that sit. His fingers went to the stones at his cuff and counted along them once, four and then three, the way another man might touch a pocket to check a key.

"A proposal for a new wing of the Drona library cleared our internal review this month, after a decade of argument in the open, the way Drona settles everything. When we carried the request to the treasury, to Kubera, the funds did not move. Conversations were held in closed rooms. The transfer has still not been released."

Elara's hangnail had opened again overnight. She found it with her other thumb under the table and pressed.

"Our sources tell us a new power is gathering inside Kubera. It has no name, no public office, no designation that can be entered into a record. Every account describes the same thing: something of extraordinary reach taking shape behind the house's visible structure, waiting only for the hand that will wield it. A power like that cannot be built without complete support. It would take the entire chain of authority in Kubera, all the way to its Grandmaster."

Her hand left the hangnail and closed on the table edge. The wood was old and rounded by a thousand other hands, and she gripped it until the tendons stood up along the back of her wrist.

"What cannot be entered cannot be weighed, and what cannot be weighed cannot be answered." His eyes came back to her. "We believe your Initiation stands among its first concerns. Kubera's representative moved against your candidates and failed, in front of the other houses, on footage every house has seen and none has acknowledged. He has since disappeared from Kubera's rolls."

Daren. Daren at dawn with his chair rolling away behind him, close enough that she'd felt his breath. *You made the system lie for you.* She saw his pen tapping twice on the printed manual and stopping, and couldn't fit the man who'd done that into the word *disappeared*.

"Kubera does not record its humiliations as accidents. You will stand at the center of what follows, Elara, when these days come due. That is why we are here: to prepare you, and to see that Drona stands behind you. Your mentors will be your shadows from this moment onward. Knowledge must not bow to wealth, strategy, or force."

The elders rose. All five, without a word passing between them, the chairs barely sounding on the floor. They looked like witnesses to a thing already decided.

"This is the conflict every Drona representative prepares for across a lifetime," Sarvajña said.

"We have each wished, at one time or another, that it would fall to any of us instead," Anandi said.

"It has fallen to you," said the Grandmaster. "You must persevere, and you must prevail. What is at stake is not Drona alone. If you fail, the foundation of everything we know to be real and just will be cracked."

Elara stood because they were standing. She was a student called to the front of a hall, and the hall was waiting for her to accept the charge and sit, and her mouth opened and the question that came out was none of theirs.

"Why me, and not one of you?"

Kavik's folded hands came apart. Anandi closed her eyes for the space of a breath, the way she had once closed them over a wrong proof. Sarvajña's fingers found the seventh stone and stayed there. Draṣṭā's smile deepened by a hair. At the end of the table, the Ancient One's hand lifted an inch off the wood, the same hand, and Elara's ribs remembered it before her mind did.

The hand went still. It did not rise.

"Not by destiny," the Grandmaster said. "Because you acted while action still mattered, and what you set in motion cannot now be carried by anyone else."

Nobody sat. The light came clean through the glass door behind her, and somewhere down the wing a brush was being guided across a damaged painting by two fingers that would not let it slip.

She had come up the corridor rehearsing a defense. Nobody had asked for one. It was a reckoning.
