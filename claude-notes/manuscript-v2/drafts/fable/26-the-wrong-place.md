# Chapter 26: The Wrong Place

*"Trinity gave me a Grade A prosthetic leg, then fired me and stopped paying for it. A few weeks later it seized up under me at a coffee shop — left me a paperweight bolted to bone. They didn't know I knew how to rebuild the machine."*

— Book 1, Chapter 13: We Have a Guest

## Now: The wrong place

Daren read the tower's overnight digest on the edge of the bed, under the dark display he had never switched on, with the pane of glass across his knees.

He read the digests every morning. A tower wrote its night down in a few dull lines, and the lines told him what the instruments had been told, and whether anyone had asked them a question they'd been instructed not to answer. Door cycles. Lift holds. Maintenance intervals, his own, each with the hour he'd given it. Most mornings the digest said the fourth level had slept.

*Fourth-level service stair, ceiling reader. Two persons, unidentified. Amber, twice. 2340.*

His hour. His stair. The reader at the stair head, which he passed under every night and which had never once had a name to ask him for, had found two people it couldn't name either, forty minutes before he came home.

The fear came up his throat before he'd finished the line. He knew it now. He knew where it went next, out of the throat and down the arm into the nerves that lay against the obsidian, and he knew what listened there and how little it needed. So he did what he had done at a railing on Meru with a moth beside his hand. He put the pane down on the blanket. He set his right hand on his knee, palm up, and opened it one finger at a time, the little finger last, and thought about nothing but the opening. The band had warmed against the bone. He waited. The room was cold enough that his breath showed. The band cooled.

Two persons. Unidentified. In a city where every wrist carried a name, the sensors failed to name a person for one reason only. Someone had stood on his stair without a terminal. Two someones, side by side, at his hour, looking at a corridor with their own eyes.

He knew that look. He'd had it from a crouching man beside an open panel, and he'd learned at the sound of a body meeting concrete what his hand did about being looked at. Last night two more pairs of those eyes had been on his stair, and he had walked under them to his door and felt nothing at all.

He got up and went to the display.

The housing stood a finger's width off the wall, where the fitters had left it. He slid two fingers into the gap and drew the notebook out. It had taken on the cold of the wall and the dust off the back of the screen, and a hair, not his, was caught in the binding where the cover had been bent back one time too many.

He hadn't burned it. There was a lamp on the table with a true flame that he could have held it over any night in the last week, and some nights he'd lit the lamp. He didn't know why the notebook was still here. He had stopped asking, because the question had no column.

He opened it on his knees. Pencil, small and upright, sharpened with a knife; the flat facets of the point showed in the heavier strokes. Weeks of the tower's nights. Each blank the man had found was circled, and the circles climbed floor by floor and tightened, toward the fourth level, toward the corridor, toward a room on it that the plans showed and the records didn't. The last page held a square with nothing written inside. The square was this room. The man had drawn the door Daren sat behind and hadn't lived to put a word in it.

Now there were two more. Whoever had leashed the man had sent two to stand where he'd stood, and they'd come with bare wrists because they'd learned from him what wrists did. They'd watched the hole in the plan, and they'd seen whatever walked into it.

He closed the book and sat with it flat under his palm.

In the wall behind the display there was a shallow dent at head height, from a night he'd stood up too fast. He looked at it a while. Then he put the notebook back in the gap, dressed, and went up the mountain before anyone could send for him.

---

The rings lay in their row on the side table, in the dull crescent of scratches they had worn into the lacquer, and the Master of the Reserve sat with his hands on his knees.

He hadn't been summoned. He'd come because the digest would reach the summit by evening with other men's opinions attached, and because the Master had told him in a gallery corridor to put down whatever he was carrying, and Daren meant to show he knew what it was.

"Two people stood on the stair last night," he said. "At my hour. The tower's reader couldn't name them. Bare wrists."

"I know."

"The digest only went to—"

"I was told before the digest." The Master's eyes stayed on the window, where the three cities lay under the cloud line. "The Ancient One called me on the old plate, with someone in the room with her whom she did not name. She said the Guard had been seen in Drona." He said *the Guard* flatly, a line in a ledger. "She has asked the table to sit out of season. I said that I would come without escort."

Daren waited for the rest, and there wasn't any. The old man was calm. He had the calm of a balance struck and agreed.

"Then she knows."

"She knows that something moves in this city which she did not sanction. She has known that for a century and slept well. What she has now is a stair and an hour and two people who saw a door open. That is a story. The table does not sit on stories." He turned one of the rings a quarter on the lacquer with a fingertip and set it back in its place. "Before the table sits, the stair will be clean. And the paper you took from the corridor will be ash."

Daren had given him the corridor the morning after, consequences first, in two sentences: a technician, a line test, a notebook with a map in it. The old man hadn't asked a question then. He wasn't asking one now.

"Clean," Daren said.

"You know the word."

He did. *Put down whatever you are carrying before the next one.* He'd taken it in the gallery for a caution about his hands. The next one had a number now, and the number was two.

He'd meant to give the account whole and leave. Instead he heard himself say the thing he'd kept behind his teeth for a week.

"I didn't choose him."

The Master said nothing.

"The man in the corridor. I walked past him. I didn't lift my hand. He looked at me, and I was afraid, and it took the fear off my arm and finished it. It fires on fear. A heartbeat early, the way it does everything." His voice had gone thin, and he let it. "I didn't know until I heard him hit the floor. I've been standing still for a week so it won't hear anything I don't mean, and I don't know how long I can—"

"I know what it does."

It came too fast. Daren had prepared for surprise, for anger, for a buyer's disgust at flawed goods. The answer had been waiting on the Master's tongue since before Daren opened his mouth, and it came out of him with no weight on it at all.

"You knew."

"It reads the hand. The hand is not always honest. One learns." The Master's eyes came off the window and settled on him with something that in another man might have been patience. "It is cold the first night. After that you stop noticing."

Daren heard it.

He had heard it once before, said to the ceiling the night the clasp closed, as if the old man were mentioning the weather. He'd taken it then for something the gold box came with. A note in a file. He heard it now for what it was: weather you've stood in. Nobody tells you the first night is cold. You wake up in it.

His eyes went to the Master's hands.

They lay on his knees, pale, the knuckles a little swollen, smaller than the office. With the rings on the table they were any old man's. The left wrist had the strip every citizen had, where the terminal lay. The right wrist had something else. Along it, old and almost gone, so faint that the lamplight found it only because the hand was turned, ran a ridge in the skin where a clasp had sat for a long time and then had not. Daren knew the ridge. He had one. His thumb went to it every night under the obsidian without his sending it.

Kubera's gold left a mark on every representative who wore it. He told himself that first. Then he saw where the ridge lay. Kubera's gold rode low, against the heel of the hand, and that was where Daren's old ridge was. This one sat a finger higher. It sat where the obsidian sat.

"I stood where you are standing," the Master said. "Once."

Out past the glass, the cloud moved over Trinity-2 and uncovered it. Daren thought of a lamp buzzing at the end of every third breath. *You are standing in the wrong place.* He'd taken it for a remark about the gallery seat. The old man had been telling him where he was.

"She'll send for you," he said. It was the only thing left in his mouth. "You said no escort. They send for the four of you. You've never known where you go."

"She need not send. I know the way to the table. I did not need to be taken." The Master lifted the first ring from the lacquer. "That is a thing one learns, wearing it. Among others."

The questions arrived all at once, and Daren couldn't ask one of them. *What is in this will not close on a hand the city is still holding.* The city had let go of this hand once, then. Someone had stood at a seal and watched this man's palm become a stranger's, and someone had said *wait*, or nobody had, and he'd found the box himself. Daren couldn't ask who. He couldn't ask what the old man had done in the dark between that night and the morning the rings came, or where a man goes, wearing it, that no one is fetched to. Every question was a reach. The band listened to reaches.

He put his hand still.

The Master slid the rings on one by one, and the office came back into the room with them. "You will have the stair clean before the table sits. You will have the paper ash. You will keep your hand where I can see it." The last ring settled. "Second only to mine."

Daren kept his face. He'd learned to keep it in these rooms before he had learned anything else. He found the words in the usual place.

"Second only to yours."

"The stair."

"Before the table sits."

It was one third of the truth. He'd kept the method from this man once, in this room, and felt the thing unsaid as a coin behind his sternum. Tonight what he kept was smaller and weighed more: he didn't know yet whether a word he'd just said was true.

---

The maglev took him down through the dark between platforms.

He stood at the rear of the last carriage, where he always stood, and the carriage did what carriages did. A records clerk angled her case away from an empty seat. At the orchard-tier halt a family came on with the evening's shopping and a mesh carrier.

He knew the carrier. One corner of the mesh had been mended with a twist of copper wire, and the wire had gone green. The daughter was taller by a hand than she'd been the night of the card, and she'd stopped whispering to the cat. The cat hadn't changed. It sat on her lap with its paws folded under it, and somewhere over the open dark between the towers it lifted its head and found the far end of the carriage.

Its ears went flat.

Last time he'd put it where he put things that had no column, and the column had turned out to hold a man on his side with one boot half off. He didn't look away. He looked back.

A small grey animal with a bent whisker on the left and a notch in one ear. It didn't know what a house was, or a record, or what a hand could be given for. It had no wrist. Nobody had ever fastened anything on it to tell it who outranked it. It had eyes and a nose, and both reported a man at the dark end of the carriage who smelled of fear and held his right arm like a cup filled to the brim.

What did it see. He'd never asked. He asked now, with the cat's eyes on him, and the answer came up plain and whole, the first plain thing in weeks: a man with something on his wrist he couldn't put down. Every instrument in the carriage reported nothing at that end. The cat reported that. It had seen it the first night, on the way to buy an analyst, and he'd called it plumbing.

The band warmed against the bone.

He felt it start, the nerves along his forearm telling the old story, *flinch, grip, get away*, and he knew what the band did with that story and how fast. The carrier was three seats from him. The daughter's hand lay on the mesh. He didn't let his eyes leave the cat. He let his shoulders down. He opened the hand at his side one finger at a time, the way you open a hand that's held something too long. He thought about the wire gone green, and the notch in the ear, and the running lights going by, and the cat went on looking at him and he went on being looked at, and he did nothing at all.

The band cooled.

The cat's ears came up. It put its chin down on its paws and kept its eyes on him anyway, slitted, unblinking, for the whole length of the crossing, the look of an animal that has decided a thing is dangerous and has also decided it won't do anything about it just now. He stood under the look. He found he could.

The mother consulted her left wrist, found nothing worth a glance, and looked away. The daughter looked where the cat looked. The strap on her wrist was the child's size, and it told her the end of the carriage was empty, and she believed it over her own cat. The cat had never been given a strap. The cat was the only passenger who'd ever really been on the train with him.

Trinity-1 assembled out of the dark. The family went out ahead of him, the carrier swinging, and the cat's eyes held on him over the girl's shoulder all the way down the platform.

---

In the unlisted room he drew the notebook out of the gap behind the display and sat with it on his knees. The lamp stood on the table. He didn't light it.

He knew what the Master meant by clean, and he knew how to begin. The tower's gates had tasted every terminal that passed them all night, and somewhere in that record were two people who'd come up a stair with their wrists covered and gone down it bare. He could ask the band for the gate log, and the band would ask in Drona's own dialect, and two names would lie on his pane before his fingers finished the request. Then there'd be a panel, or a lift, or a platform edge, and a maintenance interval written over the hour, and the stair would be clean.

He didn't ask.

He sat with his right hand open on the notebook and did nothing with the band, on purpose. It was harder than Meru. On Meru he'd held still against his own fear. Tonight he held still against an order, and the order came from a man who'd worn this and known what it listened to and fastened it on a bare wrist anyway and called it a second chance. Whatever the Master had meant by *clean*, he'd learned the word wearing this. Daren didn't want to know what it had cost him. He didn't want to be the next one to learn.

The four notes came up in his throat, low, the same four. He let them finish. There had never been a fifth; he'd never known what came after. When they ended, the room was the room.

He put the notebook inside his coat, on the left, flat against his chest, and buttoned the coat over it. It was warm from his hands. It sat against his ribs where his heartbeat could reach it, a map of his own hiding place, and while it was on him the lamp would stay dark.

Then, because it was still true and he wanted to know whether it was still true, his left hand went to the band. His fingers found the edge and hooked under it.

They stopped.

He'd expected that. What he hadn't done before was look at the stopping. In the corridor he'd looked at the man. On Meru he'd looked at the band. Now he looked at his own two fingers under the obsidian and asked them, as plainly as he'd asked the cat, what they were waiting for. The answer was the one it had always been. Without this there was no one. Kubera had emptied the room and the Master had filled it, and if he pulled there'd be a pale ridge on a bare wrist and no man to hang it on.

*No one you'd be.* That was all the fingers knew. Nobody had ever shown them anything else.

He sat with the stopped hand against his chest and the book under it. Up the mountain an old man had worn this, and taken it off, and lived. The city had let go of that hand once and taken it back, which meant there'd been a man under the band that the city could find again when it looked.

Maybe there was one under this one.

He didn't know. He let the fingers come out from under the band and laid the hand flat on the coat, over the notebook.

The man in the corridor had had a name. Daren didn't know it and never would. Somebody should, he thought, and it was the first thing he'd wanted in a long while that the band had no way to give him.
