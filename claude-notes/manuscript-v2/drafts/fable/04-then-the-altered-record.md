# Chapter 4: The Altered Record

*On the fourth day a guard came to her door with a wrench. The wrench he had been holding had been logged to him at the start of the shift, and Trinity was a careful auditor. An unaccounted tool was a hole in the inventory. The guard handed it over without ceremony — the way one returns a shipment. Rita signed for it. The husband was a part to discard; the wrench was an asset to track. It was the only piece of him the algorithm hadn’t erased.*

— Book 1, Chapter 6: To Hell and Back

## Then: Days One to Three

The red bar went out.

Gage sat under VARIANCE now, Ben under EVENT, Otto under CORRECTION, and beside each name a small amber mark recorded that a human hand had put him there. It was a quieter flag than the one it replaced. It was still a flag.

"There," Daren said. "Sorted."

Sorted was his word. Elara let him keep it.

The intake checklist ran down the margin of Gage's profile in narrow numbered lines — identity, medical baseline, chip telemetry, drone coverage, baseline behavior — and she thumbed each one against the raw readings beneath it while the room filled with the small chimes of four people agreeing with a machine. The screen took Gage apart as it showed him to her. The chip under his skin sent up his pulse, his cortisol, his sleep debt. Drones in the cloud over Miner Town mapped the heat leaving his skin. From a kilometer up she could watch him breathe, warm, strain and recover without ever meeting his eyes.

The last line read *Initiation environment compatible*. Her thumb stopped above it. Five minutes ago the system hadn't known what to do with this boy; now it wanted her signature under its conclusion. She pressed, and the panel chimed, and somewhere below her the gates opened.

Her feed split into six: a wide overhead of the chamber, twin thermal maps, twin infrared, and a sixth panel for Gage alone, his numbers scrolling in isolation from the other fifteen. Sixteen heat signatures passed through the iron doors and scattered. No guards followed. Each candidate carried one vacuum-sealed pouch, and the dispensers along the wall released water without a hand appearing to turn a tap.

For nine hours his curve tracked the cohort's. Then two alerts stacked beside his profile. He had split his pouch with Ben and Otto, dropping his own intake under the ration floor, and the other two hadn't left his proximity band since the doors closed.

Ravi pulled both logs onto the central grid. "He's working out what game we're playing with them. Nobody turns down a meal at hour nine unless he's thinking about hour forty."

"Doesn't matter what he's thinking." Daren's eyes stayed on his loss projections. "He's destabilizing the cohort."

Elara enlarged the overhead. Whenever the three moved, Gage ended up between the other two, and whenever the crowd pressed closer, Ben and Otto turned toward him before they moved. Instinct or accident; the feed couldn't say. Under the alerts sat a symmetry she didn't enter into the record: the boy someone had fed off the books was the first to feed someone else.

Her console offered three actions. Record the deviation. Notify the floor wardens. Initiate corrective observation. Each would turn a guess into an official fact. She selected the first and left the other two inactive.

"You're not escalating?" Ravi said.

"I'm not concluding before there's anything to conclude from."

At twelve hours the blue bands along the walls brightened to white, every display dimmed except the central grid, and the chamber passed from human eyes to automated ones.

The recovery station measured them as it fed them. A scanner at the door took their fatigue the way the plate upstairs had taken their blood, and the dispenser printed a receipt with every cup: name, dose, milligrams. Ravi got tea. Daren got black coffee. Rohan got coffee cut with electrolytes. Elara got something the receipt called coffee and her molars called a punishment.

Daren folded his receipt into a square the size of a fingernail and set it in his saucer. He joined Rohan at the counter. "You should've notified the wardens. Variables reveal what a system is worth."

"They're kids."

"They're candidates. Every candidate who walks out holding hands teaches the next sixteen that the system can be argued with."

Rohan set his cup down. "Force has a job. Making your report look busy isn't it."

Daren turned to the room. "Sixteen intakes, three deviations, zero recommendations. Kubera's going to ask what this shift produced."

"A record," Elara said. "Which is what it's for."

"Is that what you're drinking? It smells like a record."

"It tastes like one." She drank it anyway. "It's measured. If I leave it, somebody logs that too."

Rohan almost smiled. The split knuckles of his right hand had opened again at the console; he'd wrapped them in grey tape from the supply drawer and the tape was already going dark.

Ravi stirred his tea without drinking it. "The record worth reading is Ben's. A household that starved itself to keep alive the man who robbed it. The archive filed that as a compassion anomaly."

"Generous." Daren hummed four notes into his cup, the same four he'd brought through the door that morning. "I'd have filed it as waste."

"File it how you like. A family that decides care is worth starving for teaches a boy that loyalty outbids fear. Doesn't show up in blood. Doesn't answer to force." Ravi's glance went to Rohan's back. "Which is why neither of your consoles saw them first."

Rohan left when his half hour was logged. Ravi crossed to Elara's table after the door sealed behind him.

"Drona trains you to observe before you alter a system," he said. "You going to hold to that?"

On her wrist the three readings moved together: Gage's strain climbing, Ben's responses jittering, Otto's force markers spiking and settling. "Until the evidence gives me a reason not to."

He let the sentence sit the way a signal sat on the grid: not yet meaningful, not yet dismissed.

---

By eighteen hours nobody in the chamber slept. The screens showed shaking hands, repeated movements, pulses rising without exertion, and at the tunnel wall three heat signatures that touched at the edges and stayed touching.

The second morning the room came back to it.

"Cohesion isn't evidence of safety," Daren said.

"Neither is isolation," Elara said.

His eyes stayed on her instead of the board. Kubera sat above the other three ganas because Kubera decided what the other three could spend, and being corrected in front of them was a cost Daren hadn't budgeted. He let the silence carry the insult and turned back to his console with the stiffness of a man the room hadn't chosen as its center.

"Give me language that survives review," Ravi said.

She typed a provisional note: candidates maintaining cooperative proximity under early stress, no intervention recommended at this time. Rohan hadn't looked away from Otto's feed. "At twenty-four hours the real decisions start," he said. "The force ladder stays shut unless there's an actual threat."

By thirty-six the feeds had stopped resembling a test and begun resembling an accounting of who could stay awake. Children struck the walls to feel something besides wakefulness. Otto held Ben back from one of them. Elara's hand went to Otto's file the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth, and the small red flag Rohan had logged at intake blinked once beside his name and steadied. Across the grid Rohan was watching it too. A lesser monitor would have opened the correction protocols out of discomfort with the unknown. Rohan sat with his taped hand flat on the console and did nothing, which on Ashoka's station was a decision.

---

At forty-eight hours the consoles brightened one after another and the blue light settled over the floor and the walls and the four faces. It made everyone look healthier than they were.

Elara brought Gage's feed to full size. The chamber had reorganized itself overnight. The children still conscious had gathered in clusters along the tunnel walls, and the clusters answered one another without speaking. Her three were still together, and their readings should have been getting worse.

Gage paced his breathing to Ben's whenever Ben's panic started to climb, and both heart rates came down together. Otto barely moved. He sat like a spring held closed and stepped in to steady whichever of the other two drifted toward collapse, and he did it before the system's alert could fire.

It was a loop. She separated the signals and ran the comparison again and got the same answer. Gage carried the physical strain. Ben absorbed the emotional disruption. Otto moved when either of them approached the edge. None of the three was stable alone, and together they were holding one another inside the narrow band between function and failure.

She named it MUTUAL STABILIZATION.

The system rejected the label and kept the group-formation warning active in its place.

The rejection sat on her screen in the red of a transfusion bag, and she was sixteen, in a hospital room that smelled of solvent, watching Teresa try to lift a cup of water. Teresa had taken a fresh transfusion that afternoon. The cup rose a finger's width and stopped, and the water in it trembled, and Elara reached across and took it from her and saw the look Teresa gave her — gratitude, and fear, and the beginning of surrender. The doctors had a sentence for every rupture in Teresa's body and none for stopping them. They described her. They described her so well that the description came to seem like the illness itself, and when she died there was nothing left to add.

Elara changed the category from group behavior to cooperative compensation. The system held the phrase for less than a second and replaced it with a red notation about interpretive interference.

Her fingers stopped. In the corner of her screen the diagnostic margin was flashing a calibration value pinned at the ceiling; someone had pushed her console's sensitivity as high as it went. She looked across the grid. Daren met her eyes, gave her the picture of innocence, and went back to his loss curves.

The ceiling didn't belong to her console alone. It lived in the shared alert calibration every station inherited, and whoever had raised it through her board had raised it for the whole room. A chime went off at Ashoka's station. Rohan checked the force ladder, then the feed. "Otto's breathing changed."

Elara enlarged the panel. Otto had moved closer to the others. His shoulders were down, his hands open on his knees. Under ordinary thresholds it was a posture reset; on a board this twitchy it glowed like a breach. His heart rate had dropped six points. Ben's followed. Then Gage's.

Rohan watched the sequence twice, separating the movement from the alarm. "He's regulating them."

"Or controlling them," Daren said.

"You call everything you don't understand control."

"And you call every use of force protection when you like the hands it comes from."

Ravi hadn't looked up from EVENT. "What did you change?"

"Nothing that alters the readings," Elara said.

"That wasn't my question."

A new window opened across all four consoles under the seal of High Command. It asked for a preliminary candidate assessment and populated the fields itself: identity, physical condition, behavioral classification, group risk, recommended post-Initiation disposition. The last field was blank. The rest had been written before anyone was asked to sign. Gage was a physical destabilizer. Ben was a social influence risk. Otto was a force anomaly requiring controlled placement. Together they were a coordinated deviation cluster — efficient, bloodless, and wrong.

Ravi's cursor rested over his own group-risk field. "What are you going to call them?"

"The system already decided."

"It's asking us to confirm. That's different."

It was the first thing he'd said to her that sounded like an invitation.

On the feed Gage had shifted so that Ben was shielded from the open chamber. Otto sat on the far side watching the tunnel mouth. None of them knew their arrangement had become evidence. Elara opened the interpretive note under the approved designation and typed that the three candidates showed evidence of mutual stabilization under extreme environmental stress, and that group cohesion appeared to reduce individual failure risk. In the recommendation field she wrote: continued observation advised before separation.

The border turned amber. Justification required.

"Elara," Ravi said.

"What."

"You're asking the system to wait."

Waiting was the only thing she'd ever been able to give Teresa, and it had bought nothing. Separation had bought nothing either. She entered the justification: separation might increase individual failure risk; the current data didn't support the assumption that cohesion was the primary cause of instability. The amber held for three seconds. Then the report recorded, and a line appeared beneath it.

INTERPRETIVE DEVIATION DETECTED.

Near sixty hours Rosa's marker broke from the cohort. The monitors got fragments: Emily's vitals flat after repeated blunt trauma, Rosa moving through the haze with a second heat signature cooling against her back, a surge of violence when she reached Gage's group. The system named her the aggressor. Elara's telemetry only knew the vector, and the vector ran straight at the one cluster in the chamber still upright, the way a person runs toward help. Rohan froze the automated verdict. "Partial surveillance isn't a finding." Ravi flagged the record for contextual review, and Elara added that no separation order should issue from it.

The warning hadn't cleared when Gage's numbers jumped. Pulse leaping, breath short and violent, motion sensors registering him rising off the floor. The system pieced together fragments from his sleep cycle — a face suggested by neural activity, a platform inferred from spatial patterns — and could not say whether the nightmare had stopped when he woke. His trajectory pointed at the tunnel mouth and the restricted route behind it toward the wardens' sector. The system marked it pursuit intent and couldn't decide whether the target was a person, a memory or a place.

Ben's signal reached him first. The sensors recorded a struggle; the drone audio caught Ben's voice under the chamber noise, strained and continuous. Gage's heart rate crossed the red line and Otto's force markers rose to meet it. Then, by degrees, Gage's breathing slowed, and Ben's exertion fell, and Otto's markers fell, and the three signals settled back into their band.

The system called it coordinated containment by unauthorized subjects.

AUTOMATED SEPARATION PROTOCOL INITIATED.

Elara sat forward. "Cancel that."

The command field refused her clearance. Ground personnel had been judged insufficient against the combined strength and skill profiles of Gage and Otto, and the response order had already routed past the local wardens to the Infinity Guard chambers. Four hours' transit. The alert posted to every console, and the room's shift handoff, due at sixty hours, suspended itself: all four monitors were locked to their stations until the deployment resolved or the trial reached seventy-two hours.

Ravi's jaw set. Daren stared at the resource estimate. Rohan stood with both palms on CORRECTION, reading the order as if pressure might make it less final.

"They're exhausted children," Elara said.

"Gage is a physical threat," Daren said. "Otto's a trained force anomaly. The system isn't measuring their age. It's measuring what they can do."

"And Ben?"

"The reason you separate them before he becomes part of it."

At the water line something changed. All three stopped drinking. The system recorded it beside the chemical exposure markers without being able to say whether Gage understood the cause, and then, as fatigue took them, recorded that their linked readings were moving toward rest instead of renewed conflict.

Nobody in the monitoring room moved much either. Ravi had taken off his boots under the console. Elara had worked a hangnail on her left thumb raw against the edge of the glass and hadn't noticed until the sensor plate began reading the blood.

By sixty-four hours, when the first guard transport cleared the lower launch platform, all three boys were asleep.

The Infinity Guard found them four hours later motionless beneath the tunnel wall. Gage's pulse was slow. Ben's breathing was even. Otto's hands lay open and empty. "Candidates are asleep," the team leader reported. "No active threat."

Rohan opened the channel. "Perimeter at ten meters. Nobody inside it. They walked in on their own feet and they'll walk out on them."

Daren objected to the cost of holding twelve guards still in a tunnel. Ravi entered the absence of a threat into the event record. Elara kept the group-risk field open, because the trial hadn't ended and nothing could be recorded as survived.

At seventy-two hours the gates reopened, and one signal rose before the guards crossed the threshold. Gage's exertion markers climbed past anything the chamber had recorded as he lifted Otto across his shoulders and closed his free hand around Ben's collar. The overhead showed one upright figure moving toward the light, carrying one boy and dragging the other. No command had brought them to the exit. They crossed it together because he refused to cross it any other way.

The system passed them on survival criteria and escalated them for executive review. The notice named the committee — Icepick, Jung, the men who governed Miner Town from inside it — and, under that, flagged the operator at VARIANCE as under review.

---

Two lawful roads were open to her. She could take the reprimand and let the induction file cull the boys on schedule, or file a formal dissent and watch it crawl through committees while the separation directives executed anyway. Both ended at the same wall with her record intact. The third road was on no approved map: the cohort file was a protected governance document, it answered only to an administrative session in the restricted archive one tier below, and touching it was treason if the system traced her hand. She looked at the three signals on her screen and took the third.

The review flag was the opening. Every operator under review held one procedural right before the inquiry convened: twenty minutes in the sub-tier vaults to pull the printed shift ledgers and check them against the digital copy the board would read. The bureaucracy's way of letting the accused see the evidence. Elara initiated Drona's audit protocol before executive lockouts could take hold, and on the central display her status shifted from active monitoring to transit for audit retrieval.

Daren glanced up with a smirk at the corner of his mouth; he thought she was scurrying off to build her defense. Ravi watched the authorization post without expression. Rohan nodded once.

She walked past the curved desks with her pulse held steady against the sensors and stepped into the Skylark before its doors closed. Thirty seconds down. She used them the way she'd once used the night before an incident report, rehearsing every checkpoint her authorization covered and the one door it didn't.

The vault level smelled of paper and machine oil. A duty clerk with a pencil behind his ear that nobody had sharpened in years glanced at her audit code, logged her entry, and went back to his queue. The vault register was paper, and she signed it in her own name, because the cover only held if the audit was real.

Her name in her own hand. She was fifteen, under a maintenance tarp in a freight pod full of ration crates, the notebook pressed against her ribs while the city shrank above her. Less than an hour down. Three days on the ground: a transport route that failed, a station full of people sleeping beside their machines, an old woman at the end of a settlement who had one more story and no mountain. Teresa died in the hospital on the second night. A nurse told Elara afterward, meaning it as a kindness, that the last word Teresa said was *Elara*. She'd said it to a room with nobody in it who answered to that name.

The pen left the register. Elara pulled the folios her review required — grey tape around each, her shift codes stamped on the spines — and stacked them in the retrieval crate while the clock in her head kept subtracting.

The restricted archive lay one tier of clearance past hers, joined to the vaults by a single corridor. Her audit code opened the corridor. It did not open the archive, and every door sensor she passed took her pulse on the way. She walked at retrieval pace with the folios across her arms. At the end of the corridor, in an alcove outside the sealed office, the superintendent's duty terminal stood running and unattended.

She set the folios on the alcove shelf.

Twelve. The construct's face unfinished like a doll's, its breathing uneven on cue, and Iris two benches ahead tapping two fingers on her thigh before her hand went to the strap the exercise had tightened on purpose. Elara had written the report. Coordinated observation protocol. Authorized. Procedural. Six days of nothing, and on the seventh Preceptor Sen turned the monitor around and showed her her own pulse spiking at the exact second Iris's hand moved, her own breath held for four full seconds, the body confessing before the pen was picked up. *You lied to protect yourself from what your own body had already confessed.* Elara had hidden behind a procedure then and let it carry her name for her. Iris had carried the rest.

She opened the terminal's reconciliation routine and typed her own audit reference into the credential field.

The records post Trinity had buried her in had been built from exactly this work, and years of it had taught her the system's one lazy habit. Reconciliation needed an administrative session, and rather than wait for a superintendent the routine would mint the session token itself whenever the request arrived under a valid audit flag. The superintendent's key existed to prove a request was legitimate. So did an active audit flag, and hers was glowing on the central ledger at that second. Three seconds while the terminal checked its own paperwork and found it in perfect order. The system had been built to distrust people; nobody had taught it to distrust itself. The portal opened.

The cohort file took seconds to find and one minute to change. The terminal showed the edit the way ledgers always did, the old entry struck through beside the new:

GAGE. BEN. OTTO.
~~COORDINATED DEVIATION CLUSTER~~ STANDARD COHORT
~~ACTIVE — SEPARATION DIRECTIVES PENDING~~ WITHHELD PENDING EXECUTIVE REVIEW

A governance warning about record tampering came up. She confirmed through it and watched the status shift to saved. Three ordinary doors left open in a system that had already decided to close them.

She closed the session, and the routine cleaned up after itself the way housekeeping routines did: the self-minted token expired and the working log purged with it. The door sensors and the vault register were past her reach now. She picked up the folios and stepped back into the corridor.

Halfway down, an archivist on rounds turned the corner toward her, a mug hooked on his thumb through the handle. "Hold on."

She stopped.

"What are you carrying?"

"Shift ledgers. I'm under review."

Her pulse did what Sen had taught her it would. It climbed on *hold on*, and it sat back down on a sentence that was dull and true, because the body has nothing to argue with when the mouth isn't lying. The door sensor beside her shoulder blinked its patient green and took the number. The archivist looked at the grey tape on the spines, then at her face.

"Bad luck," he said, and went on with his mug.

She rode the Skylark up with four minutes left on the audit clock.

The review notice was still glowing on her console when she sat down. Ravi looked at her once. His eyes went to the folios under her arm, then to the archive confirmation fading from the corner of her display. He didn't need to ask where she'd been. That was what worried her.

The three linked signals held their pattern on her screen, and she left the connection visible and let the system record what it couldn't explain.

Then the central grid went dark. The lock released. The system informed them they had been under continuous observation for twenty-four hours and returned their bodies to them with the same indifference it had used to hold them.

All four fell back into their chairs at once. Ravi pressed his fingertips against his eyelids. Daren leaned forward with both palms on his knees, waiting for the floor to stop moving. Rohan turned his head slowly from the bright feeds to the dim recovery station beyond, like a man checking that the room had edges. Elara blinked against ordinary light; the walls looked washed out, then doubled, and behind her eyes the three signals stayed where the screen had put them.

Daren spoke across the dark consoles. His voice was rough from disuse. "We all know what happened in this room today. And it wasn't what was supposed to happen."

She said nothing. The accusation had found its mark, and the exhaustion in her face gave him nothing to hold.

"You fed the system interpretations it had already rejected and called them observations. Those three formed a pattern they were never meant to occupy, and every time the system moved to break it, you were standing in the way." He pushed up out of the chair and it rolled back behind him. "Being lead doesn't make the rules yours." He had crossed the floor by the end of the sentence, close enough that she felt his breath. "You made the system lie for you."

Rohan was between them before she could answer. He put one hand flat against Daren's chest, and Daren's forward motion ended against it.

"Step back."

Daren looked at the hand and then at the face above it. "This isn't your station."

"It is now. You're approaching an operator under review with your judgment shot. One step back, or I open the force ladder and write the reason myself."

Rohan's other hand rested near the ladder controls, and the first-stage warning had already surfaced on CORRECTION. Daren saw the record forming before Rohan entered it, and stepped away. Rohan lowered his hand. He didn't go back to his station.

Daren left first, carrying his anger like a report he meant to file. Ravi followed, unreadable in the blue.

Rohan stopped in front of her on his way out. "Whatever happens next, I will stand with you."

She opened her mouth — to ask what he thought next was, or what he thought it would cost him — and he was already walking. Under the corridor lights the scar along his jaw caught the blue and glinted like steel, less a mark of injury than a piece of armor he had worn so long it had grown in.

She waited until the recovery corridor had taken him before she moved. The walk to her quarters was a few dozen steps. The altered record sat inside the system like a second heartbeat, and the only question she let herself carry down the corridor was whether Rohan would still be standing there when the inquiry convened, or whether sixty hours without sleep had made that promise for him.

Far below, the mine gates stood open to the first grey light of the next cycle.

Gage walked out of the dark with a steady gait and two hundred and twenty pounds of himself under two other boys: Otto across his shoulders, Ben by the collar of his shirt. The guards' scanners took what they could measure — altered posture, a controlled pulse, a six-foot-five frame moving at the speed of its slowest passenger. They moved slowly, and they moved together. No monitor announced them. Gage believed they'd beaten the chamber with his back and his legs. Ben believed they'd kept the part of one another the chamber had wanted. Otto believed nothing he had words for; his face was turned toward the pale strip of sky past the gates.

Elara reached her door at the same hour. Her hand stopped above the panel. In the polished metal her reflection came back in pieces — one eye, the corner of her mouth, blue light cutting her face into planes that didn't fit together.

Below, three boys stepped out of the mine into whatever came after survival. Above, the woman who had kept them together stood at her own threshold, before a door that would still open for her, for now, and listened to the system decide what she would pay.
