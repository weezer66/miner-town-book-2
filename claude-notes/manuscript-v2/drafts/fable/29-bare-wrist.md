# Chapter 29: Bare Wrist

*"Lost an arm, not the rest of me. Get back to the panel," she said. Her voice was barely there — thin, cracked, coming from somewhere far away. But her eyes were steady.*

— Book 1, Chapter 23: The Blood Debt

## Now: Bare wrist

The floor of the round room was the cleanest thing Otto had ever stood on.

Pale stone, swept until the lamp lay on it in one unbroken circle, and his boots had brought the corridor in with them: two grey prints at the edge of the light, and nothing else on the whole floor to say anyone had ever walked there. He stood where he'd stopped, a little inside the near door, with his hands open at his sides. Behind him Camilla was breathing hard. He could hear the metal arm tick as it cooled from the run.

Nobody at the table was sitting except one.

Four old people had got to their feet, and the soft-faced man had left his chair and come round the end of the table and stopped, his hands still folded in front of him, a thing he'd forgotten to undo. Across the room, in the far door, stood the man from the corridor: black coat buttoned to the throat, right arm held out from his body, left hand flat on his chest over something in the coat. And at the table, under the lamp, the old man in gold sat with his hands bare on the wood and his rings in front of him in a row, and looked at none of them.

He spoke first. He spoke to the rings.

"Before anyone at this table says what they came to say, I will say it for them. It will be quicker."

His voice was dry and even and carried without being raised, a voice that had read figures to rooms for fifty years.

"Kubera's relic is not in its box. It has not been in its box for three years. The officer who took it is standing in that doorway." He didn't look up. "His name was Daren. He held a monitoring station in Drona's tower, and he was struck from my house's rolls for altering a record, in a chamber with black doors, before witnesses. I gave him a second chance in my household. That was a kindness, and I do not regret kindnesses. I did not know what he had carried out of the sealed box until a man died in Drona's tower, and then I made certain." Now he lifted his head, and looked at the three Grandmasters standing, one after another, and last at the woman in the darned shawl. "You called me without escort. I came without escort. I came to bring him to you."

Nobody at the table answered. The old woman in the shawl had gone very still.

Every eye in the room had gone to the far door.

Otto watched the man in the door take it. He'd learned to watch men take things in the alley behind the carts: where the weight went, what the hands did before the face caught up. The man's face did nothing. His weight went back onto his heels. His left hand pressed harder against his coat, and his right arm came out another inch from his body, and the fingers, which had been open, spread wider, a hand trying to get as far from itself as it could.

Something in the room began to hum.

It wasn't loud. It was under hearing, the way the turbine floor had been under hearing when Otto was six and lay with his ear on the shack boards to feel the mountain working. The lamp over the table brightened by a hair and steadied. Behind Otto, the near door moved on its hinge, an inch, and stopped. Along the curve of the wall a panel he hadn't seen before showed a thin line of light at its seam and went dark again. Every instrument this place owned had turned its head toward the far door and was waiting to be told what to do.

"This office's doors are answering him," the soft-faced man said, low, to no one. "I was not told they would."

The man in the door hadn't moved. His eyes had gone to the old man in gold and stayed there, and whatever he was feeling, his hand was feeling it louder.

Otto's left big toe lifted inside his boot. He hadn't asked it to. It came a hair off the sole and hung there, and he let it, because it had been right before.

Behind him Camilla said, "Otto," very low, and he heard her metal hand close on the doorframe.

"I know," he said, and walked.

---

He walked across the clean floor as he walked on soft ground, setting each foot down whole, and the only sound in the room was his boots. Round the end of the table. Past the soft-faced man, who didn't move. Past the standing woman with the bare wrist and white hair, whose eyes came onto him and widened by a fraction and went still. He didn't hurry. There wasn't anything to hurry for. The man in the door was watching him come, and the hum in the walls had climbed a note, and the far door stood open behind the man onto black.

He stopped at arm's length. His hands hung open.

Then the thing on the man's wrist reached for him.

He felt it as he'd felt the floor of the stillness: a pressure looking for a surface. It came over him and round him and went in under his ribs where fear lived, where it had lived the night of the stones, and it felt for the edges of him, for the flinch, the tightening, the small cold wish to be somewhere else. It went all the way down.

There wasn't anything there.

Otto stood in it and let it look. He thought of ten stones held open over fire, and of a floor at the bottom of the blue he'd stood on and almost not come back from, and of an old man's hand stopping a breath from his face, flat and still, in a dark hall where the lamp was on the floor. He'd been afraid of all of those. He wasn't afraid of this. He didn't decide not to be; there was nothing in him for the deciding to be done to. The thing on the man's wrist went over him and found a wall with no door in it, and slid, and came away.

The hum in the walls fell by a note. The lamp dimmed back to what it had been.

The man in the door made a sound in his throat that wasn't a word.

He was taller than Otto by a head, and older, and he'd come down here in a good coat with a thing on his arm that could tell every door in this place to kill, and he stood in the doorway now with his right arm out from his body and his fingers spread, and he looked at Otto's hands, and Otto's face, and Otto's hands again, like a man checking a figure he already knew was wrong.

"What are you?" he said.

"A miner." Otto kept his hands where they were. "I had a good teacher."

Up his bare forearms, at the edge of the lamplight, something showed. A faint blue, like heat coming off slag at noon. He didn't look down at it. He felt, under his breastbone, a pair of wings open and close once, slow, and didn't look for them either.

"You can stop," he said. "Nobody in here's going to hurt you."

The man's fingers stayed spread. His eyes went past Otto to the table, to the old man in gold, who hadn't turned round.

"You don't know what it does."

"No. I know what you're doing." Otto tipped his head a little toward the hand. "You're holding it off everybody. You've been doing it since the corridor. It's got to hurt by now."

The man's arm shook, once, along its whole length, and steadied.

Otto didn't move. The correct action was to be standing where he was standing, with his hands open, and he'd done it; what came next was the stop. He stood in the stop. The far door breathed cold air on the back of the man's coat, and the lamp held, and the four old people and the soft-faced man and his sister in the near door all waited for one of two men to move, and neither did.

Then a hand with metal in it took hold of his shoulder from behind and set him a step to the side.

---

The arm had started listening before she'd made it through the door.

Camilla had felt it on the run, and thought it was the run: a tightness up the metal from the wrist to the shoulder, the fingers wanting to close and being told not to and wanting it again. Then she'd stood in the doorway with her chest going like a bellows and the splinter still in her good fist, and the hum had come up through the floor into the arm's heel, and the arm had turned its hand toward the far door the way a face turns toward a voice it knows.

She'd heard that voice. Once, in a workshop two levels down, with her own arm open on the bench and the engineer's light in it. *The ear of the thing.* A web the size of a child's palm, listening up the nerves of her shoulder for what the hand wanted to do and doing it half a breath behind. She'd had that lag in her head every morning since, over cups and eggs and four dents in a table's grain, until she'd stopped hearing it.

She heard it now, because across the room something was doing the same work a beat ahead.

It came off the man in black in a shape she knew. A reach, a listening, an answer. The same web, turned round, running in front of its hand instead of after. Her arm heard it as she'd have heard her own name said in a strange mouth, and the fingers wanted to go to it. She made them let go of the doorframe. She crossed the floor past Otto with her boots loud and her good hand white round a sliver of wood, and she put the metal hand on his shoulder and moved him, and the arm did it on time, for once, because it had already been moving.

Then she was where he'd been, at arm's length from the hand, and it reached for her.

It found a wrist. The arm answered.

She felt them meet. Her metal fingers twitched, open, closed, late, and across the gap the black band fired, early, and between the two of them was a space the exact size of something she knew by feel, because it had been in her own chest for nineteen nights, on a stool, watching a finger walk down the clay to a split splinter and stop. It was the size of a fear.

"Oh," she said. "That's what you are."

The man in black was looking at her arm. Everyone was.

"It's not you," she said to him. She said it fast and flat, the voice she used on Gage when he was being slow. "It's a beat ahead of you. Mine's a beat behind me, and that's the whole difference between us. They made mine to learn what I want. They made yours to get there first." Her metal hand had come up between them without her, palm toward him, fingers curling and opening, and she let it, and watched his band answer every twitch. "It's listening to you being scared. That's the loudest thing in you right now, so that's what it hears."

"I know," he said. It came out of him scraped.

"Then you know what it's about to do." She didn't take her eyes off him. She jerked her chin at Otto. "Nothing in him for it to read. Nothing in me it wants. You're scared of *him*."

She looked at the Master.

He hadn't turned. He sat with his bare hands on the wood and his face toward his rings, and the back of his neck was an old man's neck, thin, with a fold of skin over the collar, and he didn't look round for her, and that told her she was right. Her arm told her the rest. Across the room the band was reaching, over and over, in the one direction, toward the table, toward the gold, and running up against something in its wearer that wouldn't let it finish, and coming back, and reaching again. The lamp over the table flickered each time. Nobody else in the room knew what the flicker was. She did.

And somewhere up above, in a chamber under a tower she'd never seen, a grey wall was writing all of this down.

She'd known for an hour. She'd carried it out of the control room on the run and not had time to turn it over. Every open and close of her hand since the day they'd fitted it had gone up through the rock to a room full of old people in Drona, and gone in a column, and someone had counted it. The engineer hadn't known. Flitzberg hadn't known, and he'd promised never to lie to her, and he hadn't, and it had been happening anyway.

She stood at arm's length from the thing that could kill the room, with her arm answering it, and thought: *then they're reading this too.*

Let them.

She opened the metal hand all the way, every finger, and held it there, flat, in the light, a thing to be read. Her shoulders came down. Her breath came down. Across the gap the band's reach met a hand that wasn't afraid of being watched, because it had never once been anything else, and the flicker in the lamp slowed.

"Take it off," she said to the man in black.

"I've tried."

"Try now. Something's different now." She said it as Gage said *clean*. "I can hear it."

---

His fingers found the edge of the band.

Daren hadn't told them to. They'd gone across his body on their own, the left hand to the right wrist, and hooked under the cold rim of the obsidian, two fingers, as they'd gone in a corridor with a dead man in front of him and in an unlisted room with a notebook under the mattress, and both times they had stopped.

He waited for them to stop.

The girl with the metal arm was watching him with her face set and her good hand clenched white around something. The miner boy beside her had his hands open and his eyes on Daren's, and the eyes had nothing in them he could use, no fear and no mercy, only attention. Behind them the four Grandmasters stood like four posts, and the soft-faced man had unfolded his hands at last and let them hang. And at the table, with his back to all of it, sat the man who had fastened this on him and said *it is cold the first night* in the voice of someone who knew.

His fingers didn't stop.

He felt the difference before he understood it. In the corridor he'd pictured the wrist underneath, pale and ridged and unfinished, and there had been no one standing behind it, and the fingers had let go because a man can't pull himself out of a thing when there's no one on the other side to pull. He pictured it now. The same wrist. The same ridge. And behind it, someone, after all: a man who had come down a hundred steps beside another man's footprints and kept his own feet out of them, with a dead stranger's paper pressed against his rib, because a name ought to be said in a room that listened.

He didn't know who that man was yet. He knew the man had a thing to do.

He pulled.

The band came. It came hard, over the heel of his hand, and the cold went with it, the whole length of the bone, and then it was in his left hand and his right wrist was in the air with nothing on it.

The hum went out of the room all at once. The lamp hung still. Somewhere along the curve of the wall a panel sighed and went dark and stayed dark, and the far door behind him, which had stood open on black all this time, swung in on its own weight and shut with a small plain sound, like any door.

He looked at his wrist.

Pale. A strip of skin that had never seen light. Ridged on the inner edge where the obsidian had sat, a finger higher than the old gold ridge beneath it, two marks now where there had been one. Unfinished. Bare. A twenty-year-old's wrist with nothing on it, in a room that could see him. He turned it over, and over again, and the room went on seeing him, and nothing in the room was told not to.

The band lay in his left hand, black, no heavier than it had been, cool as a stone out of a riverbed.

He walked.

Across the clean floor, past the girl and the miner, round the end of the table, to the place where the old man sat. He didn't look at the old man. He leaned over, and laid the band on the wood at the end of the row of rings, so that it made a sixth, bigger and blacker than the rest, and took his hand off it.

Then he opened his coat.

The notebook had taken the shape of his chest. It came out warm, the soft cover curled a little where it had lain against the rib, the chewed pencil stub still pushed down into the spine. He laid it on the table beside the band, square, with the worn corner toward the old man, and took his hand off that too.

He straightened. His right hand hung at his side. It was shaking now, properly, the whole hand, and he let it.

"A man died in the fourth-level corridor of the Drona tower," he said, "because my hand was afraid. He'd found my door. He'd found every door the instruments had been told to forget, and written them down, and the circles went round my room so many times the pencil went through the page." He heard his own voice in the round room, level, the voice he'd used at a station that no longer had his name on it. "This is his. I don't know his name."

Nobody at the table moved.

"Baju."

It came from the near door, behind the girl, behind the miner. A man's voice, low and even, with something under it held so hard it had gone flat.

Daren turned.

Red-black, no insignia. A pale scar along the jaw. A man built for doorways, standing in one, with his right hand split and glued at the knuckle and both hands at his sides, open, by what looked like effort.

"His name was Baju," Rohan said. "He counted things."

---

Elara had come in behind Rohan, and she'd known from the back of his neck.

She'd watched the whole of it from the screen room's grey eye and then she'd run, up the narrow stair she'd been led down, along the curving corridor toward the room she'd only ever seen from above, and he'd come out of a side passage ahead of her with the runner's lift sighing shut behind him, and hadn't seen her. She'd seen the back of his neck, and the set of his shoulders under the red-black, and the two fists he'd made and then unmade, finger by finger, as he walked. She'd come up behind him at the door and not touched him.

Now he went forward into the room, and she went with him, two steps behind, as she'd walked two steps behind the Ancient One into a blind.

The Dhammapala said, "Commander," once, without raising her voice.

He didn't stop. He didn't look at her. He went across the swept floor toward the man by the table, Baju's commander toward the man whose hand had killed him, and the man stood and waited for him with his bare wrist shaking at his side and didn't step back.

Elara saw it in Rohan's walk. The weight going forward onto the balls of his feet. The hitch under the right rib, which she'd learned in a corridor with no terminal on, caught and held and not let go. His right hand, with the fresh glue cracked across the knuckle, had closed again and he hadn't noticed. This was the thing the Dhammapala had named for him in a lamplit room, and he'd carried the name a month, and here it was: a door that would go through a wall if it had to, and wanted to, and would call that service.

She went round him. She didn't run. She put herself in the space between him and Daren, facing him, close enough to see the scar along his jaw go white, and she kept her hands at her sides.

He stopped because she was there. His eyes came down onto her and didn't see her for a second.

"Move," he said.

"No."

"He killed—"

"I know what he did. I was in the corridor. I saw his face in that door and I counted it." She didn't raise her voice. The room was listening; she'd let it. "Look at his wrist."

Rohan's eyes went past her shoulder and came back.

"It's on the table," she said. "He took it off himself. He put the notebook next to it, and he said what he did, in front of the four of them, with nothing on his arm to make anyone look away. The threat has ended. You told me what your house drills into you until it decides where you put your feet." She held his eyes. "*Stop when the threat has ended.* Not one blow after."

His breath went in and caught on the rib, and stayed caught.

"You told me about a girl with a purple belt," she said, lower. "Falling isn't the end of the lesson. You gave her the brown belt for saying it."

Behind her, Daren hadn't moved. She could hear him breathing. She could hear his hand against his coat, a small dry sound, shaking.

Rohan looked at her for a long time. Then his eyes went past her again, to the man, and stayed there, and whatever was in them she'd never seen before and hoped not to see again; and then his right hand opened at his side. The fingers came apart one at a time. The glue on the knuckle had split, and a bead of blood stood on it, and he didn't wipe it.

"I don't forgive you," he said, over her shoulder, in his ordinary voice.

"No," Daren said.

"He had a counter in his pocket. It clicked when he stood up. It's in a bag with his coat, and nobody who packed the bag knew what it was for."

"I'll say what it was for."

"You'll say all of it. Above. On a floor, with his name on the record, and yours."

"Yes."

Rohan's weight came back off the balls of his feet and settled, the floor asking him and him agreeing to it. He stepped back one pace. One, no more. Elara felt the air go out of her own chest and didn't let her shoulders show it.

She turned.

---

Daren was looking at the Master.

The old man hadn't turned round for any of it. He sat under the lamp with the row of rings and the black band and the soft notebook in front of him, and his bare hands on the wood, and he was looking at the band.

"He wore it first," Daren said.

He said it to the four. His right hand came up, shaking, and pointed, and the finger found the old man's right wrist where it lay on the table with the cuff fallen back.

"Look at the ridge. The gold leaves one by the heel of the hand. His has a second, a finger up. That's where the obsidian sits. It's on mine now." He turned his own wrist to the lamp, and the two marks showed. "He told me the first night. In the summit room, with it just closed on me. *It is cold the first night.*" His voice didn't break. It thinned. "A man doesn't know that from reading."

The Master of the Reserve didn't cover his wrist. He didn't look at it. He sat.

The Arthashastri had taken a step toward the table, small and upright in his green, and stopped. The Dhammapala's bare hand had closed at her side. And the Ancient One, who had stood through all of it with her hands folded in the darned shawl, unfolded them.

"Then I will say my part," she said, "since this table is already saying things aloud."

She didn't raise her voice either. None of them had; Elara had never heard four such quiet people.

"For a century, Drona has kept a witness. Every order given to an instrument in Kubera, in Chanakya, in Ashoka, has been copied into it without your knowing. Drona swore it had destroyed the reader's plans and kept nothing. Drona lied." Her eyes went round the table, one Grandmaster at a time, and came to rest on the soft-faced man. "And some weeks ago I learned that the witness hears Patal Loka too, through a hand this office built and did not know was listening back."

The soft-faced man's face did nothing. His hands, which had hung at his sides since the band came off, came together again and folded.

The room went quiet in a new way. Elara had known silences all her life, consoles between pulses, the dead room, a corridor with no terminal on her wrist. This one was four houses finding out at once that none of them had been blind where they'd thought they were blind, and each of them doing the sum.

The Master of the Reserve moved at last.

He didn't stand. He leaned forward a little over the table and looked at the band where it lay at the end of his rings, black against the pale wood, with the small notebook beside it. He looked at it a long time, and at nothing else, and at no one.

"Prove it," he said.
