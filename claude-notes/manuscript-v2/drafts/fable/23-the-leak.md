# Chapter 23: The Leak

*"The machines think this tunnel is a tomb. But if you live in the dark long enough, you find where the mountain breathes. There's a tiny leak here — not enough for a breeze, but enough to pull the rot out."*

— Book 1, Chapter 12: The Splinter Architect

## Then and now: The leak

Nayan was already there.

Elara saw the back of her first: grey fatigues, a straight spine, the far end of the dead-room table with her face to the wall, where she had sat on the morning Rohan had introduced four people with no names. The pencil was moving. The date and the hour stood in the top corner of the page. She had cut her hair since, or someone had, and the back of her neck was pale where it had been covered.

Their terminals went into the locker by the door and the drawer sighed shut on them. Rohan didn't sit.

"You told me I wouldn't see them again," Elara said.

"I said it in this room. I'll unsay it in this room." He stood with his back to the shut door, the way he stood on a mat before the first bow, weight even, nothing committed. "That rule kept five people alive while we went slowly. We're not going slowly. The slow way ended with Baju, and so does this."

Jeebh's chair stood against the wall on four legs, where someone had set it straight. The corner beside the locker was a corner. Elara had not seen any of the four since that morning, and she understood now that she had expected to see all of them or none, and that the room had been arranged to tell her otherwise.

The fourth panel hung where it had been pinned. The brass pin in its corner was still crooked, pushed in with a thumb by a man already turning to say something, and nobody had straightened it. Elara's hand had lifted before she knew it was going to.

The pencil stopped. "Leave it."

Nayan hadn't turned round. The pencil started again.

"You read Drona's public books," she said. "Every cycle?"

"Every cycle."

"Then you can check my copying. I've never had anyone who could. There's tea on the sill. It's cold." She turned the page. "Sit where the light is."

The light came down the shaft pale and a few seconds late, as it always did. Elara sat where it fell, with the fourth panel at her shoulder and the crooked pin in the edge of her eye, and found she could work with it there.

---

"Everything it's given," Elara said, "it's given out of Kubera's purse."

The three of them had the fourth panel between them, the Annex approval in a runner's pencil, and beside it her own list: the doubled transfer into Drona's library, the Annex, the mandate review that had put a guardian in every doorway. Three gifts. Three councils, three votes, three sets of signatures, every one of them Kubera's.

"Kubera's council voted all three," Rohan said. "It can spend its own money."

"It can. It hasn't, for eleven years, and then it did three times in one season." She set her pencil down across the list. "Either the richest house in the city found its generosity all at once, or the hand spends Kubera's money as if it's the hand's own. And if it does that—" She looked at the four panels. "What if it isn't the only one spending other people's money?"

Nayan had put down her pencil, which Elara had not yet seen her do.

"Six years," Nayan said. "I've copied four sets of books for six years. Every cycle, every house, dated. I keep them because a record that changes overnight can't argue with a pencil. I've never once put them next to each other. Nobody's asked."

"Then nobody has to," Rohan said. "Do it."

They came from the inner locker, every key turning together: six years of pads, their cardboard backs gone soft, each top corner dated in the same small hand. Then from a canvas satchel under the table. Then, when the satchel was empty, a runner Elara never saw knocked twice and left a laundry basket at the door, and the rest came out of that, under a folded sheet.

Four columns of paper.

Nayan wouldn't let them lie where they fell. Each house kept its own calendar, Drona's cycles against Kubera's tenth days against the two others, and she squared every pad to the quarter it belonged to and nothing else, shifting a stack an inch, then half an inch, until the same month stood level across all four columns. "If they don't line up, I'm reading four stories," she said. "If they line up, I'm reading one." Rohan moved the lantern when she told him to and nowhere else.

Drona went down the length of the table, oldest at the wall end. Kubera went along the floor under the panels. Chanakya and Ashoka ran between them, edge to edge, so that a person on her knees could read one quarter of one year across all four houses without moving her head. The shavings tin held down Ashoka's corner. Rohan's boot held down Kubera's. The mirror shaft dimmed when a cloud crossed, and then the day went out of it for good, and Nayan lit the lantern with the true flame and set it on the floor in the middle of the paper, where it threw the four columns long across the stone.

Elara worked on her knees. Her left knee found a crease in Chanakya's third year and she shifted off it. Pencil lead got into the lines of her palms, and she stopped noticing. Nayan went up and down the columns on her hands and feet, never on her knees, like a woman crossing a floor she didn't trust, and read every line of her own copying aloud under her breath.

They found the hand's gifts first, because they were looking for them. The doubled transfer sat in Drona's column with nothing in Kubera's to match it that quarter. The Annex approval sat in Kubera's with Chanakya's petition opposite it, eleven years of trimmed refilings in a row, and then the one with no rings. The mandate review's first postings, confirmed in Kubera's hall, in Ashoka's books as a line of duties funded. Rohan tore slips from a pad and laid one across each, and when he had laid the third he sat back on his heels and looked at the three slips: large, few, and all inside this season.

"That's it," he said. "That's all of it."

"That's all of the hand," Nayan said.

She was two years back along Drona's column, flat on her front with the lantern at her elbow, and she hadn't moved for some time.

"What have you got?"

"Filter media. Forty units." Her finger stayed on the line. She reached across with the other hand, without looking, and put it on Kubera's column, in the same quarter of the same year. "Filter media. Forty units." Chanakya. "Forty." Ashoka. "Forty."

"Four houses buy filters," Rohan said. "Four houses have air."

"Same month. Four houses that can't agree on what day it is, buying the same forty units inside the same week." Nayan's finger hadn't left Drona's line. "Every quarter. Six years. Look at the approval."

Elara looked. The approval column on a public book carried a clerk's number, every line, because a clerk's number was the whole point of a public book. These four carried none. Where the number should have been was a founding mark, the kind printed on the top of the page beside the house's name, the kind nobody was alive to sign.

"A standing order," Rohan said. He was testing from the other side, as he always did. "Every house has them. Lamps. Salt. Nobody signs for salt."

"A standing order has a clerk who set it, and the clerk has a number, and the number's on the line." Nayan tapped the blank where it should have been. "Show me the clerk. I've looked for six years' worth. There isn't one. The book carries it the way it carries the house's own name."

Nayan was moving again. "Generator parts, spring. Paper, autumn. Medical stock, every second month, three crates." Her hand went down each column in turn and never once missed. "Small. Regular. All four at once, for six years, and the way it sits, longer. I only go back six."

"Where does it go?" Elara said.

Nayan turned to Drona's receiving stores for that quarter, four pages on, and laid her finger flat. "Drona's stores took in no filter media that quarter. Not forty units. Not four." She didn't bother with the others. She waited while Elara crawled along the floor and checked them herself, and Elara found the same blank in each.

"The money leaves," Nayan said. "The thing doesn't arrive. Not anywhere a house would use it. Every house thinks it's the one doing the buying."

Nobody said anything.

"One supplier," Rohan said at last. "Four houses, one bad set of books at the far end, and we're looking at its mistake four times over."

"Then somewhere there'd be a yard with six years of filters in it, and a man wondering why nobody ever came for them." Elara shook her head. "Nothing's piling up. Whatever this is, it takes delivery. It just doesn't take it anywhere a house can see."

Elara sat back. The lantern leaned when she breathed out. She put her hand on the small line in Drona's column and then on the three slips of torn paper across the room, and she saw the difference before she had the words for it.

"It isn't the hand," she said.

Rohan looked up.

"The hand's gifts grow. A transfer. Then a wing. Then a mandate for the whole city. Each one bigger than the last, and each one dressed as something a council voted for." She touched the small line. "This never grows. Forty units six years ago. Forty units last month. It doesn't dress. It doesn't go through a council. It sits in the book like the name at the top of the page, and it takes the same small mouthful from every house at once, and the houses have never seen it because none of them has ever done what we did tonight." She looked at the four columns running away into the dark past the lantern. "The hand feeds. This one eats. Just enough. For a very long time."

Something that was not a house had been fed by all four, for longer than the paper could show. She knew that much, and she could feel the shape of what she didn't know, and for a breath the shape was a row on a grey wall with no house and no floor beside it. She let the breath go. She couldn't put that wall on this floor, and she wasn't going to try.

Rohan hadn't moved.

She had seen him still before, in a corridor with a strike coming. This was the other stillness, the one a man goes into when he has stepped on a floor and found it already warm from someone else's feet.

"Safeguards older than Ashoka," Elara said. "You said that to the Ancient One."

"My Grandmaster said it to me. She said something else with it." His voice had gone to the register he used for instructions, except that these weren't his. "'If your hunt ever crosses a path that feels like your own thinking arriving ahead of you, do not chase it. Mark the place, and bring it to me.'"

"Your own thinking, arriving ahead of you."

"We laid four sets of books side by side because no one ever had." He looked at the columns. "And something's been counting on that for longer than I've been alive. We didn't find it. We walked into it."

"I want to chase it."

"I know. So do I." He got to his feet, and she heard the breath he took to do it. "We don't chase it. We mark the place."

"Mark it how?" Nayan said.

"Your hand. Dated. One sheet. What it is, how long, how much, which houses. No guess at what it might be, because we haven't got one, and the mind hunts what the mouth names." He looked down at the small line in Drona's column. "I'll carry it tonight."

Two knocks came at the door, close together.

Nayan went to the slot and drew out the envelope and broke the wax with her thumbnail, and read, and held it out to Rohan without a word, because that was still the rule. One line, in block letters.

*The ear has spoken to the analyst. He kept the appointment.*

Under it, smaller: *He carries an old file. He will not say what is in it.*

"Kaan doesn't speak," Rohan said.

"He did," Elara said.

Rohan folded the paper once and put it under the shavings tin, on Ashoka's corner, and stood looking at it. "Everything's breaking in order."

Nayan was already back on the floor with a clean sheet, writing the date in the top corner.

---

The lamp room smelled of cloves before Rohan reached the door.

He knelt. His knees found the cushion and the rest of him arrived after them, which was new, and he put his hands on his thighs and kept the weight off his right side without letting it show. The flame stood straight until the Dhammapala breathed out, and then it leaned toward the door.

He had brought nothing. Nayan's sheet was in the inner locker, under four keys. He brought what it showed.

Four columns of paper, laid out on a floor, four houses' public books, six years of them, put side by side by someone who had kept them for six years without once doing it. On top of them, the hand's gifts: three, large, all this season, each bigger than the last, each dressed as a council's own idea. Under them, a line so small he would have walked past it alone. Filter media, generator parts, medical stock, paper. Every house buying for itself, in the same month, the same amounts, every quarter, approved on a founding mark no clerk could sign. Never growing. Goods that arrived nowhere a house would use them. Older than the hand by the look of it, and steady, and never once seen, because no house had ever put its books beside the others' and the thing had counted on that.

He gave her Elara's sentence at the end, because it was the best one anyone had said. The hand feeds. This one eats.

The Dhammapala listened with her eyes closed. One hand lay on her knee with the fingers curled. Her breathing didn't change, and the flame leaned and stood and leaned.

When she opened her eyes she looked at him for some time.

"You did not chase it."

"No."

"Good."

The lamp hissed once on something in the wick.

"There is one room in this city where the four of us sit together," she said. "Not in any house. We do not choose the day. We are fetched to it and we are returned from it, and none of us can say where it is. Once a year." She paused. "This year it has been asked to sit out of season. The asking was not mine. I had wondered what I would carry to it, and whether I would carry anything. Now I know. It will be answered there."

"By whom?"

"By whoever has been eating, if the balance is kind."

He waited. There wasn't any more.

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it has already been answered, and we are late." She said it as she might have named a stance. "You will not be in that room. You will not look for it. You will go on hunting the hand, which is still the thing in this city that is afraid, and the thing that is afraid is the thing that moves."

"Yes."

She was looking at the way he sat.

"You have been hit."

"A corridor. Days ago. It's nothing."

"You are kneeling like a man who has been hit and has decided it is nothing, and you have not slept, and you came here before you went home." Her voice didn't rise. "I told you about doors. A door that is eager to break leans into its own frame, and calls the leaning duty. Sit back from the frame. The house will need you to open more than once."

He let the weight go onto his right side. It hurt exactly as much as he'd thought it would, and he let that show too, because she had seen it already and there was no honor in hiding from someone who had seen.

She rose, and bent, and put the lamp out the old way, two fingers to the wick. The dark came in with the smell of cloves, and the smoke went up straight, with no breath left in the room to lean it.

"They will send someone," she said, without looking at him. "Someone who reads patterns."

He didn't ask who they were. He had found he could live without knowing, and he knelt in the dark a while longer, keeping his weight where she'd told him to put it, until the smoke had gone wherever smoke goes.
