# Chapter 8: The Vacancy

*Seeking the final piece, Gage sat with the Worker #4055 tag hanging between his fingers. The system didn’t retire numbers — it refilled them. When Gage survived the Initiation and entered the intake line, the processing clerk had stamped Thorton’s vacated slot onto his son’s chest without a second glance. The position was the asset; Gage was just the next body assigned to fill it. But what was once a symbol of his father’s disposal had become a key to the machine’s destruction. He walked to Camilla.*

— Book 1, Chapter 6: To Hell and Back

## Then: The Drona cafeteria

The Drona cafeteria in the last week of a cycle was loud enough to make secrecy look like fatigue.

Trays struck the return belt. The drink dispenser hissed and clicked. A maintenance cart went along the far wall on one uneven wheel, dipping every third turn like a man favoring a knee, and three students by the windows argued over a schedule none of them could change. The noise broke speech into harmless pieces. Anything overheard at the next table became one more complaint about rationed coffee.

Anandi had taken a table at the center of the room, where no one could approach unseen. Kavik sat with his back to the service line, turning a mug of nutrient stew between his hands as if he could warm it from the outside. Elara's braised grain had cooled into a surface she hadn't disturbed.

"They have thinned it again," Kavik said, into the mug. "Last week it held a spoon upright. This week the spoon falls over and apologizes."

"The ledger has to close," Anandi said.

"The ledger could close with salt in it. The kitchens have declared war on salt. I believe salt is losing."

Drona never went hungry the way Miner Town did, and it never ate the way Kubera did. Its allocations were fixed and counted to the gram, and at the end of a cycle the kitchens watered the broth and shaved the portions so the numbers came out even.

Anandi asked whether Elara had slept since the extended cycle. Whether her shoulders still ached from the days held upright at the console. Elara answered what she was asked and pushed her spoon through the grain without lifting it.

A stack of trays went over on the belt.

Kavik leaned in under the noise. "You have been carrying the council's words since you left the chamber."

"I've been trying to work out whether they gave me words or a load." She kept her voice under the room. "Sarvajña has a library wing and a funding request that stopped moving when it reached Kubera. Draṣṭā has three cards. They both see a shape, and neither of them will tell me what it is."

"Because they do not know," Anandi said.

Elara looked at her. "They're elders."

"That is a different thing from being omniscient." Anandi lifted her spoon and set it down without using it. "Sarvajña knows what the records permit him to prove. Draṣṭā knows that a pattern is approaching. Neither knowledge becomes certainty because it frightens you."

It should have eased something. Instead the space around her next question got wider.

"Then why do I feel like I've already done too much?"

"Because you acted," Kavik said.

The dispenser let out a burst of steam. Somewhere behind them a chair scraped hard across the floor.

"I altered the record." It nearly went under the clatter. "I kept Gage, Ben and Otto in the same Sector One cohort. I told myself I was preserving what they chose in the chamber. I don't know what I preserved. Their bond might protect them. It might make them visible in the worst way there is. Three boys who could've survived separately, turned into a unit every authority in Trinity wants to break."

Across the room a student laughed too loudly, and the sound broke against the ceiling and was gone.

"Or it bought them time," Anandi said.

"That isn't an answer."

"No. It is the truth you are resisting. You want an answer that makes your choice innocent. There may not be one."

Elara looked at the cold grain. She had wanted Anandi to call the alteration necessary, or wise, or already justified by what the system had been about to do. She had wanted the council's warning to turn her fear into a duty so plain it stopped belonging to her.

Kavik set his mug down. "The pathways are yours, Elara. Larger things may be moving, even toward those boys. A road existing does not excuse the one who takes it."

"And if I took the wrong one?"

"Then you will answer for it. As all of us do."

It hurt, and it didn't leave her alone with the hurt. Anandi laid her hand over Elara's closed fist on the table.

"You did not put courage into them. You did not survive the Initiation in their bodies. You refused to let Trinity file their survival as a reason to punish them before they reached the gate. That may have consequences. It also has a moral shape. Do not confuse uncertainty with permission to abandon judgment."

Elara breathed in. Hot metal, citrus cleanser, food kept warm too long. The ordinary smells steadied her more than the council chamber had. There, every silence had been built to force something out of her. Here, silence was the gap between one tray and the next.

Kavik drew a narrow slip of paper from inside his sleeve and laid it by her tray. It looked like a cafeteria receipt. An intake batch in the header. Most of the lines blank, one entry near the bottom.

WORKER SLOT 4055
PRIOR HOLDER: THORTON, DECEASED. DEPENDENT OF RECORD: GAGE.
VACANCY: OPEN, UNASSIGNED

Elara didn't touch it. "Thorton's slot. Gage's father."

"When a worker dies, the ledger keeps the household," Kavik said. "It flags his registered dependents as eligible for the vacancy. Eligible. The intake queue still pools every open slot against every incoming body and stamps them blind. Our contacts found the flag. They did nothing with it."

"The system has no column for fathers," Anandi said. "Only for numbers waiting on a body."

The slip looked colder for it. Elara remembered the reports: Gage's resistance, the bruised steadiness of him, the way he had carried the others without pretending it made him unbreakable. A tag couldn't give him anything back. It couldn't make the mine less hungry.

"Why bring it to me?"

Kavik turned his mug a quarter. "Because the slot is still open, and it should not be. When a worker dies at his post, a safety inspection opens on the slot. Nothing can be assigned to it until the inspection closes."

"How long does that take?"

"Weeks, if the inspectors are thorough. Days, if they are not."

"And you can reach the inspection."

"Our contacts can. It is the one thing in the mine they can reach."

"That doesn't give you the assignment," Elara said. "Assignment's blind. You just said so."

"Blind, yes. Random, no." He glanced across the room, measuring the distance to the nearest ears. "The clerk works down the line and stamps each worker with whatever open number the queue holds."

"Then Gage gets whatever's open."

"There is never anything open. More bodies than slots, always. A number appears and is on someone's chest within minutes. The queue runs empty."

"So an empty queue, and one inspection that closes—"

"In the minute he reaches the clerk." Kavik's voice stayed level. "Then his father's number is the only one the queue has. No one chooses him for it. The queue has nothing else to give."

"And the clerk stamps it without knowing why."

"The clerk sees an ordinary vacancy in an ordinary queue," Anandi said. "Which is how the system prefers to work."

Elara read the number again. Thorton's slot, emptied by a death Trinity had entered as a disposal and a vacancy. She could see the tag becoming another weight on Gage's chest, proof that the city expected him to replace what it had used up.

"This isn't protection."

"No," Kavik said.

"It could put him in exactly the work that killed his father."

"It could," Anandi said. "We cannot promise safety by moving one line in an allocation system."

"Then why this line?"

Anandi watched the workers clearing tables around them. "Because the queue will stamp a number on him whether we act or not. Every other slot in the pool makes him a location and a task. This one carries his father: the place he worked, the record of what he did, the history Trinity filed as a disposal. If the city insists on giving him a dead man's position, it can give him his own dead." She turned her palm up on the table. "He may see more in it than that. He may use it. He may refuse it, or decide it means only grief. Those decisions stay his."

"We are offering him a condition," Kavik said. "Nothing more. Our intention is small: that the number he carries should mean something to him instead of nothing. What he makes of it is his to make."

Elara thought of the boys walking out under the gate lights, each carrying what the chamber had done to him. She had never seen their future. She had seen what Trinity meant to do next, and refused it. This was smaller than a future and more dangerous than a certainty: a number on a routine form, a dead man's labor position, a thin line to a map Gage might one day learn to read.

Another shift came in. The three of them were swallowed in trays and chairs and voices. No one looked at their table. No one had a reason to.

Elara took the notice between two fingers.

"If we do this, we do it because the opening already exists. We put it where he'll find it. We don't tell ourselves it will keep him alive."

"Agreed," Kavik said.

"And no one touches the boys' choices after this. Not through a record. Not through a warning dressed up as help."

Anandi held her eyes. "That boundary is yours to set."

She could still refuse. Leave #4055 to the blind rotation and call restraint cleaner than intervention. Clean hands had never been the same as harmless ones.

"Then do it the way you said. Keep the inspection on his father's slot open until Gage stands at the head of the line, and close it in that minute, when the queue is empty and #4055 is the only number it can offer him. Nothing forged. Nothing named. Only timing." She set the notice down between the three of them. "I choose that."

Kavik nodded once and left his hand open on the table beside it.

Elara folded the notice along its existing crease. The paper made almost no sound under the room.

---

The administrative archive sat behind the public research stacks, narrow and windowless, three terminals built into a wall of pale glass. Drona officers reconciled grants and access orders here, and kept the old records that stopped Trinity's institutions from contradicting one another in public. Behind the glass, between a panel and the light that lit it, a moth had died years ago with its wings spread flat, and every time a terminal woke it lay there in its own small square of brightness.

The library wing was the one piece of Sarvajña's evidence that lived in records Drona could lawfully open. If the request was still frozen in Kubera's treasury, the allocation file would show the hold, and a hold had to be entered by someone. A name, an office, a date. Any of them would give the closed rooms an address.

Anandi stood at the center terminal. Kavik opened the file beside her.

It showed no hold. Expansion approved. Funding released. Designation pending. Kubera's transfer sat in the file as a completed figure with no correspondence attached, money that had moved through the system without passing through anyone's hands. The figure was twice the original request.

"Twice," Elara said.

"Twice," Kavik said. "Someone has been generous, and would rather we did not thank them."

The record was exact about everything except what they had come for. No name. No office. No date of decision. When Sarvajña stood before the council, the request had been frozen. Between his warning and tonight, someone had let the money move, and doubled it.

"They cleaned the trail."

"Redacted," Anandi said. "Cleaned implies there is nothing beneath."

She drew up the record map: the public copy, and under it the native authorization record the copy had been generated from. The native layer sat behind a legacy firewall, which took Elara's clearance and handed it back unread.

Kavik studied the denial. "Your clearance is sound. The firewall predates it. It wants permission in a language the archive stopped speaking before you were born."

"The transfer released days ago. Why file it in a dead language?"

"Because whoever filed it chose to," Anandi said. "The old protocol still authenticates. It predates every audit tool built since. A transaction entered through it is valid and nearly invisible in the same stroke."

"Can you get through it?"

"Around it," Anandi said. "The Sākṣī path."

The witness path. The original Drona filing convention, a name Elara had met only in training exercises. In the gana's first century no authorization had been trusted on its own word; every record wrote a second copy of itself in parallel, kept apart from the hands that made the first. The witness path had dropped out of the interface generations ago. It had never been decommissioned.

Kavik entered a string of old ledger intervals, rebuilding the sequence that linked the library request to Kubera's transfer to the final approval. Numbers crossed the screen one after another until a second prompt opened beneath the first. Native authorization record. Access provisional.

Elara's pulse moved once at her throat. "This leaves a trace."

"A lawful audit trace," Anandi said. "We are reading a Drona record."

The native document opened.

It was shorter than she expected. No one had asked for the additional funds and no one had granted them; the doubled sum was written into the authorization itself, one decision where there should have been two. The destination field was blank. The purpose field held a single classification: *Continuity Reserve*. Every supporting note had been stripped before the document entered the current archive.

At the bottom, where a signing authority should have been, two letters.

OG

"Who's OG?"

Kavik ran the authority registry. No officer, elder, department or gana designation matched. He widened it to retired titles, emergency councils, sealed succession records. The screen gave the same answer each time.

NO REGISTERED AUTHORITY.

And the document held. The archive had accepted the signature. Kubera had released the funds. Drona had received them. Every system built to reject an unknown authority had treated two letters as enough.

Anandi looked at them without touching the screen. "A title can be erased from a registry. That does not mean the power behind it has disappeared."

Elara thought of Draṣṭā's cards, Sarvajña's stalled request, money moving inside Kubera with no hand on it. She had lifted the public record away, then the firewall, and at the center two letters sat in a field that should have carried a name, obeyed by a system that could not say what they were.

Behind the glass the moth lay in its square of light.

The provisional window began to count down. Elara closed the document before it could close itself, and her hand stayed on the glass afterward, flat, where the letters had been.
