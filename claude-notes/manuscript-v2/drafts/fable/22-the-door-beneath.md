# Chapter 22: The Door Beneath

*The moment they were inside, it sealed behind them. The crystal from the first room hung in the center again — but now one quarter of it glowed green. Their first success, recorded and visible.*

— Book 1, Chapter 23: The Blood Debt

## Now and then: The door beneath

Two levels down, the alert had no voice. It was a lamp over the door that went from nothing to white and stayed there, and under it a sound like a spoon on a rail, and the three of them were on their feet before any of them had said what it was.

Ben went through the door first with one boot on. Gage went past him on the stair, three steps to a stride, one hand on the rail and the other open. Camilla came last, counting, because the arm woke slower than she did. For the first flight it hung off her shoulder like a bag of tools. Then it caught up with her and swung a beat behind, and by the second landing it was running with her. Twenty-two steps. Twenty-two more. The tank room's light was already up.

Otto's hand was open.

The whole hand. It was still opening when she came through the door, the fingers coming away from the thumb one at a time, slow, as if he'd been holding something heavy a long while and had decided to put it down. His eyes were open behind the mask. They weren't on anything yet.

The medic stood at the glass with her thumb on the speaker switch. She didn't turn round.

"Otto. Follow my finger."

She moved it left along the glass, and the eyes went with it, late. Right. Up. They went.

"Good. Now the palm. Put your palm to the glass."

The hand came up through the gel, not straight, drifting, and found the glass and lay flat against it, fingers spread, and stayed.

Gage put both of his against it from the other side.

He didn't speak. He stood with his two hands on the glass round Otto's one, the knuckles still rough from a month of benches, and leaned his forehead on the cold between them and stayed there. Ben was at his shoulder. Camilla stopped two strides inside the door, where she could see the panel and the three of them and the medic's face. Her metal hand had closed. She opened it.

Four lines on the panel. Four moving.

"He is breathing on his own," the medic said. She said it to the panel, and then she said it again to the glass, and her voice was the same both times. "We start the drain."

It went by stages. A valve under the base, a sound like a cistern filling somewhere far off, and the blue sank. It went down past his hair, and his hair stopped lifting and lay flat on his forehead, wet. Camilla had never seen it lie down. It went past the mask. The second medic came in with the lid key. The line came out of the mask and the mask came off his face, and there was Otto's face under it, thinner, the mouth working at nothing, a mouth that had forgotten it had a job. Past his chest. Past the hand, which stayed on the glass until the glass was dry and then slid, slowly, down it.

Somewhere in that, Ben went out. Camilla saw the door swing and didn't go after him.

They took Otto out through the open side of the base, two of them under his arms, nothing jarred. They put him on the bed beside the tank, sitting up against the wall, and the grey-haired medic put a blanket across his lap and tucked it under his knees herself, and stood back. His eyes went round the room. They weren't finding things yet. They were trying to make a world out of the light, and the light kept getting there first.

Gage stood at the side of the bed with his hands hanging.

Otto's eyes came round and found him. They stopped.

His lips moved before any sound did. On the second try the sound came, cracked down the middle, a voice nobody had used in a month.

"You promised."

Gage's face did what it did when the rock talked. Camilla watched the map go white.

Then, drier, with the corner of the mouth going, the Otto who'd called himself a car: "I jumped. Took me a while."

Gage's hands came up and covered his face.

Both of them, flat, the fingers in his hair, and he stood like that beside the bed with his shoulders going, and no sound came out of him at all. She had never seen those hands do it. They'd been open all month, laid on benches and held at his sides where a room could read them. Now they were over his face, and nobody could, and Otto looked at him from the pillow with the same patience he'd had for the big idiot since they were six.

Ben was back. She hadn't heard him come. He stood in the door with his chest going and his one boot on and the other foot bare on the metal, and he didn't come in, and he didn't say what he'd gone for.

Otto's eyes left Gage and went looking for him, and found him.

"Did they hold?"

Ben's hand had gone to his hip, over the pocket that wasn't there. He came in then.

"All ten." His voice came out steady, which cost him; she saw what it cost. "Right till we were through the door. We walked out under them. Then you let them down."

"On me."

"On you."

Otto took that and looked at the blanket a while, and his right hand came up off it, shaking, and caught the edge, and pulled it higher. Then his eyes went past Ben, and past Gage's hidden face, to the door, and to her. They went to her left side and stopped.

She let him look. There was no point making him do it later.

"I know," she said. "I'll tell you the rest when your head's ready."

He didn't nod. His eyes went from the grey hand to her face and back, and then they closed, and the medic was at the bed with two fingers on his wrist before anyone had seen her move.

"Asleep," she said. "Sleep is his. Let him keep it."

Ben had come to stand by Camilla in the door. He spoke without turning his head, low, under the medic's voice.

"It's stopped."

"What has?"

"The blue. Down in the room." He rubbed one eye with the heel of his hand. "It's not keeping time anymore. It's just lit. Steady. It's still blue."

She waited for the rest. There wasn't any. He'd counted it for twelve nights, and he had nothing to say about it, and neither did she.

Gage's hands came down. He wiped them on his trousers, both at once, and looked at Otto asleep with his mouth a little open, and then he sat on the floor with his back against the tank's base because his legs had decided for him. The medic let him.

Camilla stood in the door and counted the nights, because counting was what she did when nothing else would hold still.

Nineteen from the briefing. Twelve the day of the egg. She'd kept the count in her head and in no book, Pappy's rule for anything that mattered, and tonight it was seven. Seven nights, and the boy on the bed knew nothing about any of it: the water, the valves, the ten minutes, the finger that kept ending on the same place on the wall. He'd gone down holding ten stones over three lives and come up asking if they'd held.

She'd tell him. Tonight he could have the blanket.

She counted the room one more time, by habit, and got to four, and all four of them were awake, and she had to put her good hand on the doorframe.

---

The records archive opened at first bell.

Ravi went in through the public gate with his terminal on, because the gate needed to know him, and once the gate had him he put the terminal dark in his pocket and left it there. Past the gate, nothing asked. The paper index stood in its bays where it had stood for a century, bound in green cloth gone grey at the spines, and he followed the old number from one register to the next with a finger that had learned the trick three years ago and never forgotten it.

The number ended at a sealed case. Findings restricted. He'd known that since the day they sealed it.

He wrote the request by hand at the retrieval desk. The clerk read the number and then read him.

"This case is sealed above your grade."

"I filed the report. I want the original folio, not the terminal rendering."

She turned the custody register round and ran a finger down it. The findings were the inquiry's. The folio was its author's, released to him for one night's custody, against his signature in the paper ledger, which she turned toward him. He signed beneath the file number. His signature had changed in three years. He hadn't noticed until he saw the old one above it.

The folio came up from storage in a sleeve with a cracked corner. His handwriting. His date. He didn't read it; he turned to the fourth page, where a line was underlined in pencil, lightly, by a first-year analyst who had expected someone above him to say what it meant before the week was out. The underline was still there. Nobody above him had ever said. He kept his face where it was and closed the sleeve.

"Tomorrow?" the clerk said.

"Tomorrow."

He walked out through the public gate with the terminal dark against his hip and the folio inside his coat, flat against the two slips. It wasn't a promise he knew how to keep. He'd made it anyway, in the house's own ledger, in ink.

---

Room Four was empty at the second hour.

He stood in the doorway longer than the policy corridor liked. The table where he'd closed a document in a booking he hadn't made. The two chairs. The reading lamp with its shade tilted where someone had knocked it. No one at the table. No one behind the door.

On the nearer chair, square to the edge, lay a binder.

The spine was split and mended with brown tape. He knew the tape before he knew the binder: a trolley wedged in a cloakroom door, a heel, a grey sleeve, a weight put into his arms without a word. He'd reached for the porter's face that morning and found a door instead.

He opened it. The rings held nothing. In the sleeve inside the front cover, one retrieval tag, the cheap house paper, block letters.

*Tomorrow. Bring what you took.*

He read it once. He put it back in the sleeve and closed the binder and set it on the chair exactly as it had been, and then he sat down in the other chair, because the house would log that Room Four had been used and it might as well be true.

Nobody came.

He sat with the folio against his chest under his coat and his hands over it, a man holding his coat shut on a crowded tram. The lamp buzzed at the end of every third breath. Whoever had left the tag knew he would come, and knew what he'd gone for at first bell, and knew the house's own paper well enough to write on it. Whoever it was had not needed to be in the room.

He stayed the hour. He'd booked it. He didn't open the sleeve again.

---

The tower's register desk opened with the first bell, and Elara was at it when the shutter went up.

Paper. A ledger a hand's width thick, ruled by hand, lawful under her code, lying on a counter that a century of elbows had worn into two shallow dishes. Every cycle Drona lent the other houses' monitors rooms in this tower for the Initiation watch, and every room went into the ledger in a clerk's hand, with a date assigned and a date released. She asked for the Sector One cycle. The clerk brought it without asking why; reviewers asked for stranger things.

She found the fourth level on the third page.

A room, a number, a house, a name. *Kubera. Monitor Daren. Initiation watch.* Assigned, with a date she knew, because she'd been assigned the same day.

Released: nothing. The column was empty. Every other borrowed room on the page had been closed out within a week of the watch ending, in the same brown ink. This one had been left open and had stayed open, cycle after cycle, under the eyes of clerks who had no reason to look at it.

She stood with her finger on the line.

A name, and a room. Both lawful. Both hers now, in a hand that wasn't hers, on paper anyone could ask for. A room kept open by a man no house had on its rolls, on the floor where the hand went home. Put beside a face in a doorway, in bad light, it was a shape.

It wasn't proof. She wrote the line out in pencil, page and number and both dates, and closed the ledger herself.

Anandi was standing on the stair.

Folded paper in one hand, nothing at all in her face. Elara took the paper and opened it. Three words.

*Beneath the archive. Now.*

---

The grey wall was dark.

That was the first thing. The second was the map: paper, old, creased at the folds and flattened under two brass weights, lying on the long table where the night before last she and Rohan had sat on chairs older than the table. The Ancient One sat at the head of it with the darned shawl over her shoulders and her hands folded on the wood. No one else. Anandi had stopped at the door and gone.

"Anandi told me where you would be standing," the Ancient One said. "The wall told me who walked past you."

Elara had asked Anandi for the fourth-level maintenance plan and nothing else, and Anandi had given it and asked nothing. She put that where she could find it later.

"I have a register line," she said. She gave it as a reading: the ledger, the page, the room, the name, the assigned date, the column that had never been closed. "And I have a face in a doorway that I'd swear to. I'm keeping them apart. The line is a fact. The face is mine. Together they're still not proof that he's the hand."

"Good." The old woman's eyes didn't leave her. "Do not turn recognition into proof. You have a man who kept a room. You have not yet shown that the room is the one."

"No."

"There is a third thing, and it is not yours." The Ancient One touched the table once. "This tower writes a digest every night. Dull. Who went where, and which gates asked and were answered. Last night the digest logged two persons on the fourth-level service stair, at that hour, whom no gate could name." She let it sit. "The hand reads digests. Everything it has done, it has done inside the records. It will know that someone stood there. It will not know who. It will want to."

Elara's wrist was cold under the strap. Rohan had looked up at the stair head on the way out, and come down again, and said nothing.

"Then it's afraid now."

"Perhaps. Sarvajña would say so." The Ancient One unfolded her hands and laid one on the map.

Four houses, inked by someone who had been proud of the lettering. Drona's spires, Chanakya, Ashoka's red-black crest, and Meru above them all. Below them, where every public map Elara had ever read went quiet, the paper was quiet too: no line, no crest, only the blank, and the old woman's finger resting on it.

"What is there?"

"Something I swore never to name, and I will keep the oath even now." The finger didn't move. "Once a year, the four of us are taken to sit before it. None of us knows where we go. That is how it was built. But it can be asked to sit out of season, and I am going to ask."

She rose. It took her a while, and Elara didn't offer a hand, because the look that came with the rising said not to.

The call plate was set into the stone at the chamber's far end. No screen, no crest, four shallow marks worn into the rock round a blank center, the marks so old the edges had gone soft. The Ancient One put two fingers on Kubera's mark and turned it. Nothing lit. Somewhere under the stone, something answered with a tone Elara felt in her teeth before she heard it.

A voice came out of the wall. A man's, dry, close.

"Ancient One."

"The Guard is active. It was seen in Drona."

The pause was long enough to hear the chamber's air move.

"You have proof?"

"A witness, and a pattern."

"Who?"

"Someone I trust."

Another silence. In it, faintly, something set down on a hard surface at the other end: a ring, Elara thought, one of the rings he wore when he spoke for Kubera. Or paper.

"Where?"

"Before the table. Out of season."

This pause was the longest. When the voice came back it had changed, and she couldn't have said how.

"That has not been asked in my lifetime."

"Then you will remember the day I asked it."

"I will come without escort."

The Ancient One turned the mark back to its place, and the tone under the stone stopped, and the chamber was only a room again.

"You've warned him," Elara said, before she could stop herself.

"I have told him I am looking. Nothing more." The old woman didn't turn round. She was looking at the dark grey wall, where last night columns had filled and tonight nothing was lit. "Kubera swore to us that the box was a relic. Tonight we will learn whether anything in this city moves because I said so."

Elara looked at the wall too. A century of listening to silence, and now a test with one line in it. Whatever moved tonight would write itself there, order by order, and someone would read it.

"And me?"

"You will stay where you can see the work, and where no one can see you doing it." The Ancient One came back to the table, slowly, and sat, and put both weights back on the map's corners. "Go and tell the commander what you have lawfully. The register. Not this room."

---

Rohan was in the dead room when she got there, standing, which meant he'd been standing a while.

The lantern was lit. The four panels were where they'd always been, Baju's pin crooked in the corner of the fourth. On the table, where the corridor drawing had been, lay one sheet, face down, and his pencil beside it, squared to the sheet's edge.

She gave him the register line. Page, room, name, the two dates and the empty column. He heard it through without moving. When she finished he looked at the locker by the door, where their two terminals lay sealed in their slots.

"The reader at the stair head," he said. "On the way out. It pulsed at us. Twice. Amber."

"I know. She told me. The tower's digest has two people on that stair it couldn't name."

"Then it knows." He said it to the fourth panel. "It hasn't got our names. It knows two people stood there."

"Yes."

"I should've told you on the stair."

"You should've."

He didn't argue it. His jaw worked once along the scar.

"The Dhammapala," she said. "She'll ask you what Ashoka's commander was doing bare-wristed in Drona's tower at the hand's hour. If she hasn't already."

"She hasn't."

"When she does."

He looked at the sheet on the table, face down, and she understood that he'd been writing it when he heard her on the stair and had turned it over before she came in.

"That's your decision," she said.

He didn't pick up the pencil. He didn't turn the sheet. He put his hand flat on the back of it, the whole hand, pressing it to the wood as he'd pressed a curl of shaving flat three nights ago, and he didn't get up.
