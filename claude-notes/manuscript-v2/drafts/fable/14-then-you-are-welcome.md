# Chapter 14: You Are Welcome

## Then: The night of the card

Dusk reached Meru from below. The terraces caught the last of it: gardens fed by water the sectors had lifted a kilometer against gravity, orchards ripening out of season under tuned light, windows that looked down on the three Trinitys the way the Trinitys looked down on the mines. Attendants moved along the garden paths with trays and lamps, people who rode the lifts up each morning from the three Trinitys and rode them down again at night, permitted to keep Meru beautiful but not to sleep in it. The residents were trustees and heirs, families who had converted money into permanence so long ago that wealth no longer looked like anything at all. It looked like quiet.

They did not call themselves rulers. They called themselves stewards, and the word had lasted a century because no one below had ever been given the standing to laugh at it.

Daren stood at the rail of a terrace walk that belonged to a family he had never met and waited for the Phoenix to clear. The chamber had taken a quarter of an hour. The Master had looked at the final figure once and said nothing, which in Kubera was a seal. Meru had made him and would not house him. A weapon is stored, not housed, and his room was below, in a Drona tower where guests rotated out every cycle and nobody looked twice at a man who stayed. Too many faces up here had watched him come down the Phoenix with a bare wrist.

One of those faces was on the walk now. A Kubera guard in gold armor, visor raised for the evening watch, stood at the head of the garden stair with a tray of someone's unfinished glasses at his elbow, the condensation rings bleeding into one another on the lacquer. He had stood at the black doors the afternoon Daren surrendered the gold band.

Daren walked toward him and did not look away.

The guard's terminal would have spoken by now: clear his path, do not question him, do not record. The guard knew him anyway. Daren saw the knowledge arrive, saw the man weigh it against the instrument on his left wrist, and held his eye through the whole of the weighing. On the fourth step the guard looked down at his terminal like a man answering it, and stepped back against the hedge.

Daren walked past him and waited for the thing that ought to have come with that. Heat in the neck. Pulse in the ears. He went looking for it and found the place where it used to live, swept clean. Fear had lived in what Kubera could take: the rank, the record, the name. All three were gone, and the erasure had taken the fear with them.

What he found instead was hunger. He noticed, as a separate fact, that he hadn't eaten since morning.

The ledger was still open on the pane in his glove. The cross-gana requisition ledger was tedious rather than classified, which made it almost as safe as secrecy, and he had read it the way he'd read failure curves at THRESHOLD for two years: hunting the one line where two measures disagreed.

**DRONA — LIBRARY EXPANSION, RESTRICTED WING — PRIORITY: HIGH (REQUESTING GANA) / PRIORITY: NEGLIGIBLE (RETURN PROJECTION).**

Drona ranked a thing by what it could teach. Kubera ranked it by what it would return. In the gap between those two numbers the request had been waiting for a year, approved by one house and unfunded by the other.

*Weaken with abundance,* the Master had told him under the dark window. *Every gana has learned to defend against being starved. None of them has learned to defend against being fed too well.* Drona would reject a bribe it recognized. A library it could defend as knowledge, as justice to the past, and every defense would make the gift cleaner in its own eyes. Drona called itself the conscience of the city. Daren did not need to destroy that conscience. He needed to make it busy explaining why the gift was righteous.

So he had authorized double the sum, and sent it through the Continuity Reserve, a founder-tier channel old enough that it didn't ask the modern questions. Which director. Which office. Which account gains. At the base of the transfer, where a protocol too old to demand a name required a signature, the field had accepted two letters.

OG.

Obsidian Guard.

Whatever had signed it had not needed to force a single door. Every door it had passed through had been opened from the inside.

Gold could open a wing. It couldn't decide what the wing meant once it entered the record. Kubera moved resources, but Chanakya moved the story around them, and if Daren pushed too openly Trinity would feel the pressure and harden. He needed a hand inside the other house, someone who could turn a pressure into an interpretation and an interpretation into policy.

He had sat one station along from such a hand for three days.

Ravi, at EVENT, deciding what an incident was permitted to mean before Elara had finished reading a figure. Ravi on the last night, watching three boys survive together like a man who'd found the first true variable in a pattern no one had cleared him to study. There were older Chanakya officers. He knew Ravi.

The Phoenix doors opened behind him, and he went down.

---

At ten minutes past eight, in a room that appeared on no registry, Daren dressed for a meeting that would appear on no record.

The obsidian ensemble closed around him piece by piece, each plate seating itself against the next along guide seams too fine to see. The band went on last, over the right cuff. It read the small motor impulses that run ahead of every movement. It had never thought ahead of him. It had never once given him an idea. Every intention was his; the band finished what he had already begun. He no longer put it on the way one picks up a tool. He put it on the way one wakes a hand.

The door slid aside before his hand rose toward the release, and the door's log recorded nothing. According to every registry in Trinity the room behind him stood empty, and the band kept the building's records loyal to that story.

A lift arrived empty and held its doors a breath longer than its schedule allowed. The maglev gate queried the space in front of it, received an answer that satisfied every rule it knew, and opened without recording for whom.

The carriage was half full. A Drona records clerk, two Ashoka responders rotating home, a family returning from some sanctioned evening elsewhere. Every one of them could see the dark shape that entered the carriage. In Trinity nobody trusted their eyes alone, because nobody had needed to for a generation; the terminals watched the world and named what mattered, and people saw what their instruments underlined. Every terminal in the carriage ran its identity query on Daren, and the band answered each one in its own dialect: to the clerk's terminal, an authorization class that made him none of her business; to the responders' duty catalog, a status already filed as someone else's responsibility; to the family's modest terminals, nothing worth underlining at all. The clerk angled her case away from what her terminal considered an empty seat. In his Kubera years the gold had bought him a category: a seat yielded, a glance held a beat too long and then dropped. The obsidian bought him nothing so small. It removed him from the ledger of glances entirely, and the removal was itself the rank.

The family's cat saw him.

It rode in a mesh carrier on the daughter's lap, and somewhere over the open dark it stopped licking its paw and fixed its eyes on the far end of the carriage. No terminal spoke for a cat. Nothing ranked what it was permitted to notice. It had only its eyes and its nose, and both reported a man where every instrument in the carriage reported nothing. Its ears went flat. Its body dropped low in the carrier, and it stared without blinking for the whole length of the crossing. The daughter tapped the mesh and whispered to it. Her mother glanced down the carriage, saw a passenger standing at the dark end, plainly there, plainly visible, consulted her terminal, found no flag, no rank, no reason, and felt her attention slide off him with nothing to hold it. She had spent her whole life letting the instruments tell her which of the things she saw deserved a second look, and this one, the terminal said, deserved none.

The cat kept staring. Daren, who had long since stopped noticing the people who failed to notice him, noticed the cat.

Chanakya's platform assembled out of the night. First the glow, ten thousand recording lamps lit in tier upon tier like reading lamps on an endless desk where the city's arguments were still being graded. Then the towers, green-and-ink glass, each face catching the maglev's running lights and returning them annotated, as if even a reflection in Chanakya were expected to cite its source. The doors opened on air that carried the dry smell of old paper under the lamp-warmth; Chanakya kept its original transcripts in fiber, because paper was the one medium no credential could rewrite. The family disembarked ahead of him, the carrier swinging at the daughter's side, and the cat's eyes held on Daren over her shoulder all the way down the platform until the crowd folded them away.

At twenty minutes before nine he reached the East Policy Conservatory and went up.

The gallery above the hall held one shape the darkness fit around like a sleeve. Below lay the tiered benches, the floor of dark stone, the long projection table where policy students built their arguments in light, and along every wall at intervals the recording lamps, amber and fixed. Nothing said in this room was ever lost.

When Elara had crossed to Ashoka to ask for Rohan, every step of her honesty had been written down in advance: a review code, a lit route, an expected arrival. Tonight the conservatory held no booking, and yet no student had wandered in to rehearse and no steward had come to bank the lamps, because a maintenance window had appeared in the hall's schedule that afternoon, requested by no one. Elara had traveled as a signature on a ledger. Daren convened as an absence on a calendar. The city made room for both, and only one of them would it ever be able to prove.

He looked at the lamps the way a man looks at a lock he has already opened.

Then he waited.

---

Ravi arrived at nine exactly.

Chanakya training was explicit about meetings like this one: never attend an unverified summons, never attend alone, never attend without a record. He'd had one day, and he'd walked the lawful path a dozen times in it. Take the card to an elder. And every time, the path ended in the same place: a case sealed above his grade, investigators following what was written down against a hand that decided what was written down, and his name fixed to the file as its one visible landmark. The analyst whose defeat someone had prevented, reason unknown. His standing was already hostage. It had been hostage since the clock flashed red.

Under the sound reasoning lay a plainer one, and late in the afternoon he'd admitted it. He wanted to know.

So he broke the first rule by coming and the second by telling no one. The third he carried into the hall on his left wrist: his terminal, set to passive record, a small lawful rebellion against an unlawful evening.

The hall was half lit and empty. He came down the tiers slowly, watching the room the way he'd been taught to watch an opponent's hands rather than his eyes. The projection table lay dark. The lamps held their amber at every wall, and their presence steadied him more than he liked. In Chanakya a man could survive almost anything that had a record. It was the gaps that destroyed people.

At the bottom of the tiers he stopped, and the first lamp went out.

It did not flicker and it did not fade. It ended, the way a sentence ends. Then the next one, and the next, each lamp closing in turn, the amber watch going dark around the circumference of the room like a ring of eyes shutting one by one.

His terminal died with the last of them.

He had not known silence had a texture. The silence of a recorded room was thin, a pause between entries. This one was thick. It took his breathing and gave nothing back. Some part of him had always arranged itself for the listening, spine and voice and choice of words, and now the arrangement had nothing to lean on. Whatever had invited him here could reach into Chanakya's own memory and turn it off.

The projection table lit.

On its dark glass, drawn in lines of pale light, stood a chess position. He didn't need to study it. He had lived inside it since yesterday morning, awake and asleep: the arena match, frozen at the move where the living board had died, one move from his own defeat.

Across the top of the table, text assembled itself.

YOUR MOVE.

Ravi looked up at the gallery, the doorways, the dark tiers. Nothing offered itself.

"I had lost," he said aloud. His voice fell into the unrecorded air and nothing kept it. "There is no move."

The voice that answered came from the room itself, through the speakers that announced examination results: something assembled rather than spoken, toneless and placeless, a public announcement for an audience of one.

"Sit. Prove it."

What, in this room, did he still control? Only his own play, and the board was the one language in which an opponent couldn't lie. A voice could be scrubbed of its owner. Moves could not.

He sat, the way an analyst opens a file. The chair's left armrest had been worn pale by a century of right-handed students leaning away from their notes.

He played the losing side. The table answered each move with the Arthashastri's, and the position tightened around him as it had in the arena. He tried the defense he'd built on the walk back from the arena. It failed in four moves. The table reset the position. He tried the sharper line he'd found last night, lying awake with the king in his fist. It failed in six. The table reset the position again, patient as gravity.

"You see it now," the room said. "Say it."

"There is no square on this board that saves me. The position was lost."

"The position was lost. And yet you did not lose. Explain the difference."

"The game was never decided on the board," he said. "It was decided by whoever could reach the board from outside it."

Only after the words were out did he recognize the method, and his skin went cold with it. This was how Chanakya's own instructors taught. They built the path and made you walk it, because a lesson a man speaks in his own voice is the one he keeps. He had just recited the first lesson perfectly, to a teacher he couldn't see.

The position dissolved. The table lay dark and waiting.

"Your house reads incidents," the room said. "Something happens in the city, and Chanakya explains it afterward, in files, wearing new names. I am offering you one look at the other side of the table. The side where incidents are written."

"Written by whom?"

"By whoever can reach the board from outside it. You said it yourself."

Ravi kept his hands flat on the table's edge, because Chanakya had also trained him to know what his hands did when he was afraid.

"You interrupted the Grandmaster's checkmate," he said. "You walked into my quarters while I slept. You have switched off the lamps in the one room in this city built never to go dark. I understand the demonstration. What I do not understand is the selection."

"Chanakya has a hundred interpreters more senior than you, and every one of them earned it by learning what not to notice. You have not learned it yet. That is the asset."

"And the match?" He heard his voice change and couldn't stop it. "The lottery falls once a year. This year it fell to me."

The room was silent for as long as it takes a patient opponent to let a man finish seeing a thing on his own.

"The lottery was told to fall to you."

He had believed, until that sentence, that he was managing the evening. He'd kept his reasoning in front of him like a lamp of his own, and the sentence put it out. He had carried the match as proof that merit could still surface in Chanakya on its own. He had been selected the way a file is selected. Pulled, read, assessed.

"Why." It didn't come out as a question.

"Because a man reveals himself against a Grandmaster. You revealed patience, and pride, and one blindness. Your play was honest. The blindness is this: you fight only inside the frame you are handed. In the arena you examined every square of that board and never once asked whether anything outside its edge could reach in. Tonight you know that someone stands outside the board. The only question left is whether you would rather interpret the next verdict, or help write it."

"Writing pays," the room said then, and the table lit again.

It showed a ledger view Ravi knew intimately and had never once seen obeyed: his own standing requests. The records-retrieval priority he had petitioned for three times. The funding line for cross-house incident comparison that his section chief called ambitious, which in Chanakya meant denied. In the real ledger those lines read deferred, under review, denied. Here, beside each one, stood a single word.

GRANTED. GRANTED. GRANTED.

"Ask for anything that moves through a ledger, and it will move. No sponsor named. No debt recorded. Your work unchained from the queue it has been standing in since you were seventeen, since the day you entered the analyst track and the house taught you what waiting was."

Ravi looked at the column. Then he made himself look away from it, because he could feel what the looking was doing to him.

"A grant with no name on it is not a gift," he said. "It is a leash with the collar delivered first. Chanakya writes that lesson into children. I did not ask to be saved in the arena, and I am not for sale through a ledger."

"Good," the room said, and the approval in its tonelessness was worse than anger would have been. "A man who can be bought at the first asking is a poor investment. Now let me show you the other table."

The ledger vanished. In its place the display assembled a message-routing reconstruction, the kind Chanakya's auditors built. At the top sat the message itself, the narrow line of text that had crossed the Arthashastri's private display one move before checkmate. Ravi still couldn't read it. He could read the routing beneath it, relay by relay, down toward its origin.

The origin field was masked.

"Watch."

The mask dissolved, and characters filled the field entry by entry, the way a record fills when a clerk keys it in. A console identifier. A residential node. A credential string.

His own.

The timestamp sat forty-one seconds before the Grandmaster's checkmate would have fallen.

"That is not true," Ravi said. His voice came out lower than he meant it to.

"No. It is not true. It is only recorded. You of all people know the difference, and you of all people know which one your house believes. Records do not say what happened, Ravi. They say what they are told."

He examined the trail the way he examined everything, hunting the seam: the timestamp a tenth out of sequence, the relay that didn't exist. He found nothing. If this surfaced tomorrow, he would believe it himself. And he saw the whole proceeding in one breath, because proceedings were his trade. The panel. The analyst who could not bear to lose before his whole house, reaching into the arena's systems to manufacture his own rescue. They would hang him in the Hall of Arguments under a short notation and teach him to students as a shape of corruption.

"You would be believed," he said. There was no plea in it, only a position evaluated honestly: no counter, no exchange, no argument left.

"I show you that instrument once, so that I never have to reach for it. Fear makes analysts stupid, and I am not recruiting your fear. Let me finish with the thing I came to give you."

The false routing dissolved, origin field first, and the table showed a document Ravi had read perhaps thirty times: Chanakya's standing petition for the Analysts' Annex. A second archive floor. Retrieval that did not queue. The petition was eleven years old, and Kubera's allocation council had declined it every cycle with the same two words, non-essential, until every analyst in the house had grown up inside the refusal the way miners grow up inside dust.

"Within one quarter, Kubera's allocation system will approve this petition in full. The decision will trace back to an analysis memorandum on the cost of Chanakya's retrieval delays. The memorandum will carry your name. You will not write it. It will be found, and it will be excellent, and it will be yours. You have seen what I can make a record say against you. This is what I can make a record say for you."

Ravi sat very still.

This was neither bribe nor threat, and it went through him without resistance, because it arrived dressed as the thing he had wanted since he was seventeen. Standing. The elders' eyes finding him. The voice had tried his conscience from two directions and then walked around it, through the door marked dignity.

"And in return."

"Nothing. Tonight. A piece that moves on the first invitation is a pawn, and the city manufactures its own. When the next transfer crosses your desk wearing a name no registry can find, you will remember this room, and you will choose where you sit."

"Who are you?"

"A signature."

The lamps came back on.

They returned the way they had gone, one by one along the walls, the amber watch reopening its eyes. Ravi's terminal woke against his arm and began recording an empty hall. Whatever he said next would be kept.

He stood. He looked down at the dark table, then reached into his pocket and set the black king on the center of the glass, the piece he'd carried asleep and awake since it entered his room last night. He stood it upright, facing no opponent. Not yes, not no. Present.

Then he climbed the tiers and left without looking back at the lamps.

---

In the gallery above, Daren watched him go.

He hadn't moved in more than an hour, and the dark around his position had bent so faithfully that twice a cleaning drone had mapped the gallery as empty, its brush ticking along the rail a hand's width from his boot. Below, the black king stood in the center of the lit table, casting its small shadow across the glass.

He hadn't said yes. Daren had expected that, planned for it, wanted it. *Gold buys the poor,* the Master of the Reserve had taught him once, in an instruction no record held. *Fear rents the weak. But a proud man is purchased with his own future, and he will thank you at the signing.* The Master collected oaths. Daren had watched where oaths led: to registries, to ceremonies, to names that could be stripped in a single dawn. Doubt was a lens, and a lens could not be taken off.

He turned the arithmetic over once more, and found it ran further than the hall below.

The Master's power was ranked, chartered, recorded. Supreme inside Kubera, and bounded on every side: by three other houses, by his own succession, by every audit his office had to survive. The power he had handed Daren was bounded by none of those things, because officially it did not exist. A recorded power had a ceiling. An unrecorded one had only appetite. And no one can revoke what he cannot name. To cancel the two letters, the Master would first have to confess them, in a chamber full of ledgers, to a house that had never once heard them spoken. Daren thought of the old man's hands resting on the glass table, hands that had never lifted anything heavier than a seal, and felt the confidence settle into him the way the plates had settled that evening, seam into seam, without a sound.

He left the king where it stood. Some archivist would file a query, and the query would die the way queries did, and Ravi would pass this hall a hundred times in the years ahead knowing exactly what stood at the center of it, and whom it was facing.

The house negotiated his departure as it had negotiated his arrival, and he attended to none of it. Trinity-2 fell away behind the glass, its ten thousand reading lamps grading the city's arguments into the night. He stood at the dark end of the last carriage and did not look at the passengers who did not look at him. Somewhere over the open dark the four notes came up in his throat, the same four, low enough to stay inside the collar of the hood, and he let them finish.

The city arranged itself around a mass it could not see, and the mass rode home to a room in a Drona monitor tower where, according to every record in Trinity, no one was waiting and no one arrived.
