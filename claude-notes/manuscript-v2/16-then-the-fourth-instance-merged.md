# Chapter 16: The Fourth Instance

*He led them into a corridor with four doors, each labeled: CONTROL. PLAN. ADAPT. SACRIFICE. The guard stopped at the entrance and turned to face them. "Four rooms. Four tests. You pass all four, you live. You fail —" He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to.*

— Book 1, Chapter 22: The Engine and Eyes

## Then: The dead room

Elara worked it out on the stairs, three flights down into Ashoka's old stone, before Rohan could hand it to her.

"Because Drona's the crime scene," she said. "The hand moved through my console. Anything I pin to a wall in Drona hangs inside the system we think is being read. And my operator status still carries its flag." She took the next step harder than she meant to. "At home I'm a watched woman asking questions I can't afford to have watched."

"And a borrowed room between the platforms would be worse." Rohan didn't slow. "An empty room protects you from people. Nothing about it protects you from systems. Two officials meeting off their routes is exactly what a watching system is built to flag." "The safest place to stand is inside your own lawful shadow. You're a Drona representative conducting a procedural review with Ashoka, under the Ancient One's code. Every word of that is true, filed and boring."

"And inside the shadow?"

"This room."

Ashoka called it a dead room, and the words were painted on the door in plain white, because the room was lawful and had nothing to hide about having nothing. It was older than the present Grandmaster, kept for witness protection and planning: no recording lamps, no wall sensors, no network thread entering it anywhere. What light it had came down a shaft in the ceiling, daylight bent through three polished mirrors from somewhere far above. It arrived pale and a little late. When a cloud crossed the platforms, the room dimmed a few seconds after the sky did.

Beside the door stood a grey steel locker with a slot for each wrist. Elara unbuckled her terminal and laid it in, and the drawer closed on it with a soft pneumatic sigh. The skin where it had sat was paler than the rest of her arm, and cold. The city couldn't hear her.

Paper only. Pads on the table, pencils, a dented tin for the shavings. At the end of each session the sheets went into a locker in the inner wall, a long steel face with one key-slot for every person at the table, the keys cut fresh each time. It opened only when every key turned together. No one could take a page out alone. The wall, Elara thought, would be the body's memory, and the locker would open only for all of them at once. On no fixed days, two officers who had never been introduced swept the room by hand.

*Nothing here for a hidden hand to borrow,* Elara thought.

Rohan had brought four people, and no true names.

Grey fatigues, no insignia, no rank on any collar. A young woman sat at the far end of the table with her back to the room and her face to the wall, copying it. The wall held three handwritten panels, and she was copying them onto a pad in pencil, small and level, never looking down at her hand. In the top corner of the page she had written the date and the hour. Older sheets lay stacked at her elbow, each dated the same way.

"Nayan," Rohan said. "The eye."

"Morning." The pencil didn't stop. "I copy what I read. Dated. When a record changes overnight, I can prove it was ever different. I've got dated copies of all four houses' public books. Six years back. Nobody's ever asked me to lay them side by side." She turned the page.

Rohan was already looking at the door.

A lean, weathered man stood against it with his palm flat on the seam where door met frame, moving his hand down a finger's width at a time, the way a physician reads a ribcage. He had a climber's hands, thick at the knuckle, two fingertips calloused flat and shiny. Something in his trouser pocket clicked when he shifted his weight, and inside his coat, where it fell open, a pencil stub rode against a notebook gone soft at the corners.

"Baju. The arm."

"Seal's good." Baju (BAH-joo) took his hand off the door and wiped it on his trousers, though there was nothing on it. "Field work. Doors, lifts, platforms. I walk what the logs say was walked, and I count." He patted the pocket. It clicked again. "Machines can be told what to remember. Floors cannot be told what to remember."

"And Kaan," Rohan said. "The ear."

He nodded at the corner beside the locker, and there was a man in it.

Elara had set her pad down within an arm's length of him, and her eyes had gone over that corner and registered a wall. Medium height, medium build, a face like an unmarked door.

"He takes up a cover posting tomorrow," Rohan said. "Even I'll know only which building. He asks no questions. He receives."

Kaan (KAHN) said nothing, and went on saying it.

The last of them had his chair tipped back on two legs and his boots crossed at the ankle. He was broad and open-faced, with a market trader's ease, and he sat in the dead room as if it were his stall on a slow afternoon.

"And before you ask," he said, before Rohan could, "I don't know him. Nobody does. There's a story going round the canteens this week about an ear who stood forty minutes in a tea queue and came out knowing which cook was watering the dal. I may have started it." He tipped his chin at Kaan. "Jeebh. The tongue. I'm the one who talks."

Baju let out one breath through his nose, which Elara would come to understand was his laugh. Something in the air let out a notch.

"I keep us invisible," Jeebh (JEEBH) said, "by keeping the city bored. It's a gift. My mother said so, at length."

"They don't know each other's true names," Rohan said. "Or their units. To each other they're the eye, the arm, the ear and the tongue. If one of them is caught, or turned, the other three stay strangers to whoever's holding him. No one can surrender what they were never given."

He laid a single handwritten sheet on the table, face down, and put his hand flat on it.

Then he turned to Elara, and his voice went to the register he used on the mat for instructions.

"One more rule, and it's yours. After today you will not see these four again. You will not meet them, signal them or task them. Everything goes through me. A body has one will. If it had two, it could be pulled apart at the joint."

The objection came up in her like heat. She had always trusted her own eyes over anyone's summary. Her thumb pressed into the soft edge of the pad until it dented. Then the argument finished itself, because it was the same arithmetic that kept everyone at the table alive.

"So if the hand ever finds me," she said, "I can give it nothing but you."

"And if it finds one of them, they can give it nothing but me." Rohan nodded once. "I am the only door in this body. And a door is the one part of the house that is built to take the breaking."

Baju had sat down beside her at some point. He unwrapped a ration bar, the dense grey Ashoka kind that tasted faintly of dates and machine oil, and snapped it across his knee. He looked at the two halves, slid the bigger one along the table until it touched her hand, and ate the other in two bites, chewing at the wall.

The bar was still warm from his pocket. She hadn't eaten since the night watch. She hadn't known it showed.

The wall held their work: three instances, each on its own panel, in Ashoka's plain style.

The transfer that had doubled in flight, which no one had requested and no one had granted. The signature of two letters the system obeyed without being able to name. The calibration pushed to its ceiling through Elara's own console during the Initiation, by a hand no inquiry had found.

"Three instances," Rohan said. "Each one explainable alone. An error. An old authorization. A malfunction. I was charged to test what you see, and to act when the evidence requires it. So test it. All of you. This is not your case with my help. It is ours. What makes these one thing and not three?"

"Direction," Elara said. She had waited days to say it to people who would check her instead of comfort her. "An error has no direction. These all lean the same way. Every one moves capability toward a point we can't see. Money, access, reach. And every one is dressed as something ordinary. The acts are designed to be found one at a time."

Nayan (NUH-yun) spoke without looking up from her copy. "Then the pattern is the evidence. Only shows side by side. Whoever built them is counting on the houses never putting their records in one room."

"The houses have never put their records in one room," Rohan said.

"No," Elara said. "That is what this room is for."

She had brought her own instrument to the work. The Sākṣī (SAHK-shee) mirrors were witness copies, duplicates of the city's records written into a separate archive by hands that had no part in making the originals. A record could be altered. Its mirror, held somewhere else by someone else, remembered what it used to say. Anandi had taught her to read them.

Since the Initiation she had been watching one narrow band: cross-house allocations, the entries where one gana's money paid for another gana's wants. This hand gave before it took. So she watched for gifts arriving where no gift had ever gone. Generosity had become her tripwire.

Her terminal sat in the locker by the door, and it made no difference. Nayan had been watching the same water.

Two knocks came at the door, close together.

Nayan put down her pencil, crossed to the slot beside the frame and drew out a sealed envelope. She broke the wax with her thumbnail and read. Elara watched the restlessness in her eyes go still, and then the shaft overhead dimmed, a cloud passing somewhere high above, and the room went grey a few seconds after it should have.

"Morning allocation bulletin," Nayan said. "My runner copies it at the public terminal. A terminal you ask leaves your name on the request. A boy with a pencil leaves nothing." She laid the sheet flat. "Kubera's allocation council has approved the Analysts' Annex. In full."

Jeebh's chair came down onto four legs.

"Chanakya's archive wing," Nayan said, for the room. "When Kubera's council has to grant a thing it doesn't want to, it grants a sliver. This is everything, to the last vault."

Elara came around the table. "I know this petition. It lives in my band. Chanakya has petitioned for that expansion every cycle for eleven years. Kubera has refused it every cycle for eleven years. It was the stillest water I had."

"The council acted on a memorandum," Nayan said. "Cost of Chanakya's retrieval delays. Signed by an analyst named Ravi." She checked the sheet. "Bulletin calls it exemplary. Every figure sourced. Every objection anticipated. Eleven years of refusal answered in nine pages."

Elara's pencil stopped against the pad.

She had caught that analyst's wrist once, at the Initiation consoles, when his hand went toward the High Command button. Across the table Rohan's eyes came up and found hers, held, and went back to the bulletin. Neither of them said that the name had sat at the EVENT console close enough to touch. Kaan would have to watch the man without a story already hung on him.

"Houses change their minds," Baju said. It didn't sound like disagreement. It sounded like a man tapping the ice with his heel before the others walked on it.

"Or a talented analyst wrote a talented memo," Rohan said, testing from the other side.

"Maybe." Elara tapped the bulletin. "I've read years of petitions in that band. Here's what's wrong with this one. It is perfect. A petition refused eleven years running carries scars. Each refiling trims an ask, concedes a point, answers last cycle's objection in the margin. Every defeat stays in the text, like rings in a tree." She looked at the wall. "This memorandum has no rings. Drona was given more money than it asked for. Now Kubera gives Chanakya the thing it stopped hoping for aloud. Generosity is the fingerprint. Whoever he is, he doesn't attack houses. He feeds them."

"He," Nayan said.

She looked up from the panels, and she wasn't agreeing.

"We've got a signature, a style and a taste for giving. No face. No voice. No body. Could be a man. Could be a woman. Could be more than one person sharing two letters."

Elara opened her mouth and closed it. Heat climbed the back of her neck.

"Nayan is right," Rohan said. "The word you use shapes the thing you look for. Say he, and every woman in the city walks past your watch unexamined. From today, inside this room, it is the hand. It."

Elara nodded once. Her pencil went back to the pad and wrote *it*.

"If you are right," Rohan said, "then the pattern has a fourth instance. And look at the date. The first three happened weeks and months ago. This one happened this morning, while we were pinning the other three to a wall." His jaw worked once along the old scar. "We came here to read history. This is not history. It is traffic. It is still moving."

"And there's the name." Elara touched the signature line with one fingertip. "Either the analyst is the hand, and the hand doesn't sign its real name. Or he's standing where it wanted him to stand, and doesn't know whose weight he's carrying." Her eyes went, without her permission, to the third panel and her own console's name on it. "Someone should find out which. Before someone else decides for him."

Rohan looked at the wall, then at the four of them, and divided the ground.

"Nayan. The signature and the money. Every system that has ever obeyed those two letters. Every flow around the Annex and the doubled transfer. Find me the oldest instruction in the chain, and wherever the record disagrees with your paper, mark it."

"Gaps are footprints." Nayan was already writing.

"Baju. Movement. The days around each instance. Doors, lifts, platforms, everything that cannot be told what to remember. And the analysts' wing in Chanakya. If someone reached him, a path bent toward him, and the floor will have felt it."

"And if nothing bent," Baju said, "that's worse news, and I'll have walked a long way to bring it."

"Kaan. Chanakya. Near the archive, near the Annex, near the analyst. You do not approach him. You learn him. We do not yet know what you are watching: an instrument that was picked up, or the hand signing itself as one. Do not decide in advance."

Kaan had already moved a half-step toward the door. Elara realized she still didn't know what his voice sounded like, and might never.

"Jeebh. Cover. From tomorrow, Drona's procedural review of Ashoka grows three sub-audits so dull no one reads past the titles. And a rumor in the right canteens: the Annex was eleven years of quiet lobbying finally landing."

"Three sub-audits." Jeebh laced his fingers behind his head. "I'll make them so dull I fall asleep filing them. The rumor I'll enjoy."

"The hand's whole art is dressing the extraordinary as the ordinary," Rohan said. "A theft as a gift. An order as a habit. From now on, we wear the same coat."

Rohan turned the face-down sheet over, wrote one line on it in his own hand, and turned it back. "The eye cannot seize. The arm cannot hear. The ear cannot speak. Together, you're a net. I brief the Grandmaster tonight." Then he nodded at the bulletin.

"Same direction as the other three. Same disguise. Same perfection." He looked at the bare stretch of wall beside the third panel. "Fourth instance. Fourth panel."

Baju was already reaching for it, a brass pin held between his lips, the counter in his pocket clicking once as he stood.

---

At the hour when Ashoka's training halls went dark and its corridors learned to be quiet, Rohan knelt opposite the Dhammapala in a room that held two cushions, one oil lamp and nothing that recorded. The lamp burned with a true flame. It drew no power, reported to no grid, and left only a little smoke that smelled faintly of cloves. When either of them breathed out, the flame leaned toward the door.

He reported the way he fought, with nothing wasted. The dead room. The wall and its four panels. The body, by its working names only: the eye, the arm, the ear, the tongue, and the rule that made him its single door. Last, the bulletin. Same direction, same disguise, same perfection. Generosity as the fingerprint.

A body that answered to no Grandmaster would make him the thing he was hunting, so he told her everything but its faces. Even here, the door held.

The Dhammapala listened with her eyes closed, which in her meant the reverse of inattention. One hand rested on her knee with the fingers curled, the way they had closed on the bamboo.

"You have stopped saying he," she said, when he had finished.

"The eye corrected us. We have a signature and a style. We do not have a body. Inside that room it is the hand, and it is an it, until the evidence says otherwise."

"Good. The mind hunts what the mouth names." She opened her eyes. "And you are the only door. You have built a body whose whole safety is one man's silence. What happens to the hunt when the door is broken down?"

"The body goes still and stays safe. The wall keeps what we knew. Ashoka appoints a better door."

"That is the answer of a man who has made peace with being spent." She said it without praise and without objection, the way she might name a stance. "I will not unmake it. But hear one thing from someone older than your certainty. A door that is willing to break serves the house. A door that is eager to break serves only its own story. Watch which one you are becoming. The first keeps people alive. The second is merely brave."

Rohan's hands lay on his thighs. The split knuckle on the right one had opened again under its glue, and he watched a bead of blood rise there and didn't wipe it. He didn't pretend the strike had missed.

"There is one more thing," she said. "You hunt a hand that hides in lawful shadows. You may find ground that has already been walked, questions answered before you ask them. I told you there are safeguards in this city older than Ashoka. They do not report to me. If your hunt ever crosses a path that feels like your own thinking arriving ahead of you, do not chase it. Mark the place, and bring it to me, and I will tell you what I can, which will not be everything."

"Whose path would it be?"

"If the balance is kind," the Dhammapala said, rising, "you will never need to know."

There was a who, then, and she knew some of it. Rohan found he could live without knowing. He hoped he would be allowed to.

She bent and put the lamp out the old way, two fingers to the wick, no hurry. The smoke went up straight in the dark, with no breath left in the room to lean it anywhere.
