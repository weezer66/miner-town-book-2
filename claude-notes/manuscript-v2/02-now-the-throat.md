# Chapter 2: The Throat

*"The question is not what I did to you. The question is: what will you do to regain the freedom that was once your birthright?"*

— Book 1, Chapter 25: Don't Do It

## Now: The Room of Four Doors

Camilla came back to the sound of her brother killing someone.

It wasn't loud. That was how she knew it was bad. Gage was loud when he was losing: a shout, a fist into a wall, a door kicked hard enough to bounce. When he meant something, he went quiet, and the only sound in the room now was a man's breath being made smaller.

She lay on her back on cold metal. The floor hummed under her shoulder blades, a low, steady note she could have tuned a radio to. Somebody had sealed her again. The fresh coat sat cold and tight over the place where her left arm ended, and the skin around it pulled when she breathed. Ben's hands had been there. She could still feel where his thumbs had pressed the edges flat, the way she'd once shown him how to seat a gasket.

Her left hand was making a fist.

Her left hand was lying on the floor of a room she would never see again, on the far side of a volcano that had never existed. She knew that. The hand didn't. It clenched anyway, every finger, nails into a palm that wasn't there.

"…ready for the journey ahead," a voice said, close and calm, a man reading out a timetable.

She opened her eyes.

White light, too clean. The crystal turning above them, three quarters green, one quarter pulsing a cold, patient blue. Beneath it, four shapes she knew better than her own reflection, and one she didn't.

Gage stood in the middle of the room with his arm straight out and a man at the end of it. His hand was around Mr. Flitzberg's throat. Camilla checked the jaw first, out of habit, the hinge where the borrowed faces had always flickered: Pappy's, then Gerald's. No seam. Smooth pale skin, full cheeks going blotchy above Gage's knuckles. He was wearing his own face tonight. She couldn't decide if that was better.

He wasn't fighting. His hands hung at his sides, open. A man who could have reached for something and hadn't. There was a smudge of pencil on his cuff.

Ben was on his knees beside her, the empty spray can still in his fist, staring up at Gage with his mouth open.

And Otto.

Otto stood exactly where he'd stood when the room began, shoulders square, feet planted. His chest rose. His chest fell. His eyes were open and looking at nothing, the way the lens of a dead drone looks at nothing. A thin line of dried blood ran from his nose to his lip, and nobody had wiped it.

Camilla counted. It was what she did with a room that was going wrong: count the pieces, find the one that moved. Four people who'd walked in. One stranger. One crystal, one quarter blue. Three people breathing on purpose, and one breathing because his body hadn't been told to stop.

The piece that moved was Gage's arm. It was shaking.

She had watched that arm carry Otto out of the Initiation across his shoulders while the other hand dragged Ben by the collar, and neither arm had trembled once. This was the other kind of shaking: every muscle pulling two ways at once, the part of him that wanted to close the hand all the way, and the part that was listening for her.

"Gage," she said.

It came out as air. Her throat was raw. She remembered now: the strip of suit between her teeth, the taste of melted fabric, the knot pulled tight one-handed. She swallowed and tried again.

"Gage. Stop."

He didn't turn his head. But she saw it land. His shoulders came down a fraction, the way they had at the horse bridge when she'd counted *one, two* and he'd held his button instead of mashing it. The Engine heard the Eyes. That was the deal they'd made in the corridor outside CONTROL, and he'd kept it every room since.

Flitzberg spoke with what throat he had left. "You are angry at the right person," he said. "Do not let that comfort you. The right person is also the only one who will answer your questions, and a dead man answers nothing."

Nothing in the room moved. Then Gage's hand opened, one finger at a time, as if each had to be persuaded separately. He stepped back. He did not step far.

Flitzberg touched his own throat once and let the hand drop. He didn't cough. Camilla filed that away: a man who had decided ahead of time not to cough.

"Then answer," Gage said. His voice was gravel. "Why? What did we ever do to you?"

"Nothing," Flitzberg said. "You were not punished. You were chosen. I understand that from where you stand the two are difficult to tell apart. That is the fault of the city that raised you, and it is a fair complaint, and I will hear all of it." His eyes came down to the floor, to her. "But first your sister needs more than a spray can."

"The spray can did fine," Camilla said.

Ben made a sound that started as a laugh and turned into something else halfway.

Flitzberg raised one hand, slowly, where they could all see it, and beckoned toward the wall. A section of it slid aside that she hadn't known was a door, and she was annoyed at herself for that, even now.

Three medics came through: two with a wheeled stretcher, a third carrying cases. Ben moved before any of them reached her. He put himself between the stretcher and Camilla on his knees, both arms out, the empty can in one hand like a weapon he didn't know how to use.

The oldest of the medics, a woman with grey hair cut short and one shoelace double-knotted where the other wasn't, stopped. She didn't argue. She set her case on the floor between them, unlatched it, and turned it around so Ben could see inside.

Camilla could see too, from where she lay. Foam, cut into exact shapes, each instrument sitting in a hollow made for it alone. Things that gleamed. She had spent her whole life in a world where everything she owned was scratched, and nothing in that case had a scratch on it.

"Clean sealant," the medic said, touching each one. "Blood volume. Pain control that will not fog her. We can do it here, on the floor, if she would rather not be moved. And when we do move her, you may walk beside the stretcher with your hand on her shoulder the whole way. We have done this before, and we have never once needed a family member to let go."

Ben looked at Gage. Gage looked at the case, and something in his face gave. His pride would take a week. The exhaustion under it had been waiting all night for permission. He nodded once.

"Here," Camilla said. "On the floor."

The medic knelt. "Here, then."

"That one." Camilla tipped her chin at a slim grey cylinder in the case's second row. "What does it do?"

"It reads how much blood you've lost."

"How?"

The corner of the medic's mouth moved. "Light, through the skin. I'll show you when you can hold it."

"I'll hold it now."

"You'll hold still now," the medic said, and pressed the cylinder to the inside of Camilla's good wrist. It was warm. It hummed in a different key from the floor.

The pain control came next, cool along her arm, and the medic was right: it didn't fog her. The room stayed sharp. Only the fist that wasn't there loosened, finger by finger, the way Gage's had.

"Otto first," Camilla said.

The medic glanced up at Flitzberg. Flitzberg nodded.

A second medic crossed to Otto and shone a light into one eye, then the other. Otto didn't blink. The medic held an instrument near his temple, read it, and turned toward Flitzberg with a face that was its own report.

"What's wrong with him?" Ben's voice cracked on the last word. "He's right there. He's breathing. Why won't he come back?"

Flitzberg looked past Ben to Otto, to the boy who had held ten stones over a lake of fire with nothing but his will, and he gave the answer the way, Camilla would learn, he gave every answer that mattered.

"I do not know."

"You did this —"

"Yes. And I do not know." He let it stand. "Our instruments say nothing is wrong with him. Heart, breath, the workings of his body, all of it steady, all of it well. By every measure we have, he is unharmed. The instruments are wrong. Some part of your friend spent itself in that room, or went somewhere to pay for what the four of you needed, and it has not come back. We will keep his body strong and his door open. Past that, I will not pretend to a knowledge I do not have. You have been lied to with confidence your whole lives. I will not add to it."

"That's not good enough," Gage said.

"No," Flitzberg agreed. "It is not. It is only true."

Camilla watched her brother's face take that and not know what to do with it. Trinity lied to Miner Town every day, cheerfully, through speakers on poles. Nobody up there had ever said *I do not know.* It was the most frightening thing anyone had said to her all night, and she believed every word.

"You have a question standing in line behind that one," Flitzberg said to Gage. "Ask it."

"The guard at the four doors." Gage's voice had gone flat. "He said, you pass all four, you live. You fail — and he didn't finish it. Finish it."

"Some who fail die," Flitzberg said. "Not by our hand. Understand that exactly. No one is executed here. But the rooms you walked are not theater. The drone that took your sister's arm was not pretending, and the heat that softened her sealant was real heat. A test that cannot hurt you cannot measure you. When a team breaks in those rooms, when it panics, or freezes, or turns on itself, the rooms do not become gentle because the people in them have stopped being able. That is how the deaths happen. Others fail more quietly, and serve here in smaller ways, and are not punished for failing. A few are returned to their old lives with a story that explains their absence, and they are watched, kindly but forever, because of what they have seen." He paused. "That is the finished sentence. You are owed the whole of it, and now you have it."

Real heat. Camilla had known that on the mountain, when the sealant went soft and warm and began to slide, and she'd pressed her hand over it and kept climbing because there was nothing anyone could do about it on a mountainside. She had known it was real the whole way up. It was strange how much it helped, hearing a grown man say so.

"And Camilla's arm?" Ben was crying and not hiding it. "And Otto? Was that the price of your test?"

"Yes."

The word landed alone, with nothing dressed around it.

"I could tell you the mine would have taken more of you, and taken it without names, one shift at a time," Flitzberg said. "All of that is true, and tonight it would sound like an excuse, so I will not lean on it. The rooms we built set prices, and when a trial goes wrong, someone pays them. Your sister's arm was such a price. It fell on her, it could have fallen on any of you, and she paid it the way I have seen few grown soldiers pay anything: on her feet, telling the three of you to get back to work while she bled." He looked down at her, and she looked back, and neither of them blinked. "Do not remember her as someone this place happened to. Remember her as someone who met it standing."

"I'm lying down," Camilla said.

"Tonight," Flitzberg said, "you have earned it."

Ben wiped his face with the back of his wrist and left a streak of sealant across his cheek, blue as the crystal's last quarter.

"And your friend is the other half of the same lesson," Flitzberg went on. "His price no one set on him. He saw it coming, counted it, and reached for it himself. We watched from the other side of the glass: a boy holding a ring of stones open by will alone, blood running from his nose, long past what any measure said he had left. He spent the last of it making certain it bought all three of you out. Both of those costs belong to this office, and this office does not pretend otherwise. What I can tell you is what the costs purchased. You were not being tortured. You were being read. And what we read in the four of you, we have been looking for, here, for a very long time."

*Office.* Camilla turned the word over. Prisons had guards and labs had cages. Offices had forms, and forms had authors.

"Read for what?" Gage said. "You keep talking around it. You said freedom. Birthright. What does that mean?"

Flitzberg went quiet. She could see him measuring out an amount, the way Pappy used to measure salt into a pot, by how much the pot could take rather than how much was in the jar.

"There was a time," he said, "when the people of this region moved where they chose, sold what they dug, and had a say in who set their quotas. I am not telling you a legend. The records of it still exist — deeds, charters, pay-books, rolls of names — old enough that the city above has stopped guarding them, because it assumes no one who cares is left who can read them."

Camilla could read them. She had learned her letters from Pappy's Master Scripts under the floorboards. *If you can read the manual, you own the machine.* She didn't say it. Her mouth was very dry.

"What changed did not change in a day," Flitzberg said. "It came in stages, and every stage was signed: an emergency measure here, a safety regulation there, a lottery introduced as a mercy. Nobody stole your freedom in the night. It was transferred, lawfully, gradually, in documents, and a transfer always has paperwork. That is the birthright I named. Not a dream. A property, with a record of exactly how it left your family's hands."

Ben had gone very still beside her, the stillness he got when a pattern was finishing itself in his head and he didn't like its shape.

"Paper," he said at last, and there was no hope in the word. "You can show a man the deed to his own house while he stands in the rain outside it. The people who signed those papers own the offices where papers matter. They own the clerks who read them and the guards at the doors. Knowing how it was taken tells us everything and gets us nothing. There's no counter in Miner Town where you can stand in line and ask for your freedom back."

"Correct," Flitzberg said. "You cannot appeal to them; they own the places where appeals are heard. And you cannot rise against them, for a harder reason. Think of what happens in Miner Town after any trouble. Rations shorten. Quotas climb. Guards double at the gates, and examples are made in the shack-rows, of people who had nothing to do with the trouble at all. The city above does not fight its challengers. It bills their neighbors."

Camilla thought of her mother at the gates on the fourth day of the Initiation, holding a dead man's wrench. She thought of Gretchen, Ben's sister, who would be at the gates now, and every day after, until somebody told her to stop.

"Then what's left?" Gage said. "If we can't ask and we can't fight, what is this place? What are you?"

"The answer to that is a long one," Flitzberg said, "and it is owed to you whole: what this place is, how it came to be, and what it wants with the four of you. You will have all of it. Not tonight. I am not hiding it from you. I am saving it for when you can weigh it."

Gage's jaw set, and Camilla saw the refusal rising through him like water through a cracked floor —

Then Ben swayed on his knees and caught himself on her shoulder, and the refusal drained out of Gage all at once. He looked at the three of them, the one standing and the two on the floor, and she watched him run the count she had run. He came up with the same number. They had nothing left.

"Now," the medic said quietly, and Camilla let them lift her.

The stretcher was softer than any bed in Miner Town. Ben's hand found her shoulder and stayed there. Gage walked on her other side, close enough that she could see the pale Junkyard dust still caked in the seams of his boots, the same chalky white it had been the night he came through the ground looking for her.

At the inner doors, Flitzberg stopped and spoke without turning back.

"You wanted to know what you ever did to us. Nothing. Hold on to that. The rest can wait." The doors opened onto a corridor, and the lights in it woke one after another ahead of them, reaching into the dark like stepping stones. "Tonight, eat. Sleep. Sit with your friend, and speak to him. For all our instruments can tell, he may hear every word. Your sister will be stronger by morning. And the answers will still be here when you wake." A pause. "They have waited almost a century. They can wait one more night."

Behind them, two medics guided Otto forward by the elbows, one small step at a time. He walked when he was walked. His eyes stayed open on nothing.

Camilla turned her head on the stretcher and counted again, because she couldn't stop.

Four of them. Three looking back.

She made it four anyway, and kept it, and watched the lights run on ahead into the dark, waiting to see which way they'd go.
