# Chapter 21: Quiet Hands

*"Lost an arm, not the rest of me. Get back to the panel."*

— Book 1, Chapter 23: The Blood Debt

Gage woke, and for a moment did not know where he was, and his whole body went tight before his eyes were open.

The last time he had woken somewhere he had not fallen asleep, he had been in this ring, and it had meant that something was about to be done to him. He lay still and did not move until he had placed himself.

Two things were wrong. The bed was soft — beds in Miner Town were boards and rags, and this one sank a little under his weight and held him. And it was quiet. No shift bell. No coughing through the wall. No sound of the mine breathing.

He lay still and let himself remember, in order. The rooms. The volcano that was not a volcano. Camilla's blood. Otto standing with his eyes open and blood running from his nose, not answering his name. The old man's throat under his hand, and the old man's voice saying a dead man answers nothing.

Then food — real food, hot, on plates — and a corridor whose lights woke ahead of them, and this room, and sleep arriving like a wall falling.

He sat up. The room was grey and warm and plain. Two beds. In the other one Ben lay on his back with his mouth open, asleep the way a child sleeps, both hands loose on the blanket.

The door had no lock on his side. He got up and tried it anyway. It opened onto the same corridor, and the lights woke as he stood there, one and then the next, waiting to see which way he would go.

He went back and sat on the edge of the bed until Ben woke. Nobody was going anywhere alone in this place. Not yet.

---

The oldest of the medics — the one who had opened her instrument case on the floor the night before so Ben could see what they meant to do to Camilla — was waiting outside when they came out. She did not ask how they had slept. She looked at them the way a good foreman looks at a crew after a long shift, counting what was still standing, and then she said, "Your sister is awake, and she wants to see you. But she told me to take you to Otto first. Her words."

"She's awake?" Ben said.

"Since before you were. Otto first. She was clear about it."

The observation room was small and bright, and most of it was taken up by a tank.

It stood taller than Gage, a clear cylinder on a dark base, and it was full of something that was not water. Thicker. Faintly blue, like the sealant from the can. Otto hung inside it with his eyes closed, his arms a little away from his sides, a clear mask over his nose and mouth and a line running up from it into the lid. His hair moved slowly in the gel. He was not standing and not lying down. The gel held him the way water holds a swimmer who has stopped swimming.

Ben went to the glass and put both hands on it. Gage stayed where he was.

"Tell me what this is," he said. "Plain."

The medic came and stood beside him. "He is not frozen, whatever it looks like. He is warm. The gel is cycled — warm for a time, then cool, then warm again — and while it is warm, we tell his muscles to work. Small signals, through the gel. Nothing he would feel as pain. His body thinks it is hauling. Then we cool it, and his body thinks it is resting. Then again."

"Why?"

"Because it is the one thing we can do." She said it without apology. "His heart is strong. His breath is steady. The instruments find nothing wrong with him anywhere, and we have looked everywhere there is to look. Whatever is missing, it is not in the body. So we keep the body doing what a body does — work, then rest, then work again. Not a real day. A short one, an hour or so, and then another. Many times over. And we make it loud. A working body is a loud thing. If any part of him is near enough to hear it, we want it to hear something worth coming home to."

Ben had not taken his hands off the glass. "Has it done anything?"

The medic was quiet a moment. "His readings are flat. They have been flat since the trial room." She looked at the panel beside the tank, and then at Ben. "Except once. Last night, in the hour the two of you sat with him. One line moved. I do not know what it means. I would be lying if I said I did."

"But it moved."

"It moved."

Ben put his forehead against the glass, beside Otto's hanging hand, and stayed there. Gage let him. He watched the gel go from blue to a paler blue as the cycle turned toward warm, and watched Otto's fingers tighten by a hair, told to haul by a machine, and told himself that was not nothing.

---

Camilla was standing when they came in.

That was the first thing Gage saw, and for a moment it was the only thing: his sister on her feet, in a plain grey shirt with one sleeve, her face still the color of a bad week but her eyes awake and busy. Standing. Like the trial room, like the railing, like the end of a count she had refused to drop.

The second thing he saw was the arm.

It began at the shoulder, where the laser had taken the old one, and it was metal all the way down: dull grey, not polished, jointed at the shoulder and elbow and wrist in ways that did not try to look like skin. The fingers were long. They were still. The sleeve had been cut away to make room for it, and it sat against her like something that had always meant to be there and was waiting to be told so.

Gage crossed the room before anyone spoke and put his arms around his sister, carefully, on the side that was still hers. Her good hand came up at once and gripped the back of his shirt.

The metal hand came up too — a heartbeat late. He felt it rise, pause as if checking its own permission, and settle flat between his shoulder blades with a care that did not feel like metal at all.

"Lost an arm," she said into his shoulder. "Found one."

He did not let go right away. He stood there with his sister's new hand on his back, and he could feel how heavy it was, and how still, and how easily it could have closed. It was bigger than her other hand. It was, he understood without being told, stronger than the rest of her put together — stronger than him, probably. And it was attached to his sister, and it had just decided, a half-second after she did, to be gentle.

Ben hung back by the door, grinning and wiping his face with his sleeve, until Camilla looked over Gage's shoulder and said, "You too. Come here," and he came.

"Does it hurt?" Gage said.

"No." She let Ben go. She lifted the arm and turned it, watching the elbow, and the elbow turned a fraction behind her eyes. "It's late. Half a breath behind me, every time. They say it listens to the shoulder — what's left of the nerves there — and it will learn my timing in a few days. Right now it's guessing." She flexed the fingers, one after another, and they followed in order, each one a little behind her thought. "It guesses well."

"They put that on you overnight."

"They offered. I said yes." She looked at him then — the old look, the one that had come across the dinner table whenever he was about to be slow about something. "What would you have done?"

He had no answer. She held the metal hand out to him, palm up, the way he had once held a copper tag out to her and said, "You read the system better than I do." The fingers were cool. There were fine seams along them, almost too fine to see, and when he pressed the palm with his thumb it gave, slightly — not like flesh, but not like iron either.

"It's stronger than me," he said.

"Much." The metal fingers closed over his hand — a heartbeat late, and then all at once. Gage let out a short, undignified sound and tried to pull back, and could not. Ben laughed out loud for the first time since the mine.

"Cami."

"Half a breath late," she said, not letting go, "and still faster than you." The fingers opened. She winked at him. "It's learning what I want to do. Give it a week and it'll learn what you need to be told, too — which is usually the same thing twice."

He shook his hand out. The fingers had left no mark. That was somehow worse.

"And it's learning you," he said.

"Yes." Camilla laid her good hand over the metal wrist, and held it there, the way she held every machine she had ever taken apart: listening to it before she touched anything. "I'll learn it first," she said. "I'm faster."

He believed her. That was what scared him.

---

Mr. Flitzberg did not watch the wall. He knew what was on it. He watched the three of them watching it.

The room was a plain one — chairs, a table, one long blank wall — and the office used it for this and nothing else. The three had come in together and sat down with Camilla in the middle. Ben had turned his chair so he could see the door. Flitzberg noted both. Then the lights went down on their own, and the wall lit up, and a century began to play across it.

The office had made this account for its recruits long before Flitzberg's time, and he had changed only the ending. Ten minutes. The Interchange Station and the agreement that founded it, with the old deeds and signatures shown plainly, because recruits from Miner Town had been lied to with pictures before and deserved to see paper. The decades it held. The greed that came in afterward like water into a mine — his own phrase, from the ending he had rewritten. The six days of fighting. The founding of a ring beneath the boundary, with no house's mark on its walls, to keep the balance the houses would not keep themselves. And then almost a century of corrections, each one dressed as ordinary cause and effect, so that the city above never knew it had been corrected at all.

He knew the faces of the dead on the wall by name. He did not need to look at them. He looked at the living.

Camilla watched the dates. He saw her lips move once or twice, checking the years against each other, and saw her find that they added up. It did not comfort her. A story that added up had been built with care, and a century of care had gone into the lie she had grown up inside. Ben watched the faces — every face that appeared, as if he were counting the people this story had cost. Gage watched the wall for the first minute, and the door for the second, and then the wall again. His hands lay open on his knees the whole time. Flitzberg had seen the file on what those hands did when they closed, and he did not think keeping them open was coming easily.

The wall went dark. The lights came up.

He was standing at the end of the table. Camilla looked at his jaw first. She had seen him wear a dead man's face once, and she was checking which face he had on today. He let her look.

"You asked me last night what this place is," he said. "Now you have seen it. Not all of it. A century does not fit in ten minutes. But enough to weigh. I will answer any question you have about what you have just watched, and then I will tell you what this office wants with you, and then I will tell you what you may do about it. In that order. Ask."

Camilla went first. He had expected that.

"The lights," she said. "The heat. The machines in those rooms. The drones." Her good hand was flat on the table; the metal one lay in her lap, still. "In Miner Town they ration candles. Where does this place get its power? And who pays for it?"

"Underneath you," Flitzberg said. "This ring was built to run when both cities above it had gone dark. There are generators buried below the lowest level — old ones, built in a time when people still knew how to build things to last — and reserve banks that could keep every light you have seen burning for a year without being touched. Water from its own wells. Air from its own scrubbers. The men who built this ring had just watched their world catch fire, and they built the one place that would not need to ask anyone for anything when it happened again." He paused. "As for who pays — no one, and everyone. This office has no treasury. It has never needed one. When the ring was founded, the four houses wrote a single line into their own foundations, beneath every rule they have made since: that what this office requires, it may take. Not request. Take. When I need a thing from Drona, Drona's own records show that Drona bought it for itself. When I need money from Kubera, Kubera's own ledgers show Kubera spending it on Kubera. No clerk is ever asked. No one ever signs. The consent is older than the clerks, older than the signatures — older than any Grandmaster now living. It cannot be revoked, because no one alive can find where it was given."

Camilla's lips had parted slightly. It was, Flitzberg thought, the look of an engineer who had just been shown a machine that ran on its own weight.

"So they're paying for you," she said slowly, "and they can't see it."

"They can see the money leave. What they cannot see is where it goes. Every house looks at its own books and sees itself spending on itself. None of them has ever looked at the four sets of books side by side — and if one did, it would find the same small leak in all four, and no one to blame for it."

Ben had been waiting with his hands folded, the way a boy waits when he has been taught that his questions come after the important ones. Flitzberg turned to him deliberately. He knew this one's file too. A household that had taken in the man who robbed it and fed him from its own rations until he could walk. The city had written that down as a defect. Flitzberg had read it twice.

"How many of you are there?" Ben said. "Down here. And —" he hesitated — "do they have families? People who are waiting for them?" He said the next part quickly, as if it embarrassed him. "I have a sister. She'll be at the gates every day until somebody tells her to stop. I want to know if that's what it's like for everyone down here, or if it's just us."

Flitzberg could see which of the two questions Ben really wanted answered. He took them in order anyway.

"Fewer than you would guess. Fewer than two hundred, in the ring and above it both. The office has never been large. A large thing can be found." He let that settle, and then he answered the real question. "Some have families. Up there, in the three cities, living ordinary lives — a wife who thinks her husband keeps late hours in an archive, children who think their mother is a transit clerk. Those people go home at night and say nothing about this place — not to a wife, not to a child, not once, for the rest of their lives. That is a hard way to live with people you love. I will not tell you it is not. Some have no one, and never did. And some — " He stopped, and chose to say it. "Some came from where you came from. Miner Town does not record its missing. A family that loses a child to the Initiation grieves once, and does not expect the child back, and does not look. The ones who came to us from the ground have no one above who is waiting. That is not a thing this office arranged. It is only a thing this office has made use of. You asked. That is the answer."

Ben nodded once, slowly, the way a person nods when a thing they already suspected has been confirmed. He did not look away, and he did not ask anything else. But Flitzberg saw him glance at Gage, and then at Camilla, and understood that the boy had just counted the people in the room who still had someone at the gates, and had come up with the same number for all three.

Gage asked only one thing: "Who watches you?"

Flitzberg nodded slowly, as if a piece he had been waiting for had finally been placed.

"The four Grandmasters," he said, "once a year, in a room with no record. Not this room — never here. Each year a different place, chosen by this office and told to no one until the day. They come alone, without escort, and they leave their wrist instruments behind at the first door, because the last part of the journey is the part they must not be able to retrace. A closed car. A lift that does not show its floors. A corridor with no markings. They sit with me for a day, they see what I have done and argue over what I should do next, and they go back the same way they came. They know they met me. They could not lead anyone to where. That is the first watch. The second is the four of them watching each other. Any one of them could try to end this office. But if he did, the other three would know at once who had done it, and he would be standing alone against all three of them, with nothing left to shield him. So none of them moves. The third watch is a set of ledgers." He said the next part plainly, the way a man reports the weather. "A century of them. Every quiet correction, every buried failure, every secret this office has kept for every house. If anything happens to me — if I am killed, or taken, or simply do not return from a meeting — those ledgers open. Not to the four Grandmasters, who might trade silence for silence. To every representative of every house, all at once. Thousands of people who could not all be bought. Which means that the only people in the city with the power to end me are also the people who would be ruined by my death." He spread his hands slightly. "You will notice that nothing in that answer is trust. It is fear, arranged carefully, pointing in four directions at once. I told you last night I would not lie to you, so I will not call it anything better than that. It is only the best arrangement anyone has found."

Gage nodded slowly. He was not agreeing. He was remembering it, for later.

"Then the offer," Flitzberg said, and he sat down, because he had learned long ago that an offer made standing sounded like an order.

"You would be trained here. Fed here. Taught — everything the city above spends its whole strength keeping from the ground, you would have. In return you would do this office's work, which is quiet, and slow, and rarely thanked, and which has kept a great many people alive who will never know it. It pays in truth and in purpose. There are days when the pay is late."

"And if we say no," Camilla said.

"Then you go home." He said it plainly. "With a story that explains where you have been, which we will give you and which will hold. No punishment. No debt. You go back to the shack-rows and the shift bells, and you live your lives, and you say nothing, because no one would believe you and because you will have given your word. And you will be watched. Kindly. For the rest of your lives. That is not a threat. It is the cost of what you have seen."

"What does watched look like," Ben said, "at the end?"

Flitzberg looked at Ben for a moment. No one had ever asked him that.

"Forty years ago," he said, "a man sat in that chair and said no. He went home. He worked the seams for thirty-one years and raised two children and died of what miners die of. He never spoke. When they buried him, two of our people stood at the back. He never knew their names. They put flowers on the grave." He let that sit. "That is what watched looks like, at the end. The door of this office opens in both directions, and we do not forget the people who walked out of it."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Gage said, "Two things."

Flitzberg waited.

"Otto stays with us. Wherever we are, he is. He gets what he is getting now, and better if better exists. And nobody turns that tank off — not for cost, not because somebody gives up on him — unless the three of us are standing there and say so."

"Yes."

"And you never lie to us." Gage's voice did not rise. "Not once. Not to spare us, not to move us, not because it's quicker. The first lie ends it — all of it, for all of us — and we walk, and you don't stop us. And it goes both ways. We don't lie to you either."

"Yes."

"Three," Camilla said.

Gage turned. She was not looking at him. She had lifted the metal arm onto the table, and the fingers lay open on the wood, and she was looking at Flitzberg over them.

"Nothing in this place stays closed to me. Not the rooms we walked. Not the drones. Not whatever is in that tank keeping Otto warm. Not this." She turned the arm over, slowly, and the wrist followed her a heartbeat behind. "You put a machine on me that I did not build and cannot yet open. I will carry it. I will not carry it blind. When I ask how a thing works, you show me — all of it, down to the last wire. I don't work with anything I'm not allowed to take apart."

It was, Flitzberg thought, the first condition anyone had ever set him that the office would have asked for itself.

"Yes," he said, and then, because she had earned the whole of it: "There is a workshop two levels down. It has been waiting for someone like you for longer than you have been alive."

Flitzberg had made a great many agreements in a long life, most of them with men in rings, and he had never been offered one he liked better.

"Accepted," he said.

He let a breath pass. Then he said the thing he had been carrying since the night before, when he had looked at an intake record and seen a number on it that should not have been possible.

"You have just made me promise never to lie to you," he said. "So I will begin by telling you a thing I would rather have kept until I understood it." He looked at Gage. "The number you wear. Your father's slot. It did not come to you by chance."

Gage went very still.

"The mine gives out numbers blind — whatever is open when you reach the clerk. Your father's slot should not have been open. A dead man's slot is held until an inspection closes it, and that inspection should have taken weeks. It closed in the minute you reached the head of the line. The minute. I have seen the record. Someone, somewhere above you, arranged for you to carry your father's number — and did it so well that the clerk who stamped it on you will never know he was anyone's instrument."

"Who?" Gage's voice had gone flat.

"I do not know. That is the truth, and it is the part that concerns me. There is a hand in this city that moves people the way you moved charges on that map — quietly, and from a distance, and always with a reason. I have been hunting it for some time. I do not know whether the hand that gave you that number is the same hand, or a different one, or whether it meant you kindness or meant you for something. I do not know whether it steered you toward this ring, or whether you arrived here in spite of it." He let that stand. "I hope that one day you find out who did this for you. And when you do, I hope you will tell me. Today I only wanted you to hear it from me, before you heard it from anyone else."

Gage's hand had gone to his chest, where the tag usually hung, and found the clean shirt they had given him instead, and stayed there anyway. He did not say anything. But Flitzberg saw the look that passed between him and his sister — she had told him once, over a copper tag, that numbers belonged to positions, not to people — and saw them both understand, at the same moment, that someone had used that rule to reach him.

"Now," said Mr. Flitzberg. "What this office wants of you."

He did not use the wall. He had never used the wall for this. A mission in this office was spoken once, aloud, to the people who would carry it, and written nowhere, and he told them so before he began, so that they would understand that what they were about to hear would exist afterward only inside the four of them.

Then he told them.

It took eleven minutes. No one interrupted. Twice he used the word balance, because it was the office's word and he owed them the office's reasons. Once — near the end, and quietly — he used a word the office did not use, and that he had not planned to use, which was salvation. He used it about Sector One. He meant it.

When he was finished, no one spoke. Ben had gone very still, the way he went still when a pattern had finished forming and he did not like its shape. Camilla's metal hand had closed around the edge of the table hard enough that the wood had marked — a heartbeat after her face had.

Gage said, "When?"

Flitzberg looked at the three of them — the boy who had held his throat, the boy who counted the dead, the girl with a hand that had just marked his table — and did not give them the answer he would have liked to give.

"Sooner than you are ready," he said. "I will not pretend otherwise. There is a clock on this, and it is not mine. Something is moving in the city above us — through all four houses at once — and it is tipping a balance that has held for almost a century. I do not yet know what it wants. I know what happens when that balance breaks, because the wall just showed you. It does not break on the houses. It breaks on the ground. It always has. I cannot stop that clock to finish teaching you. I can only decide how much of it I spend on you before I send you." He stood. "So you will train hard, and you will train now, and I will keep you here exactly as long as I can and not one day longer. When I send you, you will not feel ready. No one ever has. You will go anyway, because by then you will understand what happens to Sector One if you do not. And when it is done, Sector One will never know your names — which is how you will know you did it right."

He walked to the door, and it opened for him, and he paused in it.

"Eat. Then the workshop. Two levels down, third door. I expect your sister will have it half taken apart by evening."
